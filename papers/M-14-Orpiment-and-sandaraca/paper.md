# M-14. Orpiment and sandaraca (ἀρρενικόν / ἀρσενικόν, σανδαράκη; *auripigmentum*, *arrhenicum*, *sandaraca*): state of the art

*The Apolothecary — state-of-the-art review. Version 1.0, 22 November 2026. Project DOI: [10.5281/zenodo.24422717](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24422717).*

**Certainty:** High for ἀρρενικόν/*auripigmentum* as orpiment (As₂S₃). Medium for σανδαράκη/*sandaraca*: when it is a mined mineral it is realgar (As₄S₄), but Vitruvius and Pliny also give the name to a red made by roasting white lead, which is red lead, and Pliny uses it for bee-bread. · **What is lost:** which product each author meant by *sandaraca*; the ancient mines; and most of the archaeological record of use, because the arsenic sulphides alter and are seldom reported on Greco-Roman objects.

## Abstract

Greek and Latin authors always name the yellow ἀρρενικόν (Latin *auripigmentum* or *arrhenicum*) and the red σανδαράκη (*sandaraca*) together. Theophrastus (*De lapidibus* 40, 50–51) groups them among the "powdery" substances of mines, formed by a "dry and smoky exhalation". Dioscorides (5.104–105) says they occur in the same mines, and Strabo (12.3.40) describes a deadly *sandaraca* mine near Pompeiopolis in Paphlagonia. All these passages were checked against online critical texts. Their joint occurrence, their described appearance and their use to whiten copper identify them as the two natural arsenic sulphides, orpiment and realgar (Caley and Richards 1956, 171–172). The yellow is secure. The red is not. Vitruvius (7.12.2) and Pliny (*NH* 34.176–177, 35.39) also call a red obtained by roasting white lead *sandaraca*. Gliozzo and Burgio (2022) conclude that Pliny probably confused realgar with minium. Modern analysis distinguishes realgar, its light-induced alteration products (pararealgar and others) and orpiment by Raman spectroscopy and diffraction (Trentelman et al. 1996; Chari et al. 2026). Identifications on Greco-Roman objects remain rare.

## 1. Introduction

Arsenic sulphides were valued pigments and drugs in antiquity. They were also dangerous: Strabo (12.3.40) reports that the air in the Paphlagonian *sandaraca* mine was deadly. The names cause two problems for a dictionary of lost materials.

First, ἀρρενικόν (Attic) or ἀρσενικόν (*koine*) gave us the modern word "arsenic". In the ancient texts, however, it names a yellow mineral, not the element or its white oxide. Its Latin counterpart *auripigmentum*, "gold pigment", is listed by Pliny among the natural colours, not the manufactured ones (*NH* 35.30).

Second, σανδαράκη/*sandaraca* is used for more than one thing. It is a mined red mineral (Theophrastus, *De lapidibus* 51; Dioscorides 5.105; Pliny, *NH* 34.177). It is also a red made by roasting white lead (Vitruvius 7.12.2; Pliny, *NH* 35.39). And in Pliny's book on bees it is a name for bee-bread (*NH* 11.17). The later name "sandarac" for the resin of *Tetraclinis articulata* is a further, post-classical meaning. It is not discussed here because no ancient passage using it in that sense was found in the texts checked.

## 3. Proposed identifications

**Orpiment and realgar.** The identification of ἀρρενικόν with orpiment and σανδαράκη with realgar was set out with its reasons by Caley and Richards (1956, 171–172). The two names are "nearly always mentioned together" by ancient writers, and Dioscorides and Pliny treat them in successive sections. Orpiment (As₂S₃) and realgar (As₄S₄) "nearly always occur together and are often found intermingled in the same small specimen". Pliny's third, mixed kind of *arrhenicum* (*NH* 34.178) and Dioscorides' statement that both come from the same mines (5.104) fit this mode of occurrence. Further support comes from technical texts: the Leiden Papyrus X and other early works use one or the other to whiten copper, which points to arsenic compounds (Caley and Richards 1956, 171). The ancient descriptions of the physical appearance also fit: lamellar, flaking, golden orpiment, and friable, sulphur-smelling, cinnabar-red realgar (Dioscorides 5.104–105; Pliny, *NH* 34.177–178).

**The two meanings of *sandaraca*.** Vitruvius states plainly that white lead roasted in a furnace becomes *sandaraca*, and that this artificial product is better than the mined one (7.12.2). Pliny says the same, calling the white-lead product "adulterated" (*adulterina*) (*NH* 35.39). Roasting white lead gives red lead (minium in the modern sense, Pb₃O₄). This chemistry is the basis of Caley and Richards' (1956, 193) remark that the ancients used white lead for the production of red lead, "which was a substitute for the more expensive natural pigments, cinnabar and realgar". Pliny's arrangement adds to the confusion. He lists *sandaraca* among the manufactured colours (*NH* 35.30). He places its account inside the chapter on lead (*NH* 34.176–177). Yet he describes a mined, sulphur-smelling mineral. Gliozzo and Burgio (2022) conclude that "Pliny probably confuses realgar with minium since he includes the production of *sandaraca* with that of *cerussa*". The case for each passage can be stated as follows:

- Theophrastus 40, 50–51; Dioscorides 5.104–105; Strabo 12.3.40; Vitruvius 7.7.5 and 8.3.11; Pliny, *NH* 34.177: a mined mineral, often with a sulphurous smell, and in Strabo poisonous to miners. This is realgar.
- Vitruvius 7.12.2; Pliny, *NH* 34.176 (the comparison), 35.39 (*adulterina*): a red made from white lead. This is red lead.
- Pliny, *NH* 35.30 (*finguntur*) and 35.39 (*sandaraca quomodo fieret diximus*): these present *sandaraca* as a manufactured colour, which makes the meaning ambiguous. Mayhoff's marginal cross-reference at 35.39 points to 34.177, the mined mineral.
- Pliny, *NH* 11.17: bee-bread. This is unrelated to the mineral.

**Sources.** Caley and Richards (1956, 172) argued that the only ancient sources were in Asia Minor or further east. They cite Strabo's mine at Pompeiopolis, Vitruvius' Pontus, and Dioscorides' Pontus, Mysia and Cappadocia, and note that modern geology has found orpiment and realgar at various places in Asia Minor. Pliny adds Syria for *auripigmentum* (*NH* 33.79) and reports, on Juba's authority, a Red Sea island source of *sandaraca* that did not reach Rome (*NH* 35.39). No study identifying the *sandarakourgeion* on the ground was found in the literature checked for this review.

## 4. Analytical and experimental evidence

**The phases.** Gliozzo and Burgio (2022) review the arsenic-bearing phases relevant to cultural heritage: realgar, pararealgar, orpiment, anorpiment, alacranite, dimorphite, bonazziite, uzonite, wakabayashilite, duranusite, arsenolite and claudetite. They also review some light-induced products, and the occurrence, trade, alteration and degradation of the arsenic sulphides. They warn that the usual association "red = realgar, yellow = orpiment" is unreliable (Gliozzo and Burgio 2022). Light turns realgar into yellow products. Trentelman, Stodulski and Pavlosky (1996) characterised pararealgar and other light-induced transformation products of realgar by Raman microspectroscopy. Chari et al. (2026) studied the light sensitivity of realgar (α-As₄S₄), bonazziite (β-As₄S₄), pararealgar (γ-As₄S₄), orpiment and red arsenic sulphide glass. They combined light-exposure experiments with Raman spectroscopy, two-dimensional powder XRD and spectrophotometry. They report that realgar and bonazziite alter into pararealgar, with a colour change from red to yellow. Pararealgar is itself photosensitive under high-energy visible light, changing from yellow to red. Gum binders had the least influence on the alteration (Chari et al. 2026, abstract). An ancient yellow arsenic sulphide may therefore be altered realgar rather than orpiment. Phase-specific methods are needed to tell them apart: Raman spectroscopy and diffraction. Bersani and Lottici (2016) review the application of Raman spectroscopy to mineral pigments and their degradation in archaeometry.

**Archaeological finds.** Caley and Richards (1956, 171–172) collected the early evidence:

- an orange-red pigment in a pottery vessel from Corinth (excavated in 1926), identified as realgar by chemical tests;
- a yellow pigment from a Greek grave of the fifth or fourth century BC, identified as orpiment;
- orpiment on Egyptian paintings and objects from the Eighteenth Dynasty, and a linen bag containing it in the tomb of Tutankhamun.

They concluded that the two pigments occur "much less frequently than other pigments on Greek objects", and were probably not much used in Greek times. Realgar and its alteration on ancient Egyptian papyri were studied by Daniels and Leach (2004). In the Vesuvian area, a Raman, FTIR, XRD and SEM-EDX study of pigment bowls from Pompeii and wall-painting fragments reported ochres, Egyptian blue, huntite, celadonite and malachite. Its abstract reports no arsenic sulphide (Aliatis et al. 2010). No modern analytical identification of realgar or orpiment on a Roman wall painting was found in the literature checked for this review. That is not the same as saying that none exists.

**Red lead as *sandaraca*.** No study was found that tests, on an ancient sample, whether a red sold or used as *sandaraca* was realgar or red lead. Such a test is analytically simple: lead against arsenic by XRF, then phase by Raman or XRD. What is missing are labelled samples. A pigment pot or a residue would have to be connected to the name by an inscription or by its context.

## 5. Discussion: what remains lost

The identity of the yellow is not lost. ἀρρενικόν/*auripigmentum* is orpiment, on the convergent evidence of description, joint occurrence, toxicity and technical use (Caley and Richards 1956, 171–172; Gliozzo and Burgio 2022). Four things remain unknown.

1. **The meaning of *sandaraca* text by text.** The mineral sense (realgar) and the manufactured sense (red lead) coexist in Vitruvius and in Pliny (Vitruvius 7.7.5, 7.12.2; Pliny, *NH* 34.177, 35.39). Which product a painter or a druggist received under the name is unknown. Pliny gives a price of five *asses* a pound for *sandaraca* and half that for *sandyx*, which is made from it (*NH* 35.39–40), but he does not say which kind of *sandaraca* the price refers to.
2. **The mines.** Strabo's *sandarakourgeion*, Vitruvius' mine on the Hypanis, and the Mysian and Cappadocian sources of Dioscorides have not been located and sampled in any study found. Provenance studies of archaeological arsenic sulphides therefore have no ancient reference material.
3. **The archaeological record.** Because realgar alters to yellow phases under light (Trentelman et al. 1996; Chari et al. 2026), and because analysts expect "red = realgar, yellow = orpiment" (Gliozzo and Burgio 2022), ancient uses may have been missed or misread.
4. **"Smelting" orpiment for gold.** Pliny's story of Caligula (*NH* 33.79) cannot be tested as told. Orpiment is an arsenic sulphide, As₂S₃ (Caley and Richards 1956, 171), so any gold obtained must have come from impurities in the ore or from the process. No analytical study of gold traces in orpiment from the ancient sources was found.

## 6. Open questions and proposed research

1. **A corpus of *sandaraca* passages.** Collect every occurrence of σανδαράκη/*sandaraca* in Greek and Latin medical, technical and literary texts and classify each as mined, manufactured from lead, bee-bread, or unclear. This needs a full TLG and PHI search, which was not carried out for this review. The table above covers only the main technical passages.
2. **Reproduce the Vitruvian *sandaraca*.** Roast white lead made by the vinegar method (Vitruvius 7.12.1) at controlled temperatures. Identify the products (massicot, litharge, minium) by XRD and Raman, and compare their colours with Pliny's "flame-coloured" criterion (*NH* 35.39).
3. **Systematic screening of Greco-Roman yellows and reds for arsenic.** In pigment pots, wall paintings and painted sculpture, use XRF for arsenic, then Raman with low-power excitation to avoid inducing alteration. Use the phase references of Gliozzo and Burgio (2022) and Chari et al. (2026). Re-examine the Corinth realgar reported by Caley and Richards (1956, 172), if the sample still exists.
4. **Locate and sample the Pontic and Paphlagonian deposits** named by Vitruvius (7.7.5, 8.3.11), Strabo (12.3.40) and Dioscorides (5.104). Establish trace-element or isotopic signatures for provenancing.
5. **Test Pliny's Caligula story** by assaying gold in orpiment samples from deposits in Syria and Asia Minor (*NH* 33.79).

# M-07. Alumen and stypteria — alum (στυπτηρία; *alumen*): state of the art

*The Apolothecary — state-of-the-art review. Version 1.0, 22 November 2026. Project DOI: [10.5281/zenodo.24422717](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24422717).*

**Certainty:** Medium — the chemical family (aluminium sulphates and related double sulphates) is secure, and for Melos field and laboratory work has identified the likely mineral (alunogen with some potassium alum). But the ancient commercial term covered several products from several sources, no ancient traded sample has been analysed, and the containers are identified only by indirect evidence. · **What is lost:** the range of products sold as *alumen*/στυπτηρία and the form in which they travelled; the outcome of Pliny's purity test, which falls in a lacuna in the text.

## Abstract

Ancient *alumen* (Greek στυπτηρία) was an astringent mineral salt used in medicine, dyeing, tanning and metalworking. This review sets out the ancient testimony, checked against online editions: Herodotus 2.180, Dioscorides 5.106, Pliny *NH* 35.183–190 with 33.65, 33.84 and 33.88, and Galen (Kühn XII.236–237). It then reviews the modern work. Picon (2005) distinguishes natural, artificial and synthetic alums. On Melos, Hall and co-workers identified the alum of fumaroles as mainly alunogen, Al₂(SO₄)₃·17H₂O, with some potassium alum. Photos-Jones et al. (2016) found such material strongly antibacterial. Photos-Jones and Christidis (2022) argued that it was shipped as a gel. Egyptian oasis alums were natural sulphates such as halotrichite and pickeringite (Picon, Vichy and Ballet 2005). A Roman-period workshop on Lesbos made alum from alunite (Archontidou 2005). The amphorae from Lipari are linked to alum by their distribution and by the texts, not by residue analysis (Borgard 2005). Pliny's test with pomegranate juice detects iron. Its outcome is lost in a lacuna in Mayhoff's text.

## 1. Introduction

The name "alum" now means a specific family of double sulphates, of which potassium alum, KAl(SO₄)₂·12H₂O, is the type. The ancient *alumen* and στυπτηρία were not defined chemically. Pliny's own definition is "a brine of the earth" (*salsugo terrae*, *NH* 35.183). The Greek name, from στύφω ("to contract"), points to the property that the ancients used to recognise it, astringency. Galen says the same (Kühn XII.236). The Glasgow team has argued that astringency was "primarily a means of identifying the mineral in the field" (Photos-Jones 2018). For this dictionary the question is therefore not whether alum existed. It is which minerals, from which sources, were traded and used under the name, and in what form.

The modern literature divides the problem in three. Picon (2005, p. 13) gives the sequence of production: alums were "exploités à l'état natif (on parle en ce cas d'alums naturels), puis élaborés à partir de matériaux comme l'alunite ou les schistes pyriteux (on parle alors d'alums artificiels), avant de devenir un produit courant de l'industrie chimique". In English: first exploited in their native state (natural alums), then made from materials such as alunite or pyritic schists (artificial alums), before becoming a common product of the chemical industry. The second question is whether a source was natural or artificial. The third is how the product was traded.

## 3. Proposed identifications

**Natural versus artificial alums.** Picon (2005) set out the framework above. Ferrari et al. (2021) summarise it: natural alums are hydrated double salts of aluminium sulphate with another cation, "the most common being halotrichite (FeAl₂(SO₄)₄·22H₂O), pickeringite (MgAl₂(SO₄)₄·22H₂O) and alunite". These need little more than hand-picking. Artificial alum requires processing alunite or pyritic schist.

**Melos: fumarolic alunogen.** Hall et al. (2003a) described efflorescences in the geothermal field of south-east Melos containing "significant amounts of hydrated Al sulphate, alunogen, which could represent the Melian alumen exploited in Roman times and commended by Pliny". They explain these as the product of enhanced weathering by sulphuric acid formed from fumarolic H₂S. Hall and Photos-Jones (2005) concluded that "Melian *alumen* was an alunogen-rich (aluminium sulfate) salt which probably contained potassium sulfate (K-alum and/or K-sulfate)". Photos-Jones (2018) gives alunogen and alum-(K) as "the principal and soluble component of Melos solfataric alum". The same paper distinguishes a second source on Melos: alunite in hydrothermally altered "white rock". Hall et al. (2003b) set the archaeological site of Aghia Kyriaki in this geothermal landscape. They noted active sulphurous fumaroles and alunite-bearing altered rocks nearby. They also concluded that "any relationship of the site to mineral exploitation will have to be determined by archaeological excavation".

**Egypt: natural sulphates of the western oases.** Picon, Vichy and Ballet (2005) identify alum workings in the Kharga, Dakhla and Bahariya oases. They describe the alums there as natural ones, including halotrichite, pickeringite and tamarugite, and date most of the associated pottery to the Roman period. This chapter could be consulted only in part, through the publisher's web page, so the wording above is kept general.

**Artificial alum from alunite in the Roman period.** Archontidou (2005) describes a workshop at Apothika on Lesbos. It had pits holding friable volcanic material made of alunite, quartz and feldspath, and analyses showed that "cet atelier a produit de l'alun à partir de l'alunite" (this workshop produced alum from alunite). It was in use from the first or second century AD to the sixth or seventh. If this is right, the artificial process was in use in antiquity, not only from the late Middle Ages.

**Solfataric alum, processed.** Photos-Jones et al. (2016) tested solfataric alum for antibacterial activity. Neither the abstract nor the full text was accessible for this review. The README entry says the study covered Campi Flegrei as well as Melos and described processing at temperatures not exceeding 100 °C. Those details could not be checked here and are not relied on. Photos-Jones (2018) gives the reconstruction of the purification on Melos: alum efflorescences were dissolved in rain water collected in basins or pithoi, and the solution was evaporated by solar and geothermal heat. For this she cites earlier work by Photos-Jones and Hall.

## 4. Analytical and experimental evidence

**Antibacterial testing.** Photos-Jones (2018) reports the 2016 tests. A sample made up mainly of alunogen and potassium alum reduced bacterial colonies "by an order of 10⁸ against *Staphylococcus aureus* and an order of 10⁷ for *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*", with "no significant effect" on the yeast *Candida*. The same article notes that "the toxicity of processed Melian solfataric alum has not been tested".

**The pomegranate test.** Pliny says that pomegranate juice reveals adulteration (*NH* 35.184). Hall and Photos-Jones (2005) report that white, apparently pure aluminium sulphate deposits on Melos "can contain sufficient iron (more than about 100 ppm) to darken pomegranate juice". Pliny adds that the inferior kind is stained even by oak-gall (*NH* 35.184). Gall tannins react with iron sulphate to give the black complex of iron-gall ink (Montanari et al. 2024). This review has not found a study that sets out the chemistry of the pomegranate reaction itself. The same team published a chapter on the test (Hall and Photos-Jones 2009). Only its bibliographic record was available here. The README entry gives a detection limit of "about 40 ppm, according to the Glasgow team". This review could not confirm the 40 ppm figure. The value it found, in Hall and Photos-Jones (2005), is "more than about 100 ppm".

**The traded form.** Photos-Jones and Christidis (2022) argue from "simple laboratory-based experiments, combined with X-Ray diffraction (XRD) analysis" that alum shipped from volcanic sources "would have been a gel-like material of varying colouration, from clear/off-white to darker shades, depending on level of iron impurities". Aluminium sulphates are highly hygroscopic. A gel would have arrived in better condition than a powder, which "would have resulted in water absorption and stickiness" (abstract). Photos-Jones (2018) had already suggested that Pliny's liquid *phorimon* might reflect such a supersaturated solution.

**Containers.** Borgard (2005) attributes the transport of alum from the first century BC to the fourth century AD to amphorae from Lipari (the Richborough 527 type, in four successive groups), and possibly to a group from Melos (Milo 1a). He states that the identification "is not based on chemical analyses". It rests on find contexts: at Pompeii two establishments that yielded only Lipari amphorae were a dye-works and a tannery, and at Arles some 250 Lipari and 50 Melos amphorae were found together. It also rests on the texts. Photos-Jones (2018) likewise notes that "there is no direct evidence linking this type of amphora with alum, other than the fact that they originated from Lipari". She adds that because alum-(K) is very soluble, intact contents are unlikely to survive.

**Metallurgical use, experimentally.** Ferrari et al. (2021) reproduced two recipes from the third-century AD Leiden and Stockholm papyri. In them, copper scale is pre-treated with an alum solution before being alloyed with silver. The treatment whitened the scale and helped the alloying. The authors used a modern natural alum crystal bought in Yemen, identified in their analyses as potassium alum. The result shows what the recipes can do. It does not identify which ancient alum the recipes used.

## 5. Discussion: what remains lost

The sources agree that στυπτηρία/*alumen* came in several kinds (split, round, liquid; white and black) from many places, with Egypt and Melos the most esteemed. They judged it by appearance, taste (astringency) and the pomegranate test. The modern work has made the Melian kind concrete: fumarolic alunogen with minor potassium alum, purified by dissolution and evaporation. It has also shown that other sources supplied other materials: natural iron- and magnesium-bearing alums in Egypt, and alum made from alunite on Lesbos. The README's description, "a commercial term that covers several products", is borne out.

Four things remain lost. First, no ancient traded alum has been analysed. All the mineralogy is of modern efflorescences and experimental products. Second, the form of transport is a hypothesis (gel or solid) supported by laboratory work but not by a residue. Third, the link between amphora types and alum rests on context, not chemistry. Fourth, Pliny's statement of what pure alum does on mixing with pomegranate juice is itself missing from the text as transmitted. Modern scholars reconstruct the test from chemistry, not from Pliny's words.

## 6. Open questions and proposed research

1. **Residue analysis of "alum amphorae".** Interior surfaces of Lipari (Richborough 527) and Melos amphorae from dye-works and tanneries could be analysed for aluminium-sulphate residues or their alteration products. A positive result would turn Borgard's contextual identification into a chemical one. A negative result would also be informative, given the solubility noted by Photos-Jones (2018).
2. **Replicate Pliny's grading tests.** The README proposes this. Concretely: prepare Melian alunogen, Lesbos-type alunite-derived alum and Egyptian-type halotrichite/pickeringite at graded iron contents. Then apply pomegranate juice and oak-gall (*NH* 35.184), measure the threshold of visible darkening, and publish the full data so that the 40 ppm versus 100 ppm discrepancy can be resolved.
3. **Test the gel hypothesis against the texts.** Pliny's "liquid" alum and Dioscorides' ὑγρά could be compared with the gels produced by Photos-Jones and Christidis (2022), using the ancient criteria: transparency, milkiness, smoothness and a "fiery" exhalation.
4. **Date the Melian workings.** Hall and Photos-Jones (2005) and Hall et al. (2003b) leave the age of the fumarole workings and their relation to Aghia Kyriaki to future excavation.
5. **Check the Linear B reading.** The identification of *tu-ru-pte-ri-ya* with στυπτηρία should be checked against an edition of the Pylos tablets before it is used as evidence for Bronze Age alum.

A stopping criterion for the first stage: one chemically confirmed alum residue from a dated Roman container, or a published negative result from a defined sample of containers.

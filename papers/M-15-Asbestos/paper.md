# M-15. Asbestos (ἀσβέστινον, ἀμίαντος λίθος, Καρύστιος λίθος; *asbestinon*, *linum vivum*, *amiantus*): state of the art

*The Apolothecary — state-of-the-art review. Version 1.0, 22 November 2026. Project DOI: [10.5281/zenodo.24422717](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24422717).*

**Certainty:** High for the family. The fire-proof, spinnable "stone" of Carystus and Cyprus can only be a fibrous silicate mineral of the asbestos group. The mineral species of the ancient sources and of the surviving cloths has not been published in the literature reviewed here. · **What is lost:** the mineral species of each source, the spinning and weaving technique, and most of the surviving textiles, which were described in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries and not analysed by modern methods in any study found.

## Abstract

Greek and Latin authors describe a mineral that could be combed, spun and woven into napkins, nets, lamp-wicks and funeral cloths that were cleaned rather than destroyed by fire. The main passages are Strabo 10.1.6 (Carystus), Dioscorides 5.138 (Cyprus), Pausanias 1.26.7 (Callimachus' lamp), Plutarch, *De defectu oraculorum* 434A, and Pliny, *NH* 19.19–20 (royal cremation tunics) and 36.139. All were checked against online critical texts. Laufer (1915) gathered the classical, Chinese and later evidence. He argued that the classical authors regarded asbestos as a mineral, and that the belief in a vegetable asbestos arose later. Caley and Richards (1956) named Karystos and a place south-east of Mount Troodos on Cyprus as the only known sources in or near Greece. Three ancient asbestos cloths preserved in Italy were described before modern analysis, among them one found at Pozzuoli in 1633 and a cremation shroud found in 1702 near Porta Maggiore (Laufer 1915, after Yates 1843). Recent finds include asbestos textiles from early first-millennium AD burials at Batujaya, Java (Cameron, Indrajaya and Manguin 2015) and tremolite asbestos in Roman building materials at Micia, Romania (Ion et al. 2025). No mineralogical analysis of a Mediterranean ancient asbestos textile was found.

## 1. Introduction

Asbestos is, in one sense, one of the least lost materials in this dictionary. The mineral group is well defined, the ancient descriptions are unambiguous about its two main properties — it can be spun and it survives fire — and several ancient sources are named. What is lost is concrete knowledge:

- which mineral was worked at each source;
- how fibres described by Pliny as short and "difficult to weave" (*difficile textu propter brevitatem*, *NH* 19.19) were spun;
- what the surviving cloths are made of.

The vocabulary also needs care. Pliny's Greek word is ἀσβέστινον, "unquenchable" (*NH* 19.20). Dioscorides uses ἀμίαντος, "undefiled" (5.138). Strabo and Plutarch name the stone after its source, Carystus (Strabo 10.1.6; Plutarch, *De def. or.* 434A). Pausanias calls the material a "Carpasian linen" (λίνον Καρπάσιον, 1.26.7). Laufer (1915, 304) takes this to refer to Carpasia on Cyprus. Pliny's *asbestos* at *NH* 37.146 is a different matter: it is an iron-coloured stone of the Arcadian mountains in a list of gems, and nothing in that passage says it is fibrous. Finally, Laufer (1915, 305) notes that among the Greek alchemists the word ἄσβεστος came to mean lime.

## 3. Proposed identifications

There is no serious dispute that these texts describe asbestos, a fibrous silicate. The debates have concerned three narrower questions.

**Did the ancients think it was a plant?** The dictionary entry (version 0.3) says that Pliny "thinks it is a plant from India". This view has nineteenth-century precedents. Laufer (1915, 306–307) cites Bostock and Riley's note that Pliny "evidently considers asbestos to be a vegetable", and similar statements by Dana and others. Laufer rejected it. He argued that Pliny's *nascitur* and *linum* do not imply a plant: Pliny uses *nascitur* of minerals too, and *linum* is the natural word for a woven fabric. All Greek authors call the material a stone. Pliny also treats *amiantus* and *asbestos* in his mineralogical books. The ancients knew mines at Carystus, on Cyprus and, Laufer thought, in Arcadia. Laufer traced the notion of a vegetable asbestos to the Hellenistic East, citing the *Alexander Romance* (Pseudo-Callisthenes 3.22) and a Syriac natural history (Laufer 1915, 307–308). Pliny's own words remain ambiguous. Placing the material in the book on flax, saying that it grows in India and "becomes used to living by burning" (*NH* 19.19) invite the plant reading, even if they do not require it. The question is one of interpretation. The text itself cannot settle it.

**Where did it come from?** Caley and Richards (1956, 87) wrote that "there were only two known sources of asbestos in Greece and its vicinity in ancient times". These were Karystos at the southern tip of Euboea and "a place to the south-east of Mt. Troodos on Cyprus, where the abandoned workings are still to be seen today". These two places match Strabo, Plutarch and Dioscorides. Laufer (1915, 307) adds Arcadia on the strength of Pliny, *NH* 37.146. The passage, however, gives only a colour, so this third source is doubtful. Pliny's India (*NH* 19.19) is not supported by any other classical source cited here. Laufer (1915, 309–310) shows that Chinese sources, by contrast, describe asbestos cloth as a product of the Roman Orient (Ta Ts'in). Whether Pliny's Indian origin reflects a real trade route is not known.

**Is the Scapte Hyle stone asbestos?** Caley and Richards (1956, 87–88) note that Moore took Theophrastus, *De lapidibus* 17, to refer to asbestos. They reject this because asbestos does not occur in that region, and propose a brown fibrous lignite. Laufer (1915, 302) had also preferred a bitumen-type reading.

**Which mineral species?** Asbestos is a collective name for several fibrous silicate minerals. The archaeological cases met in this review are anthophyllite in Finnish prehistoric pottery (Bianchi and Bianchi 2014) and tremolite in Roman building materials (Ion et al. 2025). No study found in this review identifies the species exploited at Karystos or on Cyprus in antiquity, or the species of any surviving ancient Mediterranean cloth. Pliny's red colour that turns bright in fire (*NH* 19.19) is the only physical clue in the texts. It is not enough to identify a mineral.

## 4. Analytical and experimental evidence

**Surviving cloths in Italy.** Laufer (1915, 308–309), following Yates (1843), lists three asbestos textiles preserved in Italy:

- one found at Pozzuoli in 1633, formerly in the Barberini collection;
- one in the Vatican Library, discovered in 1702 "a mile outside of the Gate of Rome, called Porta Maior". It was a corpse-cloth about five feet by six and a half feet, coarsely spun but soft, enclosing the skull and calcined bones of a cremated body in a marble sarcophagus. Laufer reports that the deceased was thought to date to no earlier than the age of Constantine;
- a large piece in the Museo Borbonico at Naples, found at Vasto in the Abruzzi.

Laufer read the Vatican cloth as "a remarkable confirmation of Pliny's statement" about royal funeral tunics. These descriptions predate modern mineralogy. No published fibre analysis of any of the three was found in this review.

**Recent finds and studies.** Bianchi and Bianchi (2014) write that "starting from the 17th century asbestos clothes were found in some Roman tombs in Italy". They state that the catalogue of Roman asbestos artefacts "has been substantially enriched", that some pieces have been analysed, and that others "remain under study". The specific analyses they cite were not consulted for this review. Their note is concerned with the occupational risk to archaeologists, and recommends a precautionary approach on excavations and in post-excavation work (Bianchi and Bianchi 2014).

Outside the Mediterranean, Cameron, Indrajaya and Manguin (2015) published fragments of cloth "made of mineral asbestos fibres" from early first-millennium AD burials of the Buni culture at Batujaya, West Java. They interpret them as shrouds used to wrap the bodies, and set them in the context of a circulation of asbestos cloth "from the Roman Orient to China, via South and Southeast Asia" (Cameron, Indrajaya and Manguin 2015, abstract).

In the Roman provinces, Ion et al. (2025) report "the first evidence of the presence of the mineral tremolite asbestos in Roman building materials" at the fort and settlement of Micia (Romania), a site occupied from the late second century AD. They identified the fibres by optical and scanning electron microscopy, XRD, WDXRF, FTIR, Raman, BET and thermal analysis (Ion et al. 2025, abstract). The abstract does not say whether the asbestos was a deliberate addition or a natural component of the raw material.

**Prehistoric use.** Bianchi and Bianchi (2014) state that asbestos (anthophyllite) was used in pottery in the Savo area of south-eastern Finland around 4000 BC. This is evidence of the use of asbestos fibre long before the classical texts. It has no direct bearing on the Mediterranean textile industry.

**A claim not verified.** An earlier version of this entry cited remains of an asbestos cloth or cushion stuffing from a Roman burial at Cologne, after Wild (1970, 21). Wild's book exists (catalogue record verified), but page 21 was not consulted, and the Cologne find could not be confirmed from any other source. It is therefore not used here.

## 5. Discussion: what remains lost

The identity of the material is not in doubt. Four things are lost.

1. **The mineral of each source.** No analysis connecting the Carystian or Cypriot workings to a mineral species, or to an archaeological textile, was found. Caley and Richards (1956, 87) refer to abandoned workings on Cyprus that were "still to be seen" in 1956, but give no mineralogy.
2. **The technique.** Laufer (1915, 306) observed that the classical notices "are very plain, but deficient in facts". They say nothing about mining, preparing, spinning or weaving the fibres. Pliny's remark on short fibres (*NH* 19.19) and Plutarch's "soft, thread-like strands" (434A) are the only technical details. Whether asbestos was spun pure or with a plant or animal carrier fibre is not known from the texts. No modern analysis of an ancient cloth was found that could show this.
3. **The objects.** The Pozzuoli, Vatican and Vasto cloths are known from early modern descriptions (Laufer 1915, 308–309). Modern analyses of Roman asbestos artefacts are said to exist (Bianchi and Bianchi 2014), but none was located and read for this review.
4. **The scale of use.** Pliny's price, equal to that of pearls (*NH* 19.20), and Plutarch's report that the Carystian source was exhausted by his time (434A) suggest a luxury product of limited supply. The Micia find (Ion et al. 2025) shows that asbestos also occurs in ordinary Roman building materials, which raises the question of whether some uses went unrecorded in the texts.

## 6. Open questions and proposed research

1. **Analyse the surviving Italian cloths.** Locate the Vatican, Naples (ex-Museo Borbonico) and ex-Barberini textiles in current collections. Identify the fibre species by SEM-EDS, XRD and Raman, all of which have been applied to Roman asbestos by Ion et al. (2025). Determine whether any non-mineral fibre is present. This must be done under the precautions recommended by Bianchi and Bianchi (2014).
2. **Sample the ancient workings.** Characterise the asbestos of the Karystos area and of the Cypriot site south-east of Troodos mentioned by Caley and Richards (1956, 87). Compare them with the textiles.
3. **Reconstruct the spinning.** Test whether short asbestos fibres can be spun alone or need a carrier fibre, using fibre from the same sources. This addresses the technical difficulty that Pliny records (*NH* 19.19).
4. **Compile the modern catalogue.** Bring together the Roman asbestos finds reported since the nineteenth century, including those referred to by Bianchi and Bianchi (2014), with their contexts and any analyses. A list of this kind was not found in published form.
5. **Read the Cologne claim at source.** Consult Wild (1970, 21) and the excavation report it cites, to confirm or discard the Cologne find.

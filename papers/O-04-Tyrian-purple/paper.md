# O-04. Purpura — Tyrian purple (πορφύρα; *purpura*, *ostrum*): state of the art

*The Apolothecary — state-of-the-art review. Version 1.0, 22 November 2026. Project DOI: [10.5281/zenodo.24422717](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24422717).*

**Certainty:** High for the chemistry of the colourant (6,6′-dibromoindigo and related indigoids from muricid snails); Low for the ancient industrial dyeing process, which is known only from Pliny's compressed account and from experiments · **What is lost:** the recipe and control of the dye vat (the nature of Pliny's "salt", the reducing system, temperatures, durations, proportions) and the exact procedures behind the named grades (*dibapha*, Tyrian, amethyst, *conchyliata*)

## Abstract

Shellfish purple was the most prestigious textile colour of the ancient Mediterranean. Its chemistry has been well understood since P. Friedländer isolated and identified the main colourant, 6,6′-dibromoindigo, from 12,000 *Murex brandaris* in 1909. The colourant forms from precursors in the snail's hypobranchial gland through enzymatic, oxidative and light-dependent steps. The proportions of dibromoindigo, monobromoindigo, indigo and indirubins vary with species and dyeing conditions. What is lost is the ancient way of turning the insoluble pigment into a dye bath. Pliny's account (*NH* 9.133–135) gives salt, three days' steeping, heating in a lead vessel, moderate heat for about ten days and a test with wool. It does not name the reducing agent. Modern experiments have produced dye baths by bacterial reduction in alkaline, warm conditions. Archaeology now documents production from the Bronze Age (Aegina, sixteenth century BC) through the Iron Age (Timna, Tel Shiqmona). This review sets out the checked ancient texts and the analytical, archaeological and experimental literature.

## 1. Introduction

Purple marked rank in Rome. Pliny lists the fasces, the boy's *praetexta*, the senatorial stripe that distinguishes the *curia* from the knights, and the triumphal robe (*NH* 9.127). The material is not lost in the way silphium is. The snails survive, the colourant can be extracted or synthesised (Cooksey 2001), and it has even been produced in engineered bacteria (Lee et al. 2021). What is lost is the process: how a Tyrian dyer got the pigment into solution, controlled its hue and fixed it on wool to reach the named grades and prices Pliny reports. This entry overlaps P-09 (processes to investigate) for the vat process.

## 3. Proposed identifications

**The colourant.** Friedländer (1909) reported on the dye of ancient purple from *Murex brandaris*. Cooksey (2001) summarises the result: "In 1909 Friedlander solved the identity problem by processing the hypobranchial glands of 12,000 *Murex brandaris* and obtained 1.4 grams of pigment", identified as 6,6′-dibromoindigo. Cooksey (2001) reviews the genesis of the dye, its composition, its intermediates and the synthesis of 6,6′-dibromoindigo, 6-bromoindigo and 6,6′-dibromoindirubin. The precursor in the gland, tyrindoxyl sulfate, is hydrolysed by the enzyme purpurase. The product oxidises in air through tyrindoleninone to the green tyriverdin, and "light is necessary" for the final photolysis to dibromoindigo, which releases the characteristic dimethyl disulfide smell (Cooksey 2001). That smell matches Pliny's "virus grave" (*NH* 9.127).

**Species and hue.** Cooksey (2001) gives typical pigment compositions of 77–91% 6,6′-dibromoindigo, 1–16% 6-bromoindigo and 1–14% 6,6′-dibromoindirubin for the species other than *Murex trunculus*. For *M. trunculus* he gives about 55% indigo, 7% indirubin, 35% 6-bromoindigo and only 3% dibromoindigo. Ziderman (1986) argued that the red-purple (biblical *argaman*, "Tyrian purple") was 6,6′-dibromoindigotin from the spiny dye-murex and rock-shells. In his view the blue-purple (biblical *tekhelet*, Greek *hyakinthos*) came from the banded dye-murex, *Phyllonotus* (now *Hexaplex*) *trunculus*, as a violet mixture of dibromoindigotin and indigotin. Koren (2013) argues that the final hue depended mainly on the raw pigment, DBI-rich or indigo-rich *H. trunculus*, with *Bolinus brandaris* or *Stramonita haemastoma* added for redder shades. On his account, photo-debromination in a covered vat would have been negligible. Karapanagiotis (2019) reports that dyeing conditions have a "dramatic effect" on the composition of the purple dye.

**Pliny's two shells.** Pliny's *bucinum* and *purpura/pelagia* (*NH* 9.130) are conventionally matched with modern muricid species. In the sources read for this review, no explicit correspondence was found beyond the use of *H. trunculus*, *B. brandaris* and *S. haemastoma* as the Mediterranean dye species (Koren 2013).

## 4. Analytical and experimental evidence

**Archaeological identifications.**

- **Aegina, Late Helladic IIA (sixteenth century BC).** Berger et al. (2024) identify a purple-dye workshop at Kolonna on Aegina. Shell counts and HPLC show that *Hexaplex trunculus* was used "almost exclusively".
- **Timna, late eleventh to early tenth century BC.** Sukenik et al. (2021) identified 6-monobromoindigotin and 6,6′-dibromoindigotin by HPLC in three textile samples from Slaves' Hill. These are the oldest dyed textiles of this kind currently known in the southern Levant.
- **Tel Shiqmona, about 1100–600 BC.** Shalvi et al. (2025) present a sequence of Iron Age purple-dye workshops on the Carmel coast. They describe it as the only site where large-scale, sustained manufacture has been excavated, and they connect vats and tools chemically and mineralogically.
- **Synthesis and dating.** Marín-Aguilera et al. (2019) propose a full *chaîne opératoire* and synthesise pre-Roman evidence across the Mediterranean. Reese (1980) studied crushed murex shells from purple-dye and lime production at Berenice (Sidi Khrebish, Benghazi). Van Strydonck et al. (2012) showed that direct radiocarbon dating of murex shells gave coherent dates at four Balearic sites, whereas the associated bones and charcoal were intrusive.
- **Earliest use.** Koren (2013) states that direct chemical evidence points to Minoan use of the purple as a painting pigment about four millennia ago. In his account, dyeing textiles with it began with the Levantine Phoenicians about half a millennium later.

**The vat.** The pigment is insoluble and must be reduced to a soluble leuco form, then reoxidised on the fibre. Cooksey (2001) quotes Pliny's leaden vessel and reports two lines of reconstruction:

- **Chemical reduction.** "Experiments with lead and tin usually in strongly alkaline solution have met with variable success but tin is the stronger reductant."
- **Bacterial reduction.** A biochemical reduction analogous to the woad vat was "reportedly unsuccessful for dibromoindigo". However, "based on the successful woad vat parameters (pH 7.8; 50 ºC), and the long (10 day) fermentation period", a dye bath was produced from *Murex trunculus*, and synthetic dibromoindigo was reduced "using cockles".

Koren (2013) re-reads Pliny and the Talmud with laboratory experiments and draws these conclusions:

- Pliny's "salt" must have been an alkaline salt (natron, lime or limestone), needed for the reductive dissolution.
- Seawater, being slightly alkaline, could have been used to prepare the bath.
- A 100-litre vat would have needed the flesh of about 10,000 *H. trunculus* and would dye up to about 1.5 kg of wool.
- The vat had to be kept covered for the slow bacterial reduction.

Marzano (2013) compares Pliny's description with archaeological evidence and with experiments that estimate how many molluscs a given quantity of dye required.

**Synthesis.** Lee et al. (2021) engineered *Escherichia coli* with a tryptophan 6-halogenase, tryptophanase and a flavin-containing monooxygenase, producing 315.0 mg/l of 6,6′-dibromoindigo from tryptophan. This shows that the colourant can now be made without snails. It says nothing about the ancient process.

## 5. Discussion: what remains lost

The identity of the colourant is not in doubt (Friedländer 1909; Cooksey 2001), so the "lost material" is a technique. Pliny's text is the only detailed ancient account (*NH* 9.133–138), and it is incomplete in several ways:

- It names salt but no reducing agent.
- It specifies a lead vessel, which Cooksey (2001) notes has prompted experiments with lead and tin.
- It gives proportions in a sentence that the edition marks as damaged (*NH* 9.135).
- It distinguishes grades (amethyst, Tyrian *dibapha*, *conchyliata*) by sequences of baths and by the addition of water and urine (*NH* 9.134–138), without temperatures or pH.

The modern reconstructions that work rely on the snails' own bacteria in a warm, mildly alkaline, covered bath for about ten days (Cooksey 2001; Koren 2013). That is consistent with Pliny's ten days and moderate heat (*NH* 9.133), but it is a reconstruction, not a recovered recipe.

Also lost is the empirical control of hue. Pliny's "blackish to the eye but shining when held up to the light" Tyrian (*NH* 9.135) and Vitruvius' latitudinal colour scale (7.13.2) describe results that modern work explains through species, pigment composition and dyeing conditions (Cooksey 2001; Koren 2013; Karapanagiotis 2019). No one has yet shown which combination reproduces each named grade. Finally, the commercial grading by feeding ground (*NH* 9.131) has not been connected to any measured property.

## 6. Open questions and proposed research

1. **Reproduce Pliny's sequence quantitatively.** Run Pliny's sequence of salt, three days' steeping, a lead or tin vessel, moderate heat and a ten-day test under controlled pH, temperature and oxygen. Use *H. trunculus* and *B. brandaris* separately and mixed, and measure the indigoid composition of the dyed wool by HPLC against archaeological profiles (Sukenik et al. 2021; Karapanagiotis 2019).
2. **Test the role of urine and dilution** in Pliny's *conchyliata* recipe (*NH* 9.138) on hue and fastness.
3. **Residues in vats.** Analyse residues from excavated vats (Tel Shiqmona, Aegina) for alkaline salts, lead or tin, and microbial markers, to test Koren's (2013) alkaline-salt reading of Pliny's *sal* (Shalvi et al. 2025; Berger et al. 2024).
4. **Grade signatures.** Look in Roman purple textiles for chemical signatures of double dyeing (*dibapha*) or over-dyeing with *coccum* (*hysginum*, *NH* 9.140).
5. **The papyri.** Read the Leiden and Stockholm purple and imitation-purple recipes in the Greek and in Caley's translations (Caley 1926; Caley 1927), which were not consulted here, and test them experimentally against Vitruvius' imitation purples (7.14.1–2).

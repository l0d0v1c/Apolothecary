# P-03. Natron glass in tank furnaces: state of the art

*The Apolothecary — state-of-the-art review. Version 1.0, 22 November 2026. Project DOI: [10.5281/zenodo.24422717](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24422717).*

**Certainty:** Medium (excavated furnaces, raw-glass slabs and isotopic data establish the main steps; firing temperature, duration, batch recipe and charging sequence are still inferred, and no full-scale reconstruction has reproduced a tank-furnace melt) · **What is lost:** firing schedule and temperature, batch proportions and charging sequence, the treatment of the natron before use, how the melt was controlled and how the slab was cooled and broken up

## Abstract

For most of the first millennium CE, the glass of the Roman, Byzantine and early Islamic Mediterranean was made in two stages. Primary workshops in the Levant and Egypt melted coastal sand with Egyptian natron in large tank furnaces. Each firing produced a slab of raw glass weighing several tonnes or more. The slab was broken into chunks and traded to secondary workshops, which remelted and shaped the glass. Pliny the Elder (*NH* 36.190–194) describes the sand of the river Belus, melting in "continuous furnaces" and remelting "from the lumps" in workshops. Excavated furnaces at Beni Salama (Wadi Natrun), Apollonia-Arsuf, Bet Eli'ezer and Jalame match this two-stage scheme. Strontium isotopes show that the lime in the Levantine glass came from marine shell in beach sand. Boron isotopes point to an Egyptian or North African natron. Hafnium isotopes now separate Egyptian from Levantine glass. What remains unknown is how the furnaces were run. The firing temperature has been estimated only by simulation, the duration only by ethnographic analogy, and the batch recipe only by working back from the glass. The raw glass itself shows that the batch was poorly mixed.

## 1. Introduction

Natron glass is a soda–lime–silica glass whose soda comes from evaporite minerals, especially those of the Wadi Natrun in Lower Egypt. Such glass typically has less than 1.5 wt% each of magnesia and potash, which distinguishes it from plant-ash glass (Rehren & Freestone 2015). The flux itself is treated in entry M-10 (*nitrum*). This entry deals with the process by which sand and natron were turned into raw glass on an industrial scale.

The model now generally accepted divides production into two stages (Brems et al. 2018). Raw glass was first made from sand and natron in tank furnaces at primary sites. Once the melt had cooled, the furnace was dismantled and the slab was broken into chunks. These chunks were traded widely and remelted in secondary workshops, where colourants or opacifiers might be added (Brems et al. 2018). Rehren and Freestone (2015) note that this coastal production of the eastern Mediterranean seems to have used a single firing stage, in contrast to the two firing stages documented for Late Bronze Age Egyptian glassmaking. They describe charges "on a scale of from ten to in excess of thirty tonnes" melted in large tanks, "probably for many weeks in a firing". Freestone, Brems and Degryse (2025) give slab weights of the order of 10 tonnes and note that, in any one period, raw glass seems to have been produced in both the Levant and Egypt.

The process counts as "lost" in a specific sense. The product, the furnaces and the trade are well documented. The operating knowledge is not: how hot the furnaces were, for how long, what the batch was, and how it was charged.

All five literary authors place the glass sand in the bay of Ptolemais (Akko). Pliny alone separates melting into lumps (*massae*) from remelting in *officinae*. His account fits the archaeological two-stage model closely, but none of these texts gives temperatures, durations, the size of the furnaces or quantities beyond the ambiguous "three parts" of 36.194.

## 3. Reconstructed process

The steps below combine the archaeological record with the analytical results discussed in section 4. The status of each step is given.

1. **Raw materials.** Coastal sand supplied both the silica and the lime. In the Levant the lime came largely from shell fragments in the sand (attested isotopically: Freestone et al. 2003; Brems et al. 2018). The trace-element patterns of the raw glass from Apollonia and Bet Eli'ezer are "very similar to that of Belus River sand" (Brems et al. 2018). At Beni Salama the sand came from a nearby fossil dune and the natron from the Wadi Natrun lakes (Nenna 2010). The flux was natron (M-10), probably of Egyptian or North African origin (inferred from boron isotopes: Brems et al. 2018). Experiments show that unprocessed Egyptian natron can make good glass (Jackson et al. 2018), but whether ancient glassmakers refined it is not known (see section 5).
2. **The furnace.** At Beni Salama, four large tank furnaces of the 1st–2nd centuries CE survive. Their internal basins measured 7.5 × 2 m and 6.10 × 2 m, and their walls were up to 2–2.5 m thick (Nenna 2010). At Apollonia-Arsuf, the 6th–7th-century furnaces of Areas D and N had a firing area on the west and a melting chamber on the east (Tal et al. 2004, as summarised in the IFAO catalogue record). At Bet Eli'ezer (8th century CE), 17 tank furnaces were found, each with a minimum capacity of 8 to 10 tonnes of glass (Brems et al. 2018, citing Gorin-Rosen 2000 among others). At Jalame (4th century CE), debris shows that the furnaces were built of, or lined with, a lime-rich material (Freestone et al. 2023).
3. **Charging and melting.** It has been proposed that sand and natron were fed into the furnace in several smaller batches. Each new batch would float on the glass already molten, heated from below by the melt and from above by the hot gases (Brems et al. 2018, summarising Freestone et al. 2000). This is an inference, not an observation. Nenna (2010) estimates that 22–30 tonnes of raw materials were needed per firing at Beni Salama. For comparison she cites the much larger modern furnaces of Jalesar in India, where firing lasts 30 days and raw material is charged every three days.
4. **Temperature and duration.** Natron lowers the melting temperature of silica from about 1,700 to about 1,200 °C (Nenna 2010). A computational fluid-dynamics model of the Bet Eli'ezer furnaces predicts an average glass temperature of 1,200 °C and a minimum of 1,080 °C at the bottom of the tank (Van Beeumen 2011 and Van Beeumen et al. 2011, as reported by Brems et al. 2018; not read for this review). The duration of a firing is estimated at "two weeks or more" (Brems et al. 2018, citing Gorin-Rosen 2000) or "many weeks" (Rehren & Freestone 2015). Neither figure has been measured on ancient material.
5. **Additives at the primary stage.** At Jalame, manganese was added as a decolouriser at the primary stage. Its wide range of concentrations suggests an inhomogeneous batch, which may have melted to a range of colours that were then sorted as chunks (Freestone et al. 2023). In late Hellenistic and early Roman Beirut, amber glass seems to have been produced at the primary stage from natron and sand alone. Its colour required reducing conditions, perhaps created by organic reducing agents (Freestone et al. 2025). The furnace atmosphere was thus a controlled or at least consequential variable, but how it was controlled is unknown.
6. **Cooling and breaking up.** At Beni Salama, after melting, one long wall and part of the vault were dismantled to reach the slab and cut it into transportable pieces. Each slab weighed 15–20 tonnes (Nenna 2010). The Levantine model is the same: the glass cooled in the tank, the furnace was dismantled and the slab was broken into chunks (Brems et al. 2018).
7. **Distribution and secondary working.** The chunks were traded across the Mediterranean and Europe to be remelted and worked (Freestone et al. 2025). A reconstruction in the Israel Museum shows chunks stored in a pottery vessel in the 6th–7th-century workshop at Beth Shean (Rehren & Freestone 2015, fig. 3).

The great slab of Bet She'arim (3.40 × 1.95 × 0.45 m) is often cited as an illustration of the scale of the industry. According to Freestone and Gorin-Rosen (1999), as summarised in the IFAO catalogue record, it belongs to the early 9th century. They interpret it as an experiment that combined Belus-type sand with plant ash rather than natron. The two raw materials were incompatible, and the glass failed when melted under the usual conditions. Brems et al. (2018) likewise describe it as an *in situ* failed slab of the early 9th century. It therefore documents the tank technology at the moment of transition away from natron. It is not a typical natron-glass product.

Related entries: M-10 (*nitrum*, natron) for the flux; P-08 (synthesis of Egyptian blue) for an earlier natron-dependent vitreous technology.

## 4. Analytical and experimental evidence

**Homogeneity of a single melt.** Brems et al. (2018) analysed 15 raw-glass samples from a single tank furnace at Apollonia (6th–7th century) and eight samples from two furnaces at Bet Eli'ezer (8th century) for trace elements and Sr, Nd and B isotopes. The ratios and patterns of trace elements are uniform, but absolute concentrations vary significantly within one tank. The authors conclude that "the raw materials were poorly mixed before firing and that convective currents within the molten glass were insufficient to homogenise the batch". Concentrations of (de)colouring elements are at background levels, so there was "no obvious recycling of glass cullet at this stage". The Apollonia glass ("Levantine I") has about 3.0–3.5% Al₂O₃, 7–9% CaO, 12–15% Na₂O and 69–73% SiO₂. The Bet Eli'ezer glass ("Levantine II") tends to have less soda (mostly 11–13%) and lime (6–8%) and more silica (73–77%) (Brems et al. 2018, summarising Freestone et al. 2000 and Tal et al. 2004).

**Lime source: strontium isotopes.** Glass from Bet Eli'ezer and Bet She'an has ⁸⁷Sr/⁸⁶Sr close to 0.7090 and high strontium, which points to modern marine shell (about 0.7092) in the batch. Natron glass from Tell el-Ashmunein in Middle Egypt has ratios of 0.70794–0.70798, consistent with limestone or limestone-rich sand (Freestone et al. 2003). Brems et al. (2018) confirm the shell signature for Apollonia and Bet Eli'ezer. They also warn that contamination from the furnace walls can alter the Sr isotopic composition.

**Flux source: boron isotopes.** In the Apollonia and Bet Eli'ezer raw glass, boron ranges from 37 to 60 ppm and δ¹¹B from +22.7 to +29.9‰. These values lie close to those of natron from Egypt, Libya and Tunisia and exclude Lake Pikrolimni in Greece (Brems et al. 2018, comparing with the data of Devulder et al. 2014). The authors conclude that Egyptian evaporitic lakes are the most likely source, "although other North-African sources cannot be excluded". Brems et al. (2018) also suggest that the lower soda of the later tank-furnace glass reflects a different recipe with less natron. This fits a trend of declining natron supply.

**Separating Egypt from the Levant: hafnium isotopes.** Barfod et al. (2020) show that Hf isotopes differ between Egyptian and Levantine natron glass, even where Sr and Nd overlap. They show that antimony-decolourised Roman glass is Egyptian. They attribute the difference to the sorting of zircons in Nile sediments during transport along the coast. At Jalame, the Hf isotopic composition falls within the Levantine range despite the added manganese (Freestone et al. 2023). Barfod et al. (2025) note that the alumina content of known primary glass falls from south to north along the Syro-Palestinian coast. On this basis they place one Hellenistic group (IZT-1, before 107 BCE) north of Beirut.

**Jalame re-examined.** Freestone et al. (2023) reanalysed 42 samples from the 1964–1971 excavations by EPMA, with LA-ICP-MS for trace elements on 29 samples and MC-ICP-MS for Hf isotopes on six. They confirm that Jalame glass has more alumina than Roman manganese-decolourised glass of the 1st–3rd centuries in Europe. They suggest that the source of the earlier Roman glass lies further north on the eastern Mediterranean coast. The cobalt pigment found at the site is of the high-nickel late antique type. This pushes the introduction of that cobalt source back from the sixth to the fourth century CE (Freestone et al. 2023).

**Chronology and end of production.** Phelps et al. (2016) analysed 133 vessels dated from the 7th to the 12th century. They place a fairly abrupt compositional change in the late 7th to early 8th century, which marks the establishment of the Bet Eli'ezer furnaces. Production of Bet Eli'ezer-type glass seems to have lasted less than 50 years, after which natron glass production in Palestine ceased. Egypt continued to make natron glass for up to a century longer. Plant-ash glass first appears in the late 8th or early 9th century. The authors argue that the collapse reflects a gradual reduction in the natron available at its source (Phelps et al. 2016).

**Experiment.** Jackson et al. (2018) made glass with natron from el-Barnugi in the Nile delta, a site that probably also supplied Roman glassmakers. They state that previous experiments with Egyptian natron had been unsuccessful. Their experiments show that "high-quality glass, free of unreacted batch or bubbles, could have been produced from natron in its unprocessed form in a single stage". They also show that more natron is needed than previously thought, that the mixture of sodium compounds in the deposit helps melting, and that negligible waste is produced. These are laboratory-scale melts. No experiment has yet reproduced a multi-tonne tank melt, as far as this review could establish.

## 5. Discussion: what remains lost

The *organisation* of the industry is now well understood. A small number of primary sites on the Levantine coast and in Egypt melted very large batches and exported chunks. The isotopic tools for assigning glass to these sources (Sr, Nd, B, Hf) are maturing. What remains lost is the operating practice inside the furnace.

- **Temperature.** The only figures are a theoretical melting temperature (Nenna 2010) and a numerical simulation of one site (reported by Brems et al. 2018). No study cited here has measured a firing temperature on ancient material, for example from furnace refractories or reaction rims, for any tank furnace.
- **Duration.** Estimates ("two weeks or more", "many weeks", 30 days) rest on furnace size, an ethnographic analogy with Jalesar and the cited secondary literature. None is a measurement.
- **Batch formulation and charging.** The recipe is reconstructed backwards from glass compositions. Brems et al. (2018) infer, for example, a lower natron-to-sand ratio in later tank glass. Pliny's "three parts" (36.194) is grammatically ambiguous and refers to Campanian sand, not to the Levantine tanks. Incremental charging is a plausible model (Freestone et al. 2000, as summarised by Brems et al. 2018), not a demonstrated one. The poor homogeneity of single melts shows that the batch was not well mixed (Brems et al. 2018). How it was charged in practice remains unknown.
- **Treatment of natron.** Brems et al. (2018) find more boron in the glass than in most present-day flux material. They raise the possibility that the salts were processed before use, or that a chloride-rich scum (gall) separated during melting, but call this "highly speculative". Jackson et al. (2018) show that unprocessed natron can work. Whether it was actually used unprocessed is not settled.
- **Atmosphere and colour.** The production of amber glass at the primary stage implies reducing conditions (Freestone et al. 2025). Manganese additions at Jalame imply a deliberate primary-stage decolourising step (Freestone et al. 2023). How furnace operators obtained and controlled these conditions is unknown.
- **The texts.** Strabo (16.2.25) reports that the Belus sand was carried to Sidon for melting. The excavated tank furnaces at Apollonia, Bet Eli'ezer and Jalame instead show raw glass being made on the coastal plain itself. The literary sources may describe a different period, a different stage (remelting rather than primary melting) or hearsay. None of the sources read here resolves this.

The README entry rated this process Medium to High. On the scale of this dictionary, "High" requires a reconstruction that reproduces the ancient product with matching analyses. That has been achieved only for small laboratory melts (Jackson et al. 2018), not for the tank process. This review therefore assigns **Medium**.

## 6. Open questions and proposed research

1. **Firing temperature from refractories.** Furnace bricks and lining from Jalame, Apollonia and Bet Eli'ezer could be studied mineralogically to bracket the temperatures and durations they reached. The lime-rich lining identified at Jalame (Freestone et al. 2023) is an obvious starting point.
2. **Mapping heterogeneity within a slab.** Systematic sampling across the depth and length of one tank's glass, extending the approach of Brems et al. (2018), would test the incremental-charging model: successive batches should leave compositional layering.
3. **Scaled melting experiments.** Laboratory work with el-Barnugi natron (Jackson et al. 2018) could be scaled up to a furnace of a few hundred kilograms built to the Beni Salama or Apollonia plan (Nenna 2010; Tal et al. 2004). The experiment would record temperature, atmosphere and homogeneity over time.
4. **Natron processing.** Boron concentration and isotopes in experimental glasses made from raw and refined natron could test the processing hypothesis raised by Brems et al. (2018).
5. **Hafnium mapping of the coast.** Extending Hf isotope reference data northwards along the Levantine coast (Barfod et al. 2020, 2025; Freestone et al. 2023) could locate the still-unknown primary centres of the 1st–3rd centuries CE.
6. **Textual work.** A fresh look at the manuscript evidence for Pliny *NH* 36.193 (*maxime Aegyptio* as a conjecture) and 36.194 (the "three parts") would establish what the text actually claims about the flux and the recipe.

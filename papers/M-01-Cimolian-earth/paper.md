# M-01. Cimolian earth (κιμωλία γῆ; *creta Cimolia*): state of the art

*The Apolothecary — state-of-the-art review. Version 1.0, 22 November 2026. Project DOI: [10.5281/zenodo.24422717](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24422717).*

**Certainty:** Medium — the island source and the fuller's-earth function are secure in the texts, but no ancient sample has been analysed and two different modern mineral identifications have been proposed. · **What is lost:** the deposit worked in antiquity, the mineralogy of the traded product, and its processing.

## Abstract

Cimolian earth was a white or purplish earth named after the Cycladic island of Kimolos. Greek and Latin authors from the late fifth century BC to the first century AD used it as a washing earth, as a finishing agent for cloth and as a medicine. This review sets out the ancient testimony, checked against online critical texts: Aristophanes (*Frogs* 712), Theophrastus (*De lapidibus* 62), Strabo (10.5.1), Dioscorides (5.156 Wellmann) and Pliny (*NH* 35.195–198, with many medical mentions). It then follows the modern identifications. Caley and Richards (1956) proposed a sepiolite-type clay that mineralogists had called "cimolite". Robertson (1986) wrote a history of fuller's earth framed around calcium montmorillonite. The geology of Kimolos is now well known. Christidis described smectite-rich bentonites with opal-CT and, in places, the zeolite mordenite. Tsolis-Katagas and Mavronichi described primary kaolins with alunite. The island therefore offers more than one candidate clay. No archaeological or museum sample of the ancient product has been characterised, and no ancient extraction site on Kimolos has been identified in the literature reviewed here. The review ends with a programme to settle the question: compare deposits, run fulling tests and search for residues.

## 1. Introduction

Cimolian earth is usually classed among the fuller's earths (Caley and Richards 1956, 209; Robertson 1986). Pliny describes its place in the fullers' sequence of operations (Pliny, *NH* 35.196–198). The name comes from Kimolos, a small island close to Melos (Strabo 10.5.1; Caley and Richards 1956, 209). For a dictionary of lost materials, the question is not whether the earth existed, since the texts are clear on that. The question is what the traded product was, mineralogically, and from which deposit it came. The answer is complicated because Kimolos hosts several types of altered volcanic rock: smectite-rich bentonites (Christidis 1998a, 2001) and kaolin-alunite assemblages (Tsolis-Katagas and Mavronichi 1989). An ancient author's "white earth" could correspond to more than one of them.

Two points emerge from the texts. First, "Cimolian" was already used by Pliny as a class name, since Sardinian and Umbrian earths count as *Cimoliae genera* (35.196). The name of the island had become the name of a product type, so not every *creta Cimolia* in Rome need have come from Kimolos. Second, both Dioscorides and Pliny describe two colour varieties, a white and a purplish one, and Dioscorides prefers the purplish one for medicine (Dioscorides 5.156; Pliny, *NH* 35.195).

## 3. Proposed identifications

**Sepiolite / "cimolite" (Caley and Richards 1956).** In their commentary on *De lapidibus* 62, Caley and Richards note that Theophrastus gives no description, so the earth "cannot be identified from what he says". They rely instead on Dioscorides' two varieties and "natural fatness", and on Pliny's use for cleaning clothes, which "at once suggests that it had the properties of a fuller's earth". They then state that the principal clayey material of this kind on Kimolos is "a particular variety of sepiolite, a hydrated magnesium silicate to which modern mineralogists have given the name cimolite", used in modern times for cleaning cloth, and "in all probability the same as the Kimolian earth" (Caley and Richards 1956, 209). They give no analytical data for this claim. The current status of the mineral name "cimolite" was not verified for this review.

**Calcium montmorillonite (Robertson 1986).** R. H. S. Robertson, a clay technologist who also wrote notes on Theophrastus' earths in *The Classical Review* (Robertson 1958, 1963), published a 421-page history of fuller's earth. Its subtitle, *A History of Calcium Montmorillonite*, identifies fuller's earth generically with Ca-montmorillonite (Robertson 1986). The bibliographic details were checked, and the book was reviewed in *Clay Minerals*, *Isis* and *Applied Clay Science* (Highley 1986; Multhauf 1987; Kühnel 1990). The book itself and the reviews could not be consulted for this review. Its treatment of the Kimolos material is therefore not summarised here.

**Kaolin.** The README entry notes that some secondary sources compare Cimolian earth with kaolin. No scholarly source for that comparison was found for this review. Geology, however, makes it plausible: southern Kimolos has primary kaolins (kaolinite with quartz ± cristobalite, commonly with alunite) formed by hydrothermal alteration of Pliocene–Pleistocene volcanics (Tsolis-Katagas and Mavronichi 1989).

The two competing modern identifications therefore point to different mineral families: a magnesian fibrous clay (sepiolite) in Caley and Richards, and a smectite (montmorillonite) in Robertson's framing. Neither rests on analysis of an ancient sample.

## 4. Analytical and experimental evidence

**Ancient material.** No published analysis of an archaeological or museum sample identified as Cimolian earth was found. The closest field programme is the work of Photos-Jones, Hall and colleagues on the "elusive earths" of neighbouring Melos. Their 1998 survey at the Roman site of Aghia Kyriaki was designed "with the long term aim of putting the elusive 'earths' on the archaeological map of ancient industries". It stressed how little material evidence the extraction and processing of industrial minerals leave behind (Photos-Jones et al. 1999, abstract). A follow-up geological study of the site found that hydrothermal alteration had produced "white rocks" with potential industrial minerals such as kaolin and alunite. It concluded that any relationship between the site and mineral exploitation "will have to be determined by archaeological excavation" (Hall et al. 2003, abstract). No comparable survey of Kimolos was found in the literature reviewed.

**Geology of Kimolos.** The modern deposits are well characterised.

- Christidis (2001) described an alteration profile in a Kimolos rhyolite. It passes from fresh glass to bentonite containing homogeneous Chambers-type montmorillonite, and then to a mordenite-bearing bentonite (abstract read).
- Christidis (1998a) measured cation exchange capacity, swelling capacity, pH, rheology and foundry properties of several Kimolos bentonites. These properties depend mainly on bentonite grade (smectite content) and on how far the smectite quasicrystals disaggregate. That in turn depends on the interlayer cation, the presence of mordenite and undevitrified glass, the oxidation state of iron, and smectite–opal-CT interaction. Glass shards and opal-CT reduce swelling, while zeolites increase cation exchange capacity (abstract as reproduced by the Technical University of Crete repository).
- Christidis and Scott (1997) studied the white Lower Pleistocene bentonites of Milos and Kimolos, which contain opal-CT and sometimes zeolite besides smectite. The Kimolos white bentonites are brighter and less yellow than those of Milos. Whiteness is inversely related to the abundance of Fe oxides and anatase, and to the amount and oxidation state of structural Fe in the smectite (abstract via repository record).
- A companion study compared the mobility of major and trace elements during alteration of andesite and rhyolite to bentonite on Milos and Kimolos (Christidis 1998b; title and metadata only).
- Tsolis-Katagas and Mavronichi (1989) described the kaolin–alunite zone of southern Kimolos (abstract read).

**Experimental work on fulling.** No modern experiment testing Kimolos clays in the fulling sequence Pliny describes (*NH* 35.198) was found.

## 5. Discussion: what remains lost

The texts secure three facts. The earth came from or was named after Kimolos (Strabo 10.5.1; Ovid, *Met.* 7.463). It was used for washing and finishing cloth (Aristophanes, *Frogs* 712; Pliny, *NH* 35.196–198). It was a medicine in white and purplish forms (Dioscorides 5.156; Pliny, *NH* 35.195).

The following remain lost:

1. **The deposit.** Kimolos has both smectite-rich bentonites and kaolin-alunite rocks (Christidis 2001; Tsolis-Katagas and Mavronichi 1989). No ancient working has been located in the sources reviewed.
2. **The mineral.** The sepiolite proposal of Caley and Richards (1956) and the montmorillonite framing of Robertson (1986) are incompatible as descriptions of a single material. Both are inferences from function and modern usage, not from analysis.
3. **The colour varieties.** The white and purplish kinds (Dioscorides 5.156) may correspond to the white and Fe-bearing bentonite grades that Christidis and Scott (1997) relate to iron oxide content. This is a hypothesis, not a finding of that paper, which does not discuss antiquity.
4. **The trade name versus the island product.** Pliny's "Cimolian" Sardinian and Umbrian earths (*NH* 35.196) show that the word could denote a function rather than a provenance in Rome.
5. **Processing.** Dioscorides warns against counterfeits (5.156) and Pliny records Cimolian earth as an adulterant (*NH* 35.36; 28.110). Whether the island product was washed, levigated or blended before export is not stated in any source checked.

## 6. Open questions and proposed research

1. **Deposit survey with an archaeological eye.** Map old workings on Kimolos and sample each candidate lithology: white bentonite, Fe-bearing bentonite, mordenite-bearing bentonite and kaolin-alunite rock. Characterise the samples by XRD (with oriented and glycolated mounts to separate smectite from sepiolite or kaolinite), cation exchange capacity and colour. This would test both modern identifications directly.
2. **Fulling test.** Reproduce Pliny's sequence (*NH* 35.198): washing, sulphuring, then scouring with the test earth. Use raw wool and dyed cloth, and measure grease removal, whiteness and colour recovery for each Kimolos grade. Compare the results with Sardinian or Umbrian clays if such samples can be sourced. This is the pilot project proposed in the dictionary entry.
3. **Residue search.** Look for clay residues in excavated *fullonicae* and on archaeological textiles, and compare their mineralogy and trace elements with the Kimolos reference set.
4. **Medical-earth comparison.** Run the mineralogical and microbiological protocols already applied to *miltos* and Lemnian earth (Photos-Jones et al. 2018) on Kimolos samples, so that the medical use reported by Dioscorides and Pliny can be compared on the same basis.
5. **Textual work.** Verify Robertson's (1986) treatment of the ancient testimony against the book itself. Collect later Greek medical attestations (Galen and others) that were not checked here.

# M-05. Lemnian earth — sphragis (Λημνία γῆ, σφραγίς; *terra Lemnia*, *terra sigillata*): state of the art

*The Apolothecary — state-of-the-art review. Version 1.0, 22 November 2026. Project DOI: [10.5281/zenodo.24422717](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24422717).*

**Certainty:** Medium — the island, the sealed-tablet form and a processing sequence are well attested, and post-medieval tablets have been analysed, but no tablet of ancient date has been characterised and the cause of its reputed efficacy is still a hypothesis. · **What is lost:** the composition of the ancient product and the exact preparation (rite and technique) that turned a local clay into the sealed medicine.

## Abstract

Lemnian earth was a medicinal clay from the north-east Aegean island of Lemnos, shaped into small tablets and stamped with a seal, hence the names *sphragis* ("seal") and, later, *terra sigillata*. Theophrastus, Dioscorides, Pliny and Galen all mention it. Galen describes a priestess digging the earth with a rite, washing it, keeping one layer and stamping it, and he says he took twenty thousand tablets away from the island. This review sets out the ancient testimony, checked against online editions, and the modern work. Since 2008 a Glasgow-led group has argued that the artefact was not simply the raw clay. Analyses of three sixteenth- to eighteenth-century tablets in the Pharmacy Museum of the University of Basel showed antibacterial activity that clays sampled near the presumed source at Kotsinas did not show. DNA sequencing of the two most active tablets detected fungi of the genus *Talaromyces*. A 2024 experiment co-cultured a related fungus with clays and tested the leachates on mice. All analysed tablets are post-medieval. The ancient product therefore remains uncharacterised.

## 1. Introduction

Lemnian earth is among the best documented of the medicinal earths of antiquity. Galen's eyewitness account of its preparation is unusually detailed. Stamped earths of this kind stayed in European pharmacopoeias into the modern period (Macgregor 2013; Venieri et al. 2020). It is therefore a good test case for this dictionary. The name and the place are known, and objects bearing the name survive in museum collections. Yet the ancient material itself has not been identified with certainty.

Two sets of questions follow. The first is philological: what do the ancient authors actually say about the source, the preparation and the uses, and do they agree? The second is analytical. Was the sealed tablet a natural clay, or had it been modified by processing? If it was modified, what did the processing add or remove? Hall and Photos-Jones (2008) phrased the problem in a way that has guided later work: "Lemnian Earth, the artefact, was not the same as Lemnian Earth, the raw material".

## 3. Proposed identifications

**A clay, with nothing special in it (nineteenth to early twentieth century).** Venieri et al. (2020) summarise the early chemistry. Lemnian earth was the first of the medicinal earths to be tested chemically, by Klaproth in 1807. De Launay (1895) analysed samples from the extraction pit and found "no more than just 'clay'". Thompson (1914) analysed sixteenth-century tablets and concluded that they were clay of no pharmacological value. These works are cited here as reported by Venieri et al. (2020). They have not been consulted directly.

**The documentary history (twentieth century).** Hasluck (1910) and the later survey by Macgregor (2013) put Lemnian earth in a long sequence of stamped medicinal earths. The abstract of Macgregor (2013) describes these clays as recovered in bulk, made into small stamped pastilles, and later imitated from other sources in central and northern Europe. It also says he identifies the products by their seals and assesses the claims made for them.

**The artefact is not the raw material (2008 onwards).** Hall and Photos-Jones (2008) argued from the documentary evidence, the geochemistry of the raw material and the extraction ritual. They concluded that the sealed product was different from the clay in the ground, and that the ritual might preserve physical and chemical steps of processing. The Oxbow chapter by Photos-Jones and Hall (2014) brings Lemnian earth together with alum and astringency. This review has read only its title, so no claim from it is reported. Photos-Jones et al. (2017) gave the presumed extraction locality as Kotsinas, in north-east Lemnos.

**A clay with a fungal component (2017–2024).** The Basel analyses (Section 4) led the same group to propose that the active part of the post-medieval tablets was organic. They proposed a fungal secondary metabolite (Photos-Jones et al. 2017) and then the fungus *Talaromyces* itself (Christidis et al. 2020). Christidis et al. (2020) say in their abstract that there is "historical evidence to suggest that the addition of a fungal component may have been deliberate". In the body of the same paper, they write that "we do not know how the LEs examined here acquired their specific organic load". Venieri et al. (2020) link this hypothesis to Galen's account of the extraction (Kühn XII.169–170). Milling et al. (2024) took the hypothesis that Lemnian earth was "an artificially modified" clay "involving a clay-fungus interaction" as the starting point for an experiment. Photos-Jones (2023) has also discussed Lemnian earth as *miltos*, a pigment. Only the bibliographic record and title of that article were available for this review.

## 4. Analytical and experimental evidence

**Basel tablets, qualitative stage.** Photos-Jones et al. (2017) studied three Lemnian *sphragides* in the Museum for the History of Pharmacy, University of Basel, dated to the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. They compared them with clays from Kotsinas. Their abstract reports a probable local origin for the tablets. All three had a significant antibacterial effect against *Staphylococcus aureus* but none against *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*. The Kotsinas clays had no effect on *S. aureus*. Ultra-performance liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS) found organic constituents in one tablet that were absent from a modern clay, and the authors proposed a fungal secondary metabolite as the active ingredient.

**Basel tablets, quantitative stage and DNA.** Christidis et al. (2020) re-examined the same three Lemnian tablets (museum numbers 700.4, 700.17 and 700.18) together with three Silesian earths. They used ICP-MS for chemistry, XRD and TEM-EDX for mineralogy and DNA sequencing for the microbial load, and they measured MIC₆₀ against *P. aeruginosa* and *S. aureus*. The accepted manuscript was read. Only two Lemnian tablets, 700.17 and 700.18, were active against both pathogens. 700.4, which is mainly dolomitic, was active against the Gram-positive organism only. Reference smectites and kaolinites were inactive, but became antibacterial when spiked with boron or aluminium. The two active tablets were deficient in both elements, so the authors ruled these out as the cause. Both contained *Talaromyces* spp. with certainty. The authors concluded that the fungal load, not the bacterial one, drives the bioactivity. Natural clays from Lemnos contained neither the fungus nor any activity. Venieri et al. (2020) give the bulk mineralogy of the two active tablets. 700.17 (red) contains 37.6 % kaolinite, 41 % illite, 17.7 % quartz and 3.8 % hematite. 700.18 (yellow-grey) contains 66 % montmorillonite, 18.1 % illite, 6.9 % quartz and 9 % albite. They also report that bioxanthracene, a fungal secondary metabolite, was detected in 700.18.

**Experimental reconstruction.** Milling et al. (2024) co-cultured the fungus *Penicillium purpurogenum* with a smectite slurry and with a kaolin slurry. They write that smectite and kaolin "are the principal constituents of LE". The leachate of the fungus–smectite culture inhibited Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria *in vitro*. Added to the diet of mice, it increased intestinal microbial diversity and short-chain fatty acid production. The fungus–kaolin culture did not have these effects. The authors suggest that the therapeutic properties of Lemnian earth "may have been based on the potential for modulating the gut microbiome". This is a test of a model material made in the laboratory, not of any historical tablet.

**Limits of the evidence.** Every analysed object is post-medieval. Photos-Jones et al. (2017) date the three tablets to the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, and Christidis et al. (2020) date the Basel group to the sixteenth to eighteenth centuries. Christidis et al. (2020) note that the practice "appears to have waned or stopped completely" in late antiquity and early Byzantine times and was revived under Ottoman rule. They base this on Hasluck and Hasluck (1929) and Tourptsoglou-Stephanidou (1986). Neither work was consulted here. If they are right, the analysed tablets come from a revived tradition and cannot stand in for the ancient product.

## 5. Discussion: what remains lost

The ancient sources agree that the product came from Lemnos, that it was sealed, and that its main use was as an antidote. They disagree on how it was made. Dioscorides 5.97 says it came from an underground, cave-like working, was mixed with goat's blood and was stamped with a goat. Galen, who went to the island and asked, says it was dug from a barren hill by a priestess, washed and decanted, and stamped with the seal of Artemis. He reports that the islanders laughed at the idea of goat's blood. Theophrastus treats Lemnian *miltos* as a pigment only. Pliny classes the sealed earth as a *rubrica* close to minium. Galen separates the sacred earth from the craftsmen's *rubrica* and from a fuller's earth found on the same island. The ancient name may therefore have covered more than one material.

The modern work has established three points, all for post-medieval tablets. The tablets and the local clays have broadly similar mineralogy. The tablets can be antibacterial while the local clays are not. The active tablets carry a fungal load that the local clays lack. Three things are still unknown. One is whether the fungus was added on purpose, entered during manufacture by accident, or was acquired during centuries of storage; Christidis et al. (2020) state that they do not know. Another is whether any of this applies to the ancient product. The last is which processing steps, beyond Galen's washing and decanting, were carried out. The recipe and the ancient composition are therefore lost, as the README entry says. The working hypothesis of a processed clay is supported by Galen's text. The hypothesis of a fungal active ingredient rests on early modern objects only.

## 6. Open questions and proposed research

1. **Find an ancient sample.** No *sphragis* of Hellenistic or Roman date has been analysed in the literature reviewed here. A survey of museum collections and excavation stores for stamped clay tablets of ancient date would come first. The aim would be to check whether any survive, and if so to apply the Basel protocol to them (mineralogy, chemistry, DNA, bioactivity).
2. **Test Galen's washing sequence.** Kotsinas clays could be processed as Galen describes (soak, stir, settle, decant, keep the "fatty" middle layer, dry to a wax-like paste, dry in shade). The fractions would then be compared with the Basel tablets for clay mineralogy, grain size and microbial load. This addresses the README's open question directly and needs no new hypothesis.
3. **Separate storage from manufacture.** Fungal communities could be sequenced in the core and on the surface of the same tablets, and in other clay objects stored in the same museum. This would test whether the *Talaromyces* signal could have come from the collection rather than from manufacture.
4. **Date the samples independently.** The Basel tablets are dated by their seals and by collection history. Any study of change through time needs dated objects from several periods.
5. **Read the primary documentary studies.** Hasluck (1910), Hasluck and Hasluck (1929) and the travellers' accounts collected by Tourptsoglou-Stephanidou (1986) are the stated basis for the Ottoman-period evidence. They should be read directly before any claim about deliberate fungal addition is taken further.

A stopping criterion for the first stage: the analysis of at least one stamped earth of securely ancient date with the same protocol as Christidis et al. (2020).

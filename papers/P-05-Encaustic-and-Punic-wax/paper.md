# P-05. Encaustic and Punic wax: state of the art

*The Apolothecary — state-of-the-art review. Version 1.0, 22 November 2026. Project DOI: [10.5281/zenodo.24422717](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24422717).*

**Certainty:** Medium. Pliny gives the steps for making *cera Punica*, and molten-wax painting is attested in the texts and on the mummy portraits, but the quantities, temperatures and chemistry of the product are not known, and no ancient painting has been shown to contain it · **What is lost:** the proportion and nature of the *nitrum*, the boiling times and the chemical state of the product; whether, how and where Punic wax was used in painting; the working temperatures and tool handling of encaustic.

## Abstract

Encaustic is painting with pigmented beeswax fixed by heat. *Cera Punica* ("Punic wax") is a beeswax treatment described by Pliny the Elder. For more than two centuries the two have been linked by the idea that Punic wax was a partly saponified, water-miscible wax that let painters work "cold". This review sets out the ancient evidence, checked against online critical texts. It then weighs that evidence against recent analyses of Romano-Egyptian mummy portraits, mostly from the APPEAR project. Pliny's recipe (NH 21.84) is a sequence of airing, boiling in sea water with *nitrum*, skimming and sun-bleaching. He recommends the product for medicine (21.84). The only painting use he names is a hot, oil-tempered wax coat on vermilion walls (33.122). The case for cold Punic-wax painting on the portraits has rested on metal soaps and on wax-ester depletion. Recent studies argue that lead white and ageing produce both of these, so neither marks a deliberate treatment. A beeswax with sodium-bearing ions on a Roman marble head has been read as Punic wax. Experimental Punic wax has been made in the laboratory. No study has yet produced a marker that tells the ancient product apart from aged beeswax.

## 1. Introduction

The word *encaustic* refers to a technique, not only a material: painting with wax colours that are "burnt in" (Greek ἐγκαίω) by heat (Sutherland, Sabino & Pozzi 2020). The best-preserved body of ancient wax painting is the corpus of Romano-Egyptian funerary portraits, the "Fayum" or mummy portraits. Some of these bear raised tool marks left by molten wax applied with small metal instruments (Mazurek & Spaabæk 2026).

The interpretation of these paintings became tied, long before instrumental analysis, to Pliny's description of *cera Punica*. Sutherland, Sabino & Pozzi (2020) trace how antiquarian experiments with encaustic began after the discoveries at Herculaneum and Pompeii. They cite the Comte de Caylus (1755), Vincenzo Requeno and Paillot de Montabert among the early experimenters. The theory of a modified wax medium was then promoted by Ernst Berger around 1900, but A. P. Laurie dismissed Punic wax as "one of the ingenious fictions that have so long obscured the scientific investigation of the classical methods of painting" (quoted in Sutherland, Sabino & Pozzi 2020). The same authors show how recipes put forward by Donner von Richter (1893), Doerner (1921) and Doxiadis (1995) passed into handbooks as if they were analytical results (Sutherland, Sabino & Pozzi 2020).

The Getty-led APPEAR project (Ancient Panel Paintings: Examination, Analysis, and Research) has since produced the first large analytical survey of the portraits' binding media. Its results come from fourteen participating museums (Mazurek, Svoboda & Schilling 2019). This review asks two separate questions. What can be said about the *process* of making Punic wax? And what can be said about the *process* of encaustic painting?

Two points follow directly from the texts. First, the only account of how Punic wax was made (21.84) appears in a passage on bees and wax, and its stated use is medical. Second, every passage that actually ties *cera Punica* to painting (33.122, and Vitruvius 7.9.3 if the reading *Punicam* is accepted) describes a wax **melted** with oil and **heated** on the wall. None describes a cold application. Pliny's three kinds of encaustic (35.149) are all heat-based: the third explicitly uses waxes "melted by fire".

## 3. Reconstructed process

### 3.1 Punic wax (*cera Punica*)

The steps attested in Pliny, *NH* 21.84, are:

1. Yellow beeswax (*cera fulva*) is aired repeatedly out of doors.
2. It is boiled in sea water taken from the open sea, with *nitrum* added. *Nitrum* is the natural sodium carbonate salt, natron (M-10).
3. The whitest top fraction (*flos*) is skimmed off with spoons into a vessel containing a little cold water.
4. The skimmed fraction is boiled again, separately, in sea water, and the vessel or the water is cooled.
5. Steps 3–4 are repeated three times in all.
6. The wax is dried on a rush mat in sun and moonlight under thin linen. It may be boiled again after sunning to make it whiter still.

Materials involved: beeswax, sea water and *nitrum* (M-10).

The modern disagreement concerns step 2. Sutherland, Sabino & Pozzi (2020) summarise the two readings. On one, the recipe is a purification or clarification of beeswax. On the other, the alkaline salt partly or wholly saponifies the wax, making it miscible with water and usable cold. The text itself gives no quantity of *nitrum*, no boiling time and no temperature (Pliny, *NH* 21.84). It therefore cannot settle how far saponification went.

Stacey (2011) prepared Punic wax experimentally following Pliny's method and analysed the product by GC-MS. She compared it with ointment residues from a multi-compartment Roman bronze container in the British Museum. In one compartment, she concludes, the beeswax "seems to have been prepared as the 'Punic wax' described by Pliny" (Stacey 2011, abstract). This is the only proposed archaeological identification that matches Pliny's stated medical use. The abstract does not give the chemical criteria for the identification, and I could not consult the full text.

### 3.2 Encaustic painting

From the texts, encaustic involved wax coloured with pigments (Pliny, *NH* 21.85, 35.49). The wax was applied with a *cestrum* or *vericulum* (a small pointed tool), or with a brush once melted by fire (Pliny, *NH* 35.149). It was then "burnt in" (35.122). The surface treatment of vermilion walls adds the use of coals to make the wax "sweat", then polishing with candle and linen (Pliny, *NH* 33.122; Vitruvius 7.9.3).

From the mummy portraits, some paintings show raised, characteristically shaped tool marks from molten beeswax applied with small metal instruments (Mazurek & Spaabæk 2026). Others look more blended and less impasted. These have been called "cold, modified, Punic or emulsified" wax paintings (Mazurek & Spaabæk 2026). Sutherland, Sabino & Pozzi (2020) note Petrie's alternative explanation: the warm Egyptian climate let painters work fast with simple, unmodified molten wax.

The pigments in the APPEAR samples are a restricted palette: lead white, red and yellow iron oxides, carbon black, red lake, kaolinite, quartz, and in some samples Egyptian blue (M-03), indigo, red lead and vermilion (Mazurek & Spaabæk 2026). At Tebtunis, a group of portraits attributed to one workshop used "wax-based and lead white–rich paint" with iron pigments (haematite, goethite, jarosite; cf. M-04), Egyptian blue, minium, indigo and madder lake (Salvant et al. 2018, abstract).

## 4. Analytical and experimental evidence

### 4.1 Early analyses and the "Punic wax signature"

As summarised by Sutherland, Sabino & Pozzi (2020), Kühn (1960) proposed that infrared detection of metal carboxylates (soaps) could tell Punic wax apart from untreated beeswax. White (1978) then read a lowered proportion of wax esters relative to hydrocarbons in one mummy-portrait sample as evidence of hydrolysis during the preparation of Punic wax. Mazurek (2020) reports that an early paper identified "Punic wax" in the Petrie Museum portrait UC 19612 from its brushstrokes and from reduced wax esters and alkanes. Sutherland, Sabino & Pozzi (2020) add that similar claims of saponified wax, or wax mixed with soaps derived from oil, recur in later studies. They state that the claim that soap identification can separate deliberate saponification from natural ageing "has not yet been demonstrated convincingly in historical samples". I have not read Kühn (1960) or White (1978) themselves; their content is given here only as reported in the 2020 APPEAR chapters.

### 4.2 The APPEAR survey

Mazurek, Svoboda & Schilling (2019) analysed 61 Romano-Egyptian paintings by GC/MS, 51 of them funerary portraits. In the beeswax paints, the fatty acids were present as lead soaps (abstract). The same survey found that the so-called tempera portraits were mostly bound with animal glue rather than egg (abstract). Mazurek (2020) found that UC 19612 was chemically like most of the portraits tested: it held significant palmitic acid as lead soap, with fewer wax esters and alkanes. She concludes that lower wax-ester content than in fresh beeswax "does not necessarily mean it was intentionally modified".

Mazurek & Spaabæk (2026) took nine encaustic portraits from the Ny Carlsberg Glyptotek and the National Museum of Denmark and set out to test the soap criterion directly. They compared lead content (SEM-EDX on cross sections) with palmitic-acid soap and wax-ester content (GC/MS). Lighter, lead white-rich paints contained more palmitic acid soap. For example, the flesh tones of ÆIN 682 held about 15% lead and 43% palmitic acid, against 3% lead and 16% palmitic acid in its black paint. A few outliers did not fit the trend. The authors conclude that lead is positively correlated with palmitic-acid soap formation. The presence of soaps "should not be used as an indication of cold applied or Punic wax". Differences in beeswax profiles reflect lead content, evaporation, pigments and wax-ester hydrolysis, not a deliberate modification (Mazurek & Spaabæk 2026). They also report that, across over thirty wax-paint portraits, fatty-acid soaps are independent of painterly style (Mazurek & Spaabæk 2026). Spaabæk & Mazurek (2020) attribute the high palmitic acid in lighter samples of the same Copenhagen portraits to wax-ester hydrolysis and lead palmitate formation. They leave open whether an oxidised oil found in one sample was an intentional additive, contamination or restoration.

### 4.3 Experimental encaustic and Punic wax

Stacey et al. (2018) prepared a range of experimental encaustic paints and studied them before and after ageing by GC-MS, FTIR (invasive and non-invasive), X-ray diffraction and thermogravimetric analysis. Their abstract states that differences in the technology of production alter the properties and composition of the medium. Pigment addition and ageing modify these properties further. The abstract does not specify how the experimental paints were prepared, and I could not read the full text. Neri et al. (2021) cite this study for the point that sodium carboxylates form both in Punic wax and in alkali-treated wax, so a single compound cannot be linked to a specific processing technique.

### 4.4 Wax on sculpture: *ganosis*

Neri et al. (2021) analysed a sample from a colossal Roman head from the theatre at Dougga (Tunisia), dated to the end of the second century CE. They used video-microscopy, a cross-section and time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (ToF-SIMS). The red haematite layer is bound in a beeswax that is co-localised with sodium carbonate ions, which they call "Punic wax". It is covered by a further layer of this wax and finally by a layer of pure beeswax. They interpret the sequence as the application of the recipes in Vitruvius 7.9.3–4 and Pliny 33.122. Their reference materials included a commercial "Punic wax" and dehydrated natron. The identification rests on the presence of sodium salts in a beeswax layer, and the authors themselves acknowledge the specificity problem raised by Stacey et al. (2018). Neri et al. also describe the Dougga treatment as a "cold technique", in contrast with hot wax on two Hellenistic statues. Their summary of Pliny, which uses the old chapter numbers (35, 41 = §149; the recipe cited once as "21, 49" and once as "21, 149", the latter apparently a slip), assigns a cold, saponified emulsion to the third encaustic method. In the Mayhoff text, however, that third method uses waxes "resolutis igni", melted by fire (35.149). Pliny 33.122 also specifies wax "liquefacta candens". The texts therefore do not support a cold application as such.

### 4.5 Wall painting and the "wax and soap" hypothesis

Cuní (2016) reviews analyses of Roman wall-painting binders and argues that their results cluster by research group. He suggests that extraction efficiency and microbial contamination may be confounding factors. He reports emulsified-wax components in Roman wall paintings from Pompeii, Herculaneum, Oplontis, Rome, Gijón and several Spanish sites. He also argues that an emulsified-wax paint applied on fresh lime would become water-resistant as its soap turns into calcium soap (Cuní 2016). The earlier paper by Cuní et al. (2012) is the source cited for the hypothesis of a water-soluble "wax and soap" encaustic. I could not read that paper or its abstract, so its findings are not reported here. Cuní (2016) declares a competing interest: he is technical director of a firm producing water-soluble encaustic paint for artists.

## 5. Discussion: what remains lost

**The process of Punic wax is known in outline, not in substance.** The sequence of operations is clear in Pliny, *NH* 21.84. The parameters that decide what the product is are all missing: the amount and composition of the *nitrum* (M-10), how long and how hard the wax is boiled, and the salinity and alkalinity of the bath. A light treatment with a weak alkaline brine would mainly wash and bleach the wax. A heavier one would hydrolyse part of the esters. Nothing in the text says which was intended. The reading of the product as a *purified, whitened* wax fits the text's own emphasis on whiteness (*candidissima*) and on medical use. But I found no analytical study that demonstrates this reading, so it remains an interpretation of the text.

**The link between Punic wax and panel painting is not attested.** Pliny gives the recipe in book 21, on flowers and bees, not in his history of painting in book 35. In book 33, on metals and their products (33.122), he gives a hot, oil-tempered use on walls. He never says that portraits were painted with it. The "cold Punic wax" model of mummy-portrait painting was built on antiquarian and early twentieth-century readings (Sutherland, Sabino & Pozzi 2020). Its analytical props, metal soaps and wax-ester depletion, are now explained by lead white and ageing (Mazurek 2020; Mazurek & Spaabæk 2026).

**No discriminating marker exists yet.** The soap criterion has been shown to be ambiguous (Mazurek & Spaabæk 2026). The sodium-salt criterion used on the Dougga head faces a problem its authors themselves note: sodium carboxylates form in both Punic wax and alkali-treated wax (Neri et al. 2021). A positive archaeological identification of *cera Punica* would need a marker that survives centuries of burial and conservation and that differs from aged beeswax mixed with soap-forming pigments such as lead white (Mazurek & Spaabæk 2026). On the evidence reviewed here, such a marker has not been established.

**Encaustic as a craft is only partly recovered.** Tool marks show that molten wax was worked with metal instruments (Mazurek & Spaabæk 2026), and the texts name the *cestrum*, the brush and the burning-in (Pliny, *NH* 35.122, 35.149). Working temperatures, how wax was kept liquid, whether panels were warmed, and how much additive (oil, resin) was deliberate are not known. Sutherland, Sabino & Pozzi (2020) list oils and resins detected in some portraits but say a wider body of well-provenanced data is needed to decide whether they are deliberate additives.

## 6. Open questions and proposed research

1. **A parameter-mapped replication of *NH* 21.84.** Vary *nitrum* concentration (natural natron rather than pure sodium carbonate), number of cycles and sun exposure. Characterise each product by GC-MS, FTIR and ToF-SIMS, and age it artificially with and without lead white. This would test whether any Pliny-compatible product is chemically distinct from aged beeswax. It would extend the work of Stacey (2011) and Stacey et al. (2018).
2. **Sodium and salt markers.** Assess whether sodium carboxylates or residual chlorides and carbonates survive burial and can be separated from soil salts and conservation residues, building on Neri et al. (2021).
3. **Pigment-free controls on portraits.** Sample paints with little or no lead (carbon black, iron oxides) to test wax-ester hydrolysis independently of lead-soap formation, following the outliers noted by Mazurek & Spaabæk (2026).
4. **The textual question.** Establish from a critical apparatus whether Vitruvius 7.9.3 reads *ceram Punicam* or *ceram Ponticam*, and whether the Greek term is γάνωσις or καῦσις. Then check Dioscorides' chapter on wax against Wellmann's edition.
5. **Medicinal residues.** Analyse more Roman medical containers for wax treated as in Stacey (2011), since that is the use Pliny states.
6. **Shared terminology.** Adopt the usage proposed by Sutherland, Sabino & Pozzi (2020): "encaustic" only where heat application is visible, "wax" or "beeswax" where the binder is identified without such evidence. Drop "Punic" as a label for painting style.

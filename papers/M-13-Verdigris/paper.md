# M-13. Verdigris (ἰός; *aerugo*, *aeruca*): state of the art

*The Apolothecary — state-of-the-art review. Version 1.0, 22 November 2026. Project DOI: [10.5281/zenodo.24422717](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24422717).*

**Certainty:** Medium — the ancient recipes describe the corrosion of copper by vinegar or fermenting grape residues, which places the products among the copper acetates; but no ancient sample made by these recipes has been analysed, and modern reconstructions show that one recipe can give several phases. · **What is lost:** the phase composition of each ancient product, the "worm" verdigris (*scolex*) recipe's product, and most of the archaeological record of its use as a pigment.

## Abstract

Verdigris (Greek ἰός, Latin *aerugo*, called *aeruca* by Vitruvius) was a manufactured copper green used in medicine, in goldsmiths' work and, according to Theophrastus and Vitruvius, as a pigment. The ancient descriptions are unusually precise. Theophrastus (*De lapidibus* 57), Vitruvius (7.12.1), Dioscorides (5.79 Wellmann) and Pliny (*NH* 34.110–116) describe copper exposed to vinegar vapour, to sour grape-marc, or to vinegar sprinkled on filings, and a separate "worm" verdigris (σκώληξ, *scolex*) ground with vinegar, alum and salt or natron. All of these passages were checked against online critical texts. Modern chemistry has shown that "verdigris" is a family of neutral and basic copper acetates. At least five phases have solved crystal structures, and several phases in historic samples remain unidentified (Bette, Eggert and Dinnebier 2023). Reproducing an old recipe gives a mixture of phases (San Andrés et al. 2010). Archaeological identifications before AD 500 are few. A copper carboxylate green attributed to verdigris in beeswax has been found on a second-century Romano-Egyptian portrait (Radpour et al. 2022). The products of the ancient recipes have not yet been reproduced and analysed one by one.

## 1. Introduction

Verdigris is one of the few ancient materials for which we have several independent manufacturing recipes. The question for this dictionary is therefore not what kind of substance it was. The recipes agree that it was the green crust produced when copper is corroded by acetic acid from vinegar or from fermenting grape residues (Theophrastus, *De lapidibus* 57; Dioscorides 5.79; Pliny, *NH* 34.110–111). The question is which compound or mixture each recipe produced. Modern work shows that the name covers many chemical compounds. All contain copper. They also contain acetate, formate, hydroxide and water, and sometimes chloride, carbonate or sulphate (Bette, Eggert and Dinnebier 2023). The ancient recipes vary the copper (red copper, Cyprian copper, "white" copper), the acid source (vinegar, grape-marc), the contact (vapour, immersion, burial, sprinkling) and the additives (alum, salt, natron, children's urine). Each variation could change the product. None of these variations has yet been tested systematically against the texts.

Two terminological points matter. First, verdigris must be kept apart from the other copper products that the same authors describe. "Flower of copper" (χαλκοῦ ἄνθος, *aeris flos*) and copper scale (λεπίς, *squama aeris*) are by-products of smelting and casting, not of corrosion by acid (Dioscorides 5.77–78; Pliny, *NH* 34.107–109). Second, verdigris enters the goldsmiths' solder that Pliny calls *santerna* (Pliny, *NH* 33.93, 34.116). That solder belongs to entry M-09 and is treated here only where it concerns verdigris.

## 3. Proposed identifications

The ancient ἰός/*aerugo* has been equated with copper acetate since the early modern chemistry of the manufacturing process. Caley and Richards (1956, 191–193) note that the grape-marc method of Theophrastus is "in principle at least, still in use today". They describe the French process: copper sheets are stacked with fermented marc, crystals of copper acetate form, and the sheets are then exposed to air and moistened until a basic acetate crust develops. They identify the product of this process as chiefly "monobasic copper acetate". They also observe that verdigris had never been found in analyses of ancient pigments up to their time. Because basic copper acetate is not very stable, they suggest that a verdigris paint would probably have altered over the centuries into a more stable copper compound such as the basic carbonate (Caley and Richards 1956, 193). This was a hypothesis, not a finding. It explains why the absence of verdigris from early analyses cannot be read as evidence that it was not used.

The modern position is more exact and more complicated. Bette, Eggert and Dinnebier (2023) describe verdigris as "a collective term for synthetic copper based green and blue pigments" and organise the phases of the system Cu(CH₃COO)₂–Cu(OH)₂–H₂O by an x–y–z notation. There is neutral verdigris (the 1–0–0 and 1–0–1 phases) and several basic phases (2–1–5, 1–1–5, 1–2–0, 1–3–0, 1–3–2, 1–4–3, 7–1–4). The crystal structures of the 1–0–0, 1–0–1, 1–3–2, 1–2–0 and 2–1–5 phases have been solved, mostly from powder diffraction data. Historic samples and systematic syntheses also show "at least four additional, hitherto unknown pigment phases", and the thermodynamic stability and solubility of the phases are not known (Bette, Eggert and Dinnebier 2023, abstract). The blue 1–2–0 phase, Cu₃(CH₃COO)₂(OH)₄, was synthesised and characterised by Bette et al. (2017). The 2–1–5 phase, Cu₃(CH₃COO)₄(OH)₂·5H₂O, was obtained by long-term crystallisation from aqueous solution at room temperature (Bette et al. 2018).

On this basis, the most that can be said is this. Recipes using vinegar vapour or sour marc should yield neutral and/or basic copper acetates (Bette, Eggert and Dinnebier 2023). Which phases, in which proportions, is an open question for each ancient recipe. Švarcová et al. (2021) treat copper acetates as one group among the ancient copper-based greens and blues. The other groups are siliceous copper pigments, carbonates, chlorides and sulphates. In their abstract, copper acetates and other organometallics are covered only "to a lesser extent" than the mineral and siliceous pigments.

The "worm" verdigris is a special case. Both Dioscorides (5.79) and Pliny (*NH* 34.116) add alum and common salt, or natron, to the vinegar and copper. The chloride, sulphate or carbonate supplied by these additives could enter the product. Bette, Eggert and Dinnebier (2023) list chloride, carbonate and sulphate among the ions that some verdigris compounds contain. No study was found that has reproduced the *scolex* recipe and identified its product, so its composition is unknown.

## 4. Analytical and experimental evidence

**Reconstructions of recipes.** San Andrés et al. (2010) reproduced "an old recipe" for verdigris and obtained "a complex mixture of hydrated copper(II) acetates containing neutral verdigris and two varieties of basic verdigris". They combined Raman spectroscopy with FTIR and XRD, because reference standards were lacking. They assigned diagnostic bands to hydroxyl groups, Cu–O bonds and the acetate group, and used band wavenumbers and intensities to estimate the relative proportions of the components (San Andrés et al. 2010, abstract). The abstract does not say which historical recipe was reproduced, and it was not one of the Greek or Latin recipes discussed here. The general lesson is that a single recipe is expected to give a mixture.

**Archaeological identifications.** Positive identifications of verdigris on objects from Greco-Roman antiquity are rare in the literature reviewed here.

- A Romano-Egyptian funerary portrait of the second century AD was studied by reflectance and luminescence imaging spectroscopy with complementary point analyses (Radpour et al. 2022). The green leaves over the pink and white garments map to a spectral endmember "similar to that of copper carboxylate, most likely produced by mixing copper acetate (i.e. verdigris) with heated beeswax". FTIR of a sample of the green over the white garment identified verdigris by its characteristic bands. The authors conclude that "verdigris appears to be the source material for the copper carboxylate pigment" (Radpour et al. 2022). For the verdigris-and-beeswax formulation they cite Daniels (2007), a study of copper- and beeswax-containing green paint on Egyptian antiquities.
- In a technical study of Roman-period Egyptian mummy shrouds, Roberts (2020) lists verdigris among the copper-based greens reported on Egyptian artefacts in the literature. She found no copper-based green on the shrouds analysed: the only copper-containing pigment was an altered Egyptian blue that appeared green.
- At Pompeii and in the Vesuvian area, micro-Raman and FTIR studies of green wall-painting samples and pigment pots identified green earths (celadonite, glauconite), malachite, and mixtures of Egyptian blue with yellow ochre. They did not report verdigris (Aliatis et al. 2009, abstract; Aliatis et al. 2010, abstract).

These results fit Caley and Richards' (1956, 193) expectation that verdigris would be hard to find. They do not prove it. The few positive cases concern encaustic or wax-bound paint, where the copper is now present as a copper carboxylate rather than as an acetate phase (Radpour et al. 2022). This complicates any attempt to tell from an archaeological sample which ancient recipe was used.

**Methods.** Distinguishing the acetate phases requires powder diffraction and vibrational spectroscopy together. Most known structures were solved from powder data, and synthetic products are usually multiphase and polycrystalline (Bette, Eggert and Dinnebier 2023). Raman with FTIR and XRD can separate neutral from basic verdigris in a mixture (San Andrés et al. 2010). On aged paint, imaging spectroscopy can locate copper carboxylate greens, which must then be confirmed by molecular analysis of a sample (Radpour et al. 2022).

## 5. Discussion: what remains lost

The general identity of ἰός/*aerugo* is not lost. The recipes are clear enough, and they agree across Greek and Latin authors from Theophrastus to Pliny, so we can say with confidence that they describe copper acetate formation (Theophrastus, *De lapidibus* 57; Vitruvius 7.12.1; Dioscorides 5.79; Pliny, *NH* 34.110–111). Four things are lost or unknown.

1. **The product of each recipe.** Vapour over vinegar, burial in sour marc, sprinkling filings and grinding in copper mortars are different chemical regimes. Modern work shows that the phase obtained depends on conditions and that many products are mixtures (San Andrés et al. 2010; Bette, Eggert and Dinnebier 2023). None of the ancient recipes has been reproduced exactly and analysed phase by phase in the literature reviewed here.
2. **The *scolex*.** With alum and salt or natron added, the "worm" verdigris may not have been a pure acetate at all (Dioscorides 5.79; Pliny, *NH* 34.116). Its composition is unknown.
3. **The "natural" verdigris of the Cyprian mines.** Dioscorides (5.79) reports, as hearsay (φασιν), a verdigris that effloresces on copper-bearing stones and another that drips from a cave in the dog-days. Pliny (*NH* 34.110) says verdigris is scraped from the copper-ore stone. Without acetic acid these mine products cannot have been acetates. They may have been basic copper sulphates, carbonates or chlorides, but no study identifying them was found. This is the strongest textual reason to think that *aerugo* sometimes named a non-acetate copper green.
4. **Its use as a pigment.** Theophrastus and Vitruvius place verdigris among pigments, but Pliny does not list it among colours (Pliny, *NH* 35.30; Caley and Richards 1956, 193). Only one secure pre-AD 500 identification was found in the literature reviewed here, the Romano-Egyptian portrait (Radpour et al. 2022). How widely verdigris was used as a paint in the Greek and Roman world is therefore unknown.

The dictionary entry for M-13 (version 0.3) listed *aeris flos* as a synonym of verdigris. The ancient texts do not support this. Pliny's *aeris flos* and Dioscorides' χαλκοῦ ἄνθος are scales thrown off molten copper on quenching (Pliny, *NH* 34.107; Dioscorides 5.77), not products of acid corrosion. For that reason the heading of this article uses Vitruvius' *aeruca* as the second Latin name instead.

## 6. Open questions and proposed research

1. **Recipe-by-recipe reconstruction.** Reproduce each method in Dioscorides 5.79 and Pliny, *NH* 34.110–111 with copper of known composition: vapour over vinegar, immersion, burial in soured marc, filings sprinkled with vinegar, and the garland-makers' foil. Use wine vinegar and marc of known acidity. Identify the products by XRD with Rietveld refinement and by Raman/FTIR against the phases characterised by Bette and co-workers (Bette et al. 2017, 2018; Bette, Eggert and Dinnebier 2023).
2. **The *scolex* experiment.** Reproduce the "worm" verdigris with alum and salt and, separately, with natron. Grind it in a copper mortar, dry it in summer sun, and test the correction with urine. Analyse for chloride-, sulphate- and carbonate-bearing phases.
3. **Ageing.** Age the reconstructed products in lime fresco, in gum and in beeswax. This would test Caley and Richards' (1956, 193) hypothesis that verdigris paint converts to more stable copper compounds, and would show which alteration products an analyst should look for on ancient surfaces (compare Radpour et al. 2022).
4. **Targeted survey of collections.** Re-examine Greco-Roman greens and imitation-gem glasses, for which the Stockholm papyrus is said to use verdigris (Caley and Richards 1956, 193). Look specifically for copper carboxylates and acetate phases, not only for malachite and green earths.
5. **The Cyprian "natural verdigris".** Sample efflorescences in ancient copper workings on Cyprus and identify them. This would show what a miner in the first century AD could have called ἰός.

# M-04. Miltos and sinopis (μίλτος, Σινωπικὴ μίλτος; *rubrica*, *sinopis*): state of the art

*The Apolothecary — state-of-the-art review. Version 1.0, 22 November 2026. Project DOI: [10.5281/zenodo.24422717](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24422717).*

**Certainty:** High for the family (red iron-oxide earths, hematite-bearing); Medium for the named varieties, none of which has been matched to a geochemically characterised source in an archaeological pigment. · **What is lost:** how the Kean, Lemnian, Sinopic (Cappadocian) and other varieties differed, and how buyers judged them.

## Abstract

Greek μίλτος and Latin *rubrica* designate red earths used as pigment, for marking, on ships, in agriculture and in medicine. Several varieties were named and priced: Kean, Lemnian, Sinopic (*sinopis*), Egyptian, African and Balearic. This review checks the ancient testimony against online critical texts: Homer's "red-cheeked" ships; Theophrastus, *De lapidibus* 51–54; the Athenian decrees on Kean ochre (IG II² 1128); Vitruvius 7.7.2 and 7.11.2; Strabo 3.2.6 and 12.2.10; Dioscorides 5.96 and 5.126; Pliny, *NH* 33.115–117, 35.30–36, 35.50 and 36.147. Modern scholarship identifies *miltos* with red ochres and earthy hematite. Field and laboratory work on Kea suggests that *miltos* was an umbrella term that included purple as well as red iron oxides. Kean and Lemnian samples differ chemically, mineralogically and in antibacterial behaviour. "Sinopic" earth was Cappadocian, shipped through Sinope and later Ephesus, and a pigment still dug in Cappadocia (*yoşa*) has been proposed as its descendant. No provenance method yet distinguishes the varieties in dated archaeological pigments. Lytle (2013) reopens the question of why Athens sought exclusive control of Kean ruddle.

## 1. Introduction

As a primary pigment, red ochre had "manifold uses in antiquity" (Lytle 2013, 520–521). Its *identity* as a class is therefore not lost. Lytle (2013, 520) summarises *miltos* as "ruddle or red ochre", consisting "essentially of red iron oxides, but often mixed with earth, sand, clay, or other impurities". What is lost is the knowledge that made named varieties valuable. Theophrastus ranks Kean *miltos* best (*De lap.* 52). Pliny gives the price of *sinopis* by grade (*NH* 35.31–32). Athens legislated to control the export of Kean ochre (IG II² 1128). These distinctions imply properties that buyers could recognise but modern analysis has not yet tied to particular sources. The Lemnian earth used in medicine is treated separately in entry M-05.

Two textual facts stand out. First, the "Sinopic" earth was Cappadocian. Sinope (and later Ephesus) was the port, not the source (Theophrastus, *De lap.* 52; Strabo 12.2.10; Dioscorides 5.96). Second, Pliny's terminology does not map neatly onto the Greek: he equates Greek *miltos* with Latin *minium* in the same sentence in which he discusses *rubrica* (*NH* 33.115). The Greek–Latin correspondence μίλτος = *rubrica* is therefore an editorial convention, not a statement of any single ancient author.

## 3. Proposed identifications

**Red ochre and earthy hematite (Caley and Richards 1956).** In their commentary on *De lapidibus* 51–54, Caley and Richards argue that μίλτος covered "earthy hematite containing a high proportion of ferric oxide as well as red ochre in the modern sense". They base this on Theophrastus' remark that iron mines contain *miltos* (Caley and Richards 1956, 177). They reject an older view, held for example by Leaf (1916), that Sinopic *miltos* was cinnabar: cinnabar does not occur in ancient Cappadocia, whereas iron oxides and ochres do. They read Dioscorides' "liver-coloured" as pointing to an earthy hematite (Caley and Richards 1956, 179–180). They note that Kea is not named as a source by Dioscorides, Pliny or Vitruvius, and suggest that the Kean mines were exhausted before the Christian era (Caley and Richards 1956, 176). They also distinguish the Lemnian red ochre of Theophrastus from the Lemnian medicinal earth, citing Galen (Caley and Richards 1956, 177–178). The Galen passages were not checked for this review.

**Miltos as an umbrella term (Photos-Jones et al. 1997).** Geological fieldwork, physico-chemical analysis, heating experiments and simple laboratory tests on Kean iron oxides "corroborate previous observations about the superior quality of some Kean iron oxides". The authors propose that *miltos* may have been "an industrial mineral" and "an umbrella term for a variety of materials including mineralogically distinct purple as well as red iron oxides" (Photos-Jones et al. 1997, abstract).

**Sinopis as Cappadocian *yoşa* (Kassab Tezgör 2022).** Kassab Tezgör combines the texts with archaeological evidence and with *yoşa*, a red pigment still extracted in Cappadocia and used by the potters of Avanos. She argues that *yoşa* is "undoubtedly the same as the miltos of Sinope". She dates the shift of the export route from Sinope to Ephesus probably to the end of the second century BC. She suggests that export stopped presumably at the end of the third century AD, after which *sinopis*/*sinopia* came to denote any high-quality red ochre (Kassab Tezgör 2022, abstract and pp. 45–54). A preliminary XRD analysis of a *yoşa* sample identified hematite. She notes that "only trace analysis will provide the distinctive chemical signature" needed to tell it from other red ochres (Kassab Tezgör 2022, 54).

## 4. Analytical and experimental evidence

**Kea.** Photos-Jones et al. (1997) is the reference characterisation of Kean iron oxides; see § 3. The abstract does not give the analytical results in detail, and the full article was not consulted.

**Kea versus Lemnos (Photos-Jones et al. 2018).** Five samples were studied with physical and biological methods: four geological samples from Kea and one archaeological sample from Lemnos. The analyses covered bulk chemistry, mineralogy, microbial ecology and the nanofraction. The authors conclude that (a) Kean *miltos* and Lemnian earth/*miltos* "must have been chemically and mineralogically different". (b) The Lemnian sample would have been more effective against certain Gram-positive and Gram-negative pathogens. (c) Two chemically and mineralogically similar Kean samples nevertheless showed different antibacterial action, which they attribute to microbial ecology at genus or species level. They suggest that *miltos* entered Greco-Roman medical literature because of its bioactivity, not only its red colour (Photos-Jones et al. 2018, abstract).

**Cappadocia.** One preliminary XRD identification of hematite in modern *yoşa* exists (Kassab Tezgör 2022, 54). No trace-element or isotopic characterisation of Cappadocian red earths aimed at provenancing ancient *sinopis* was found.

**Ship and agricultural use.** Lytle (2013) argues that the literary evidence for marine uses of *miltos* is weak. He adds that "it is pitch that made ancient wooden hulls watertight" and that no evidence shows *miltos* had a necessary sealing role (Lytle 2013, 521–522). He nevertheless revives Hasebroek's view that the Athenian interest in Kean ruddle was naval. He bases this on overlooked archaeological evidence that ruddle was used in ship maintenance, and on agricultural writers who describe *miltos*, sometimes mixed with pitch, applied to vines and trees against rot and wood-boring larvae (Lytle 2013, 523–524). He notes that a modern experiment on biocidal effects (Katsaros 2008) was too short to be conclusive and wrongly assumed that *miltos* was red lead (Lytle 2013, 522 n. 8). Pliny's recommendation to drive off ants by smearing tree trunks with *rubrica* and liquid pitch (*NH* 17.266, checked against Mayhoff) belongs to the same agricultural tradition.

**The process of burning ochre.** Theophrastus (*De lap.* 53–54), Vitruvius (7.11.2), Dioscorides (5.96) and Pliny (*NH* 35.35) all record artificial red made by roasting yellow ochre. Caley and Richards (1956, 181–182) interpret it as dehydration of hydrated ferric oxide to anhydrous ferric oxide. Lytle (2013, 522 n. 9) suggests that Athens could have made a good red from its own high-quality Laurion yellow ochres. No archaeometric study distinguishing burnt-ochre red from natural red in Greek or Roman pigments was found in the sources reviewed.

## 5. Discussion: what remains lost

1. **What distinguished the varieties.** The texts rank Kean (Theophrastus), Sinopic or Cappadocian (Strabo, Dioscorides, Pliny), Lemnian (Pliny) and Egyptian or African (Pliny, Dioscorides) earths. They give criteria: density, weight, liver colour, freedom from stones, uniformity, spreading on dilution (Dioscorides 5.96), and the three shades of *sinopis* (Theophrastus, *De lap.* 53; Pliny, *NH* 35.31). None of these criteria has been measured on securely provenanced reference material.
2. **Whether "Sinopic" meant a source or a grade.** Kassab Tezgör (2022) argues that the name eventually denoted quality rather than origin. Pliny already lists *sinopis* from Egypt, the Balearics, Africa and Lemnos (*NH* 35.31), which supports an early generic use.
3. **The fate of Kean *miltos*.** The decrees show it was valued in the fourth century BC (IG II² 1128). Its absence from Vitruvius, Dioscorides and Pliny led Caley and Richards (1956, 176) to suggest exhaustion. That inference rests on silence.
4. **Why Athens wanted it.** Lytle (2013) offers a naval-agricultural explanation against the earlier consensus that there was no functional necessity (as summarised by Lytle 2013, 523). The question is historical rather than material, but properties such as particle size, iron content and binding with pitch could be tested.
5. **The medical dimension.** Bioactivity may have depended on microbial ecology rather than mineralogy (Photos-Jones et al. 2018). If so, the "lost" medical property of a given earth may not be recoverable from aged samples. See M-05.

## 6. Open questions and proposed research

1. **Reference collection.** Sample red-earth sources on Kea, Lemnos, in Cappadocia (including *yoşa* workings), in Egypt, North Africa and the Balearics. Characterise them by XRD, trace elements and, where informative, Fe or Sr–Nd isotopes, as Kassab Tezgör (2022) calls for.
2. **Archaeological targets.** Analyse dated red pigments, such as ceramic residues from the Athenian Agora, Roman wall paintings at Sinope and in the Bay of Naples, and *dipinti*, against the reference set. Test in particular Pliny's claim that the four-colour painters used Pontic *sinopis* (*NH* 35.50).
3. **Natural versus burnt red.** Develop markers (crystallite size, residual goethite, dehydroxylation textures) to separate roasted yellow ochre from natural red. Then test whether Athenian painters preferred imports or local burnt ochre (Lytle 2013, 522 n. 9).
4. **Ancient quality tests.** Reproduce Dioscorides' criteria (5.96) and Theophrastus' "self-sufficient" intermediate grade (*De lap.* 53) as laboratory measurements: density, colour coordinates, dispersion and tinting strength.
5. **Ship coatings.** Analyse red-pigmented pitch from shipwrecks for iron-oxide provenance, building on the evidence collected by Lytle (2013).

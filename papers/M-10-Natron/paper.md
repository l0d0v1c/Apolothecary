# M-10. Natron (νίτρον, Ionic λίτρον; *nitrum*): state of the art

*The Apolothecary — state-of-the-art review. Version 1.0, 22 November 2026. Project DOI: [10.5281/zenodo.24422717](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24422717).*

**Certainty:** High for the identity — a natural evaporite of sodium salts, chiefly carbonates, is supported by the ancient descriptions, the modern deposits and glass analyses alike. Lower for the map of sources and the reasons supply ended. · **What is lost:** the full list of exploited deposits and how they were chosen, the ancient treatise by Theophrastus, and a firm explanation of why the trade ended

## Abstract

Greek νίτρον (Herodotus' Ionic λίτρον) and Latin *nitrum* name a natural alkaline salt. Ancient texts attest its use in dyeing, cooking, medicine, embalming and glassmaking. Pliny devotes *NH* 31.106–122 to it. He names sources in Media, Thrace, Macedonia (the *Chalestricum*), Egypt (near Naucratis and Memphis) and Lydia, and he tells the story of glass discovered with lumps of *nitrum* (*NH* 36.191). Strabo places two *nitriai* above Momemphis in the Nitriote nome. Dioscorides ranks the varieties. Modern research identifies the main Roman-period source with the evaporite lakes of the Wadi Natrun and al-Barnuj (el-Barnugi) in Egypt. Precipitation there is seasonal (Shortland 2004). The glass family made with this flux dominates Greek, Roman and Byzantine glass (Rehren & Freestone 2015). Glassmaking experiments with raw el-Barnugi natron gave good glass but needed more flux than expected (Jackson et al. 2018). Natron glass production ended in Palestine in the eighth century and in Egypt up to a century later, plausibly because the natron supply failed (Phelps et al. 2016). Boron isotopes are being tested as a provenance tool (Devulder et al. 2014). Other possible deposits, such as Lake Pikrolimni in Macedonia, remain unconfirmed.

## 1. Introduction

The entry is one of the least "lost" in this dictionary. The substance still forms in the same places, and its chemical signature is preserved in millions of glass fragments. What has been lost is mostly knowledge about the trade: which deposits were worked, how the product was graded, and why the supply that fed Mediterranean glassmaking for well over a millennium dried up. Rehren and Freestone (2015) list both questions as active research topics: whether natron sources other than the Wadi Natrun were exploited in antiquity, and why the Wadi Natrun seemingly stopped supplying the eastern Mediterranean glass industry, even in Egypt, towards the end of the first millennium AD.

A note on terms. Caley, translating the Stockholm papyrus, wrote that "the 'nitron' of the Greeks referred to the natural deposits of alkaline salts from various sources", that the uses show it "to have been essentially sodium carbonate", and that "natron" is an acceptable inclusive term (Caley 1927, repr. Jensen 2008, note to Stockholm recipe 12). This review follows that usage.

## 3. Proposed identifications

There is no serious modern disagreement about what *nitrum* was in the glass and embalming contexts. Caley's note identifies it as essentially sodium carbonate, while acknowledging that it came from various sources (Caley 1927, repr. Jensen 2008). Jackson et al. (2018) state from analyses of Egyptian natron that it is a complex mixture of different sodium compounds. Shortland (2004) describes the precipitation of sodium carbonate minerals in the Wadi Natrun lakes. Rehren and Freestone (2015) define "mineral-natron glass" as soda-rich glass based on evaporite minerals, in particular from the Wadi Natrun, typically with less than 1.5 wt% each of magnesia and potash. That threshold separates it from plant-ash glass.

Two points are less settled. The first is how far Pliny's other *nitra* match the Egyptian product: the Macedonian *Chalestricum*, the Median *halmyrax*, the Thracian *agrium* and the Lydian *aphronitrum*. The second is whether Pliny's *nitrum* made from burnt oak (*NH* 31.107) was a potassium-rich plant ash, which would make the ancient category broader than the modern mineral. The text says only that it was rare and long abandoned, and gives no detail that would settle the question.

## 4. Analytical and experimental evidence

**The Egyptian deposits.** Shortland (2004) reviews how the evaporites form in the Wadi Natrun, lake by lake and for the wadi as a whole. The precipitation of sodium carbonate minerals is seasonal and probably subject to long-term fluctuation with climate, which matters for ancient exploitation, particularly for glass (abstract). The same paper states that the deposits have been used in crafts and industries for over 6,000 years (abstract). Jackson et al. (2018) describe el-Barnugi in the Nile Delta as a site that probably also supplied Roman glassmakers (abstract). Pliny's *nitrariae* near Naucratis (*NH* 31.111) lie in the western Delta. None of the sources read for this review discusses whether Strabo's *nitriai* "above Momemphis" (17.1.23) are the Wadi Natrun.

**Glass.** Rehren and Freestone (2015) place mineral-natron glass as the glass family of the Greek, Roman and Byzantine empires. They describe a Late Antique model in which a few glass factories produced glass from imported natron and regional sand. Phelps et al. (2016) analysed 133 well-dated vessels from Israel by LA-ICP-MS. They found an abrupt change in composition in the late seventh to early eighth century, a short-lived production at Bet Eli'ezer (under 50 years), and then the end of natron glass production in Palestine. The first plant-ash glass appears in the late eighth to early ninth century. Egypt went on making natron glass for up to a century longer. The authors argue that the collapse was probably caused by a reduction in available natron. Because Palestine was affected first and Egypt later, they suggest the cause lay at the source and was long-term and gradual rather than a short-term event (abstract).

**Experimental glassmaking.** Jackson et al. (2018) note that earlier attempts to make glass with Egyptian natron had failed. Using unprocessed natron from el-Barnugi, they produced high-quality glass, free of unreacted batch or bubbles, in a single stage. More natron was needed than previously assumed, the mixture of sodium compounds helped melting, and almost no waste was produced. They discuss what this means for identifying production sites and for the natron trade in the light of Pliny (abstract).

**Provenance tracers.** Devulder et al. (2014) proposed the boron isotopic composition of Roman natron glass as a provenance indicator for the flux. Only the title and metadata of this paper were seen for this review, so its numerical results are not reported here. Rehren and Freestone (2015) also note Nd-isotope work by Degryse and co-workers. That work shows that not all natron glass carries eastern Mediterranean Nd signatures, which may point to other, still unknown production regions.

**Other candidate deposits.** Dotsika et al. (2004) sampled the brines, sediments and borehole waters of Lake Pikrolimni (Kilkis, northern Greece). They describe the lake salts as of Na–Cl–(CO3–SO4) type, with strong summer evaporation and winter dilution (abstract). Later papers by the same group ask in their titles whether Pikrolimni was "a natron source" (Dotsika et al. 2009; 2012). Their content was not read for this review. Pliny's Macedonian *Chalestricum* (*NH* 31.107) makes any Macedonian alkaline lake a natural candidate. Rehren and Freestone (2015) also mention ongoing research on possible natron sources in western Turkey.

**Mummification.** Brier and Wade (1997) report a human mummification experiment, carried out in May 1994, that used natron to reproduce the Egyptian technique (abstract). Lucas's papers on natron in Egypt and its use in mummification (Lucas 1932a; 1932b) are early studies of these questions. Their content was not consulted for this review.

## 5. Discussion: what remains lost

Little is lost about *what* natron was. A good deal is lost about *where* it came from and *why* it stopped. Pliny's list of sources (Media, Thrace, Macedonia, Egypt, Lydia) and Dioscorides' grading (Brygoi, Philadelphia, Egypt, Carian Magnesia) show that ancient buyers knew several deposits with distinct qualities. Modern analytical work, as reviewed here, firmly ties Roman glass to Egypt only. Rehren and Freestone (2015) still list other sources as an open question.

The end of the supply is better dated than explained. Phelps et al. (2016) date the collapse in Palestine to the eighth century and point to the supply itself. Shortland (2004) shows that precipitation in the Wadi Natrun is seasonal and may vary with climate. Rehren and Freestone (2015) cite Whitehouse (2002) and Shortland et al. (2006) for the decline, but the arguments of those two works were not read for this review. Climate, over-exploitation and political disruption cannot yet be separated with the evidence summarised here.

The single most important ancient text on the subject is also lost. Pliny says that no one wrote about *nitrum* more carefully than Theophrastus (*NH* 31.106). Diogenes Laertius lists a Theophrastan book *On salts, nitron and alum* (5.42). Nothing of it survives beyond what Pliny may have used.

## 6. Open questions and proposed research

1. **Survey the alternative sources.** Sample Lake Pikrolimni and other alkaline lakes in Macedonia and Thrace, and any Lydian or Carian evaporites (Pliny's and Dioscorides' Lydian *aphronitrum*; Dioscorides' Magnesia). Measure B, Sr and Nd isotopes and trace elements, and compare them with Roman natron glass groups that do not match Egyptian signatures (Rehren & Freestone 2015).
2. **Test the climate hypothesis.** Date lake-sediment records from the Wadi Natrun across the first millennium AD and compare them with the chronology of Phelps et al. (2016).
3. **Repeat the el-Barnugi experiment** (Jackson et al. 2018) with Wadi Natrun natron collected in different seasons. Shortland (2004) predicts compositional variation, and the experiment would test whether it affects batch recipes.
4. **Read and summarise** Shortland et al. (2006), Devulder et al. (2014) and the Pikrolimni papers in full. This review had access only to abstracts or titles.
5. **Collect the fragments of Theophrastus' *On salts, nitron and alum*** preserved by Pliny and later authors in a modern commentary. This review found no such collection.

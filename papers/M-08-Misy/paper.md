# M-08. Misy (μίσυ; *misy*): state of the art

*The Apolothecary — state-of-the-art review. Version 1.0, 22 November 2026. Project DOI: [10.5281/zenodo.24422717](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24422717).*

**Certainty:** Low — the ancient descriptions place misy among the weathering products of the Cypriot copper-sulphide ores and describe its colour and texture, but no ancient sample has been analysed, modern authors disagree on its mineralogy, and the only experimental work identified could not be read in full. · **What is lost:** the mineral identity (a single phase or a mixture), the source deposits, and the way it was used in gold refining.

## Abstract

Misy was a golden-yellow mineral substance obtained above all from the copper mines of Cyprus. It was used in medicine and, according to Pliny, by those who purify gold. With *chalcitis*, *sory* and *melanteria* it forms a group of substances that later authors called "vitriols". This review sets out the ancient testimony, checked against online editions: Dioscorides 5.100–102, Pliny *NH* 34.117–122 and 33.84, and Galen, *De simplicium medicamentorum temperamentis ac facultatibus* IX (Kühn XII.226–229). Galen visited the mine at Soloi. He describes three superposed bands (sory at the bottom, then chalcitis, then misy) and says he saw misy grow on chalcitis in his own stock over the years. Modern identifications vary. Hoover and Hoover (1912) give two different ones in different notes: an oxidised pyrite, mostly iron sulphate, and a yellowish material, possibly ochre, impregnated with vitriol, in part copiapite after Dana. Montanari et al. (2024) produced yellow iron-sulphate phases by heating melanterite. They did not discuss misy, but their work shows how heat treatment changes such materials. No ancient sample is known.

## 1. Introduction

Misy belongs to a family of ancient mineral drugs connected with copper-sulphide deposits: *chalcitis*, *misy*, *sory* and *melanteria*, with *chalcanthum* (copper vitriol) close by. Pliny states that "chalcitis contains three kinds, of copper, of misy and of sory" (*NH* 34.117). Galen treats sory, chalcitis and misy as drugs "of the same kind of power", differing in fineness of parts (Kühn XII.228). The Latin word *vitriolum* appears only in the thirteenth century (Montanari et al. 2024). The grouping of these substances as "vitriols" is therefore a later one.

The problem for this dictionary is the same as for the other substances in the group. The names are well attested, and the descriptions are concrete (colour, sparkle, texture, behaviour on heating). But they describe products of weathering that are mixtures and that change with time and heat. Galen himself saw them change. Montanari et al. (2024) make the general point about yellow, red and black vitriols: "the identification of these compounds is significantly less straightforward, and often chalked up to Decknamen (code names) or unspecified impure ores".

## 3. Proposed identifications

**Iron sulphate from oxidised pyrite.** In their note on gold parting in Book X of Agricola, Hoover and Hoover (1912, p. 459) take Pliny 33.84 as a description of cementation with salt. They write: "There can be no doubt from the explanations of Pliny and Dioscorides that misy was an oxidized pyrite, mostly iron sulphate."

**A yellow, vitriol-impregnated material, partly copiapite.** In their note to Book XII (pp. 573–574), Hoover and Hoover (1912) quote Dioscorides, Pliny and Agricola's *De natura fossilium* on the four substances. They conclude that "misy is a yellowish material, possibly ochre, … impregnated with vitriol", and they report that "Dana (System of Mineralogy, p. 964) considers misy to be in part copiapite — basic ferric sulphate". The two notes in the same work are not the same identification. The first makes misy a ferrous sulphate product of pyrite. The second makes it a yellow material, possibly ochre, carrying vitriol, with Dana's partial identification as a ferric sulphate. Dana's work is cited here only as Hoover and Hoover report it.

**Agricola's genetic scheme (sixteenth century).** As translated by Hoover and Hoover (1912, pp. 573–574), Agricola held that "misy effloresces not only from sory, melanteria, and chalcitis, but also from all the vitriols", and that "misy is yellow or gold-coloured". This agrees with Galen's observation of misy forming on chalcitis.

**A weathering product of Cypriot sulphide ores.** Kassianidou (2013) describes the Troodos deposits as "massive copper sulphide deposits, mainly composed of pyrite and chalcopyrite". This review found no published study that samples the gossans or weathering zones at Soloi or Skouriotissa to match Galen's three bands.

## 4. Analytical and experimental evidence

**No analysed ancient sample.** No sample of misy from an ancient context, or from a museum collection with a secure ancient label, was found in the literature reviewed.

**Thermal behaviour of iron sulphates.** Montanari et al. (2024) heated melanterite (FeSO₄·7H₂O) and followed the phase changes by variable-temperature XRD. Melanterite turned fully into rozenite at 60 °C, and szomolnokite began to form above 75 °C. A mixed ferrous–ferric hydroxy-sulphate, which they call IS(OH), formed only when the sample was held at 150 °C for a long time. Anhydrous iron sulphate was never identified. Their "wet method" also gave a yellow powder from melanterite. They connect these products to the yellow and red vitriols of medieval and early modern texts, not to misy. Their results matter here for two reasons. A yellow iron-sulphate product can form from green vitriol at modest temperatures. And Pliny reports a view, which he rejects, that misy was made by burning the stone in pits (*NH* 34.121). Whether ancient misy was natural, roasted or a mixture cannot be decided from texts that disagree on exactly this point.

**Gold-parting experiments.** Celauro, Loepp and Ferro (2017) published experimental reproductions of ancient gold cementation and scorification in *Acta rerum naturalium* 21: 177–200. The bibliographic data were confirmed in the reference list of Berger et al. (2021), which Crossref holds. The article itself could not be downloaded for this review. The indexed summary seen in search results says that cements containing a natural weathered mineral complex called "misy" were tested, following Galen, Dioscorides and Agricola. No further detail is reported here, because the paper could not be read. Berger et al. (2021) carried out cementation experiments of their own to study silver and copper isotopes in gold parting. Only their bibliographic record was read, so their methods and results are not reported here.

## 5. Discussion: what remains lost

The texts agree on four features of misy. It is golden and sparkles when crushed. It is best from Cyprus. It is related to, and formed from, *chalcitis*. And it is caustic and astringent in medicine. Galen adds a stratigraphic observation from Soloi and an observation of change over decades in his own stock. Pliny alone records its use in purifying gold, at *NH* 34.121, and gives a cementation recipe with misy at 33.84.

The modern identifications disagree. A single reference work, Hoover and Hoover (1912), offers both a ferrous sulphate from oxidised pyrite and a yellow, vitriol-bearing, possibly ochreous material. Through them it also reports Dana's partial identification with copiapite, a ferric sulphate. Galen's and Agricola's descriptions of misy growing on chalcitis and on other vitriols fit a secondary efflorescence whose composition would depend on local chemistry and on how long it had been exposed. If so, "misy" may have been a trade grade defined by appearance, not a single mineral. That is a hypothesis. No analysis supports or refutes it, because no ancient sample has been analysed. The identity is therefore lost, and so is the role misy played in Pliny's gold refining: whether it supplied sulphate, iron, acidity or all three.

## 6. Open questions and proposed research

1. **Sample the Soloi and Skouriotissa weathering zones.** The README proposes this. A practical design would sample yellow efflorescences on weathered chalcopyrite and pyrite at several depths, as an analogue of Galen's three bands. Mineralogy would be measured by XRD and by Raman or FTIR, and chemistry by ICP. The results would then be compared with the ancient criteria: golden sparkle when crushed, hardness, a sandy texture without earth, and the behaviour of chalcitis on heating.
2. **Repeat Galen's observation.** Fresh chalcitis-type material (partly weathered chalcopyrite or pyrite) could be stored under controlled humidity and the phases growing on it monitored over months to years. This would test whether the product matches the ancient descriptions of misy.
3. **Read and replicate the gold-parting work.** Celauro et al. (2017) should be obtained and read. Their identification of "misy" and their cementation results should then be tested against Pliny 33.84 (gold with twice its weight of salt and three times its weight of misy).
4. **Search collections for labelled samples.** Early modern pharmacy collections, like those that preserved Lemnian earth, may hold jars labelled *misy*. Analysing them would at least fix what the early modern trade meant by the name.
5. **Check the pseudo-Aristotelian fragment.** The Troodos passage quoted by Kassianidou (2013) should be read in a critical edition. It is one of the few texts that lists misy with stypteria and sory as products of a named Cypriot mountain.

A stopping criterion for the first stage: a published mineralogical characterisation of yellow efflorescences from the Soloi mining district, compared point by point with Dioscorides 5.100 and Pliny *NH* 34.121.

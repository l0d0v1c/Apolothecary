# P-02. Attic black gloss: state of the art

*The Apolothecary — state-of-the-art review. Version 1.0, 22 November 2026. Project DOI: [10.5281/zenodo.24422717](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24422717).*

**Certainty:** Medium to High — laboratory reproductions fired oxidising–reducing–oxidising have been matched analytically to ancient black gloss (High for the principle), but the firing schedules and slip recipes of individual workshops are still inferred (Medium for practice) · **What is lost:** how the slip was refined and whether additives were used; the actual kiln schedule (temperatures, durations, how reduction was produced); how many firings a given vessel received; how bichrome black-and-red vessels were made.

## Abstract

Attic black gloss, the shiny black surface of Athenian black-figure, red-figure and plain black vessels of the sixth to fourth centuries BC, is a lost process in the strict sense: no ancient text describes how it was made, and it has been reconstructed entirely from the material. The consensus model holds that a fine, non-calcareous, iron- and illite-rich clay suspension was painted on the vessel and fired in a single cycle under oxidising, reducing and re-oxidising conditions; the painted areas vitrify during reduction, trap black iron(II) spinels (hercynite, magnetite) and stay black while the porous body re-oxidises to red. Analytical work since 2007, much of it by a Getty Conservation Institute team using electron microscopy and synchrotron X-ray spectroscopy, has shown that this single-firing model is an oversimplification for some vessels: relief lines, intentional ("coral") red gloss and layered red-under-black surfaces required separate firings. Zinc enrichment in slips has been read either as a trace of slip processing or as a natural feature of Attic clays. This review sets out the ancient evidence (essentially none), the reconstructed steps, the analytical and experimental record, and what remains unknown.

## 1. Introduction

Black gloss was obtained by applying a clay paint to leather-hard vessels before firing; physicochemical analysis and replication have shown that the paint was a fine colloidal suspension of iron-rich, non-calcareous clay with a substantial illite content (Solard et al. 2023, reviewing earlier work). The resulting surface has been characterised as a highly resistant potash–alumino–silicate glass coloured by magnetite nanocrystals smaller than 200 nm (Lühl et al. 2014). Mean compositions of ancient Attic black gloss compiled by Aloupi-Siotis and reproduced by Solard et al. (2023, Table 1) give about 30 % Al₂O₃, 46 % SiO₂, 5.4 % K₂O, 14.5 % FeO and under 1 % CaO.

The technique reached its technological and aesthetic peak in Attica between the sixth and fourth centuries BC; Attic black gloss vessels were widely exported, and "Atticising" imitations were made in other regions (Solard et al. 2023). The term "gloss" (as opposed to "glaze") has itself been debated (Solard et al. 2023, n. 1, citing Aloupi-Siotis 2020); this review keeps "black gloss", the term used in the dictionary entry.

The interest of the process for *The Apolothecary* is that it is a case in which the product is abundant (Attic black gloss vessels "flooded the Mediterranean pottery market", in the words of Solard et al. 2023), the principle is well understood, and yet the operating knowledge of the Kerameikos workshops — recipes, schedules, controls — has to be inferred from the pots themselves.

## 2. Ancient sources

No ancient text describing the preparation of the slip or the firing schedule was identified in the course of this review, and the analytical studies reviewed here all reconstruct the process from the material rather than from texts (e.g. Walton et al. 2015, whose abstract notes that "little direct evidence has been established" as to the refinement methods). The few ancient witnesses that bear on the craft are indirect:

## 3. Reconstructed process

The steps below follow the consensus model as summarised by Solard et al. (2023) and Cianchetta et al. (2015), with the evidence for each.

**3.1 Choice of clay.** The slip clay was iron-rich, poor in calcium carbonate and rich in illite (Solard et al. 2023; Cianchetta et al. 2015). Illitic clays are rich in potassium and, according to Chaviara and Aloupi-Siotis (2016) as summarised by Solard et al. (2023), disperse spontaneously in water without a deflocculant; the same authors identified five sources around Athens with suitable characteristics. Calcareous clay is avoided because a gloss with high CaO tends to flake (Solard et al. 2023, citing Chaviara and Aloupi-Siotis 2016). The ancient red earths of the dictionary (M-04, *miltos* and *sinopis*) are also iron-oxide materials, but none of the studies reviewed here identifies them as an ingredient of the slip.

**3.2 Refinement of the slip.** The clay was refined by levigation — settling out carbonates and coarse particles and collecting the fine fraction (Cianchetta et al. 2015) — or by suspension in water followed by peptisation and precipitation, and the fine fraction was then thickened by evaporation (Solard et al. 2023, reviewing Winter 1978 and Aloupi-Siotis 2008). Additives such as bone powder, wood, plant or seaweed ash may have been used to aid dispersion (Solard et al. 2023), but this is a hypothesis. Modern replications use deflocculants: Noble (1960) used sodium hexametaphosphate; Cianchetta et al. (2015) used sodium silicate and sodium carbonate at 0.25 wt %, diluted to a specific gravity of 1.2 and settled for 20 hours; Tomei and Jimenez Rivero (2023) followed a modern sigillata recipe (1,200 g clay, 2.8 litres of water, 6.5 ml sodium silicate, 72 hours' settling). High alumina and iron contents relative to silica are the analytical sign of this refinement in both Attic and Atticising glosses (Solard et al. 2023).

**3.3 Application.** The paint was applied to leather-hard vessels before firing (Solard et al. 2023). On red-figure vessels, different applications of black gloss — relief line, contour line and background slip — can have different microstructures (Walton et al. 2013).

**3.4 Firing, stage 1 (oxidising).** From ambient to the maximum temperature, around 850–900 °C, the vessels dry, the clay minerals dehydrate, and sintering and incipient vitrification begin in body and paint (Solard et al. 2023). Iron is present as iron(III) oxides, so the whole vessel is red.

**3.5 Firing, stage 2 (reducing).** As the temperature falls, the iron oxides of paint and body are reduced to the black spinels magnetite (Fe₃O₄) and hercynite (FeAl₂O₄); reduction also accelerates vitrification of the paint layer, which becomes impermeable to oxygen and seals the black oxides (Solard et al. 2023). In their wood-fired replication, Tomei and Jimenez Rivero (2023) closed the test window and added "abundant green leaves until thick smoke came out of the chimney" once the kiln reached 800–900 °C.

**3.6 Firing, stage 3 (re-oxidising).** On re-admission of air, the porous body re-oxidises to red hematite (α-Fe₂O₃), with maghemite (γ-Fe₂O₃) as a transitional phase, while the vitrified paint stays black (Solard et al. 2023; Cianchetta et al. 2015). In the replication of Tomei and Jimenez Rivero (2023), foliage was stopped once the temperature went below 750 °C; the kiln was sealed after about 10 hours of firing and opened after 48 hours.

**3.7 Variants.** (a) *Multiple firings*: some vessels were fired two or more times, with slip applied between firings (Walton et al. 2013; Cianchetta et al. 2015). (b) *Intentional red gloss* ("coral red") was made from a similar iron-rich clay paint but finished in oxidising conditions (Solard et al. 2023). (c) *Bichrome vessels* (black on one side, red on the other) may have been made in a single firing by stacking that shielded the red side from reducing gases, by using a coarser and therefore less vitrified fraction for the red, or by a second, purely oxidising firing (Solard et al. 2023, reviewing Aloupi-Siotis 2008, Tite et al. 1982 and Richter 1951).

## 4. Analytical and experimental evidence

**Nature of the black layer.** Maniatis, Aloupi and Stalios (1993), using TEM, SEM and laser reflectance, described the black layer as about 20 µm thick, mainly polycrystalline magnetite particles of 0.2 µm and smaller in an amorphous vitreous matrix, topped by a clear glassy film about 0.1 µm thick, rich in Al and Fe and poor in Si, which they held responsible for the sheen. Ingo et al. (2000), by XPS and SEM-EDS, concluded that the gloss was made from an illitic clay enriched in iron oxide particles, fired in an oxidising–reducing–oxidising cycle at a top temperature of about 950 °C, and reported iron grains with discrete Fe³⁺, Fe²⁺ and Fe⁰ areas in a vitreous Al–K–Fe silicate. Tite, Bimson and Freestone (1982) examined the high-gloss surfaces of Attic and Roman Samian wares by electron microscopy; their paper was not available to this review and is cited only through later summaries.

**Iron speciation and reproductions.** Lühl et al. (2014) developed confocal XANES to measure Fe³⁺/ΣFe in the surface layer and correlated Archaic, Classical and Hellenistic Attic sherds with reference specimens produced under oxidising–reducing–oxidising conditions at different top temperatures, using suspensions of different particle size prepared from raw illitic clays of Attica. Cianchetta et al. (2016) characterised replicates painted with a refined illite clay while systematically varying temperature, atmosphere and duration of each stage, so as to determine the conditions that give black and red gloss and to estimate ancient firing conditions by comparison. In the replication series published by Cianchetta et al. (2015), with ten-hour stages, black gloss on a red body was obtained only with the first oxidising stage below 900 °C and the re-oxidising stage below 700 °C, the boundaries moving lower with longer annealing; exceeding them reddened the intended black.

**Multiple firings.** Walton et al. (2013) compared relief line, contour line and background slip on one red-figure sherd by STEM and found that the relief line had a more melted microstructure, which "could only be produced through a separate firing, at a hotter temperature". On the neck of a red-figure hydria attributed to the Berlin Painter (J. Paul Getty Museum 81.AE.206.d.2005), Cianchetta et al. (2015) used full-field XANES and XRF with replicates and found hercynite in the black gloss, hematite in an underlying red gloss and a mixture of maghemite and hematite in the body. They concluded that a first, uniform slip was fired at high temperature (hematite formation requires about 950 °C) to make the red layer, and that the black decoration was then painted and fired in a second three-stage cycle at lower temperature. They note that such stratified structures were not often encountered in their studies, and that the case supports the view that Kerameikos firing practice was "more complex and less standardized than previously thought".

**Coral red.** Walton et al. (2009) distinguished two chemically distinct coral reds on late sixth- and fifth-century Attic pottery: one compositionally similar to the black gloss (LCM), one higher in calcium and magnesium (HCM). Walton and Trentelman (2007) suggested from trace elements that the HCM red's carbonate component kept it porous enough to re-oxidise during the final stage of a single three-stage firing, whereas the more refined LCM red would have needed two separate firings. Cianchetta et al. (2015) linked these two reds to two pathways observed in their replicates: re-oxidation of a still-porous slip, or vitrification so complete before reduction that the slip could not be reduced.

**Zinc.** Walton et al. (2015) measured 19 slips and their bodies by LA-ICP-MS and found Zn of 271–1959 ppm in slips against under 361 ppm in bodies. They speculated that the clay was treated with vitriol (zinc-rich acid mine runoff) to cause flocculation and remove carbonates, and noted the same signature in Corinthian and Etruscan black glosses. According to Solard et al. (2023), Chaviara and Aloupi-Siotis (2016) argued instead that high Zn is a natural trace of some ferruginous clays in silver-lead mining areas such as Laurion. Muşkara and Kalaycı (2021), using handheld XRF on ten black-figure vases in the Izmir Archaeological Museum, found more Zn in black gloss and added colours than in bodies and proposed that Zn/Ti ratios might help to distinguish painters. Solard et al. (2023) found Zn in only two of their gloss samples and considered it impossible to decide between processing marker and provenance indicator.

**Beyond Athens.** Solard et al. (2023) analysed Attic and Atticising black gloss of the fourth century BC from Manfria (Sicily), Iasos and Priene by petrography, pXRF, µ-XRD² and SEM-EDS. Raw materials and phase assemblages were very similar to Attic references; bloating pores suggest maximum temperatures of 850–950 °C; variation in hercynite crystallinity suggests variable reduction intensity, and surface appearance (shiny, metallic, matt) appears to correlate with mineralogy. They interpreted the shared technology as transmission by apprenticeship rather than imitation.

**Experimental archaeology in kilns.** Balachandran (2019) used experimental firings to propose a four-stage "sensory" firing alongside the three-stage chemical model, arguing that each stage had its own sensory signature; only the abstract was read. Tomei and Jimenez Rivero (2023) built a one-metre updraft kiln on archaeological dimensions and fired Hellenistic shapes coated with a sigillata-type slip; the black came out "dark, shiny, and uniform on most vessels", uneven where the slip was too thin. Their slip and clay were not Attic, so the experiment tests kiln management rather than Attic materials.

## 5. Discussion: what remains lost

The chemistry is no longer the problem. Iron reduction, the sealing of black spinels by vitrification and the re-oxidation of the porous body are documented by several independent methods (Maniatis et al. 1993; Ingo et al. 2000; Lühl et al. 2014; Cianchetta et al. 2015), and laboratory reproductions match ancient material (Lühl et al. 2014; Cianchetta et al. 2016). What is lost is the workshop knowledge.

*Slip preparation.* Levigation is inferred from composition, but whether Attic potters used deflocculants, which ones, and whether the Zn signature records a processing step or a clay source are unresolved (Walton et al. 2015; Solard et al. 2023). The recipes used in modern reproductions rely on modern chemicals (Noble 1960; Cianchetta et al. 2015; Tomei and Jimenez Rivero 2023) and do not tell us what the ancient potters did.

*Firing schedule.* Laboratory furnaces with gas control (Cianchetta et al. 2015) establish temperature windows, but not how a wood-fired kiln was driven into and out of reduction, nor for how long. The 950 °C stage needed for the Berlin Painter's red layer and the sub-700 °C re-oxidation in the replicates (Cianchetta et al. 2015) give boundaries, not a schedule.

*Number and sequence of firings.* The single three-stage firing remains the default, but multiple firings are attested on several vessels (Walton et al. 2013; Cianchetta et al. 2015). How common they were is unknown; Cianchetta et al. (2015) say explicitly that it needs further investigation.

*Workshop control and variability.* The fourth-century material suggests a traditional craft with small variations between workshops rather than a standardised industry (Solard et al. 2023). How individual workshops judged progress — for instance by withdrawing test pieces, as suggested by the Penteskouphia plaques and the replication of Tomei and Jimenez Rivero (2023) — is reconstructed, not documented.

## 6. Open questions and proposed research

- **Replication with Attic materials in a wood-fired kiln.** Combine the controlled replicate series of Cianchetta et al. (2015, 2016) with a full-scale updraft kiln of the kind built by Tomei and Jimenez Rivero (2023), using Attic illitic clays of the kind sampled by Lühl et al. (2014), and log temperature and oxygen partial pressure through each stage.
- **Deflocculant without modern chemicals.** Test the additives proposed in the literature (plant or seaweed ash, potash, bone ash; Solard et al. 2023) and the vitriol hypothesis (Walton et al. 2015), then measure Zn, Na, K and P in the resulting gloss against ancient values.
- **Zn mapping against clay sources.** Analyse Laurion-area and other Attic clays for Zn and compare with slips and bodies of the same vessels, to choose between the processing and provenance explanations (Walton et al. 2015; Solard et al. 2023).
- **How common were multiple firings?** Survey relief lines, coral red and layered surfaces across a dated series of workshops and painters, using the phase fingerprints set out by Cianchetta et al. (2015).
- **Ancient evidence.** Check the Ephesian decree (I.Ephesos 1420) against its edition and look for other epigraphic or papyrological mentions of "black pottery" production, which would be the only written evidence for black gloss as a recognised specialism.

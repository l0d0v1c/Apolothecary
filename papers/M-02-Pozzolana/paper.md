# M-02. Pozzolana (no Greek technical term attested; *pulvis Puteolanus*): state of the art

*The Apolothecary — state-of-the-art review. Version 1.0, 22 November 2026. Project DOI: [10.5281/zenodo.24422717](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24422717).*

**Certainty:** High — the identity (Campanian volcanic ash, mixed with lime) is fixed by the texts and confirmed by geochemistry and mineralogy of Roman harbour concretes. · **What is lost:** the working recipe (lime type and state, proportions in practice, placement and curing), not the material itself.

## Abstract

Vitruvius, Strabo, Seneca and Pliny describe a "powder" (*pulvis*) from the volcanic district around Puteoli, Baiae and Cumae. Mixed with lime, it set under water and made harbour moles possible. This review collects the passages, each checked against an online critical text: Vitruvius 2.5.1, 2.6.1–6 and 5.12.2–3; Strabo 5.4.6; Seneca, *Naturales quaestiones* 3.20.3; Pliny, *NH* 16.202, 35.166 and 36.70. It then summarises the archaeometric evidence. Coring of Roman maritime structures (the ROMACONS programme) supplied the samples. Trace-element work tied the volcanic component of a Baianus Sinus breakwater to the Phlegraean Fields. Synchrotron microdiffraction revealed Al-tobermorite and phillipsite. These formed both during an early hot stage and through long-term low-temperature water–rock reactions. Lime clasts point to "hot mixing" with quicklime. A construction site at Pompeii, abandoned in 79 CE, preserves dry lime–pozzolan premixes. Phlegraean pozzolan has been identified as far away as a first-century well-cistern in the Venice Lagoon. What remains lost is practice rather than substance. We do not know how the lime was prepared, what proportions builders actually used, how the mix was placed underwater, and how all this varied across sites and centuries.

## 1. Introduction

The modern word "pozzolana" echoes the place name Puteoli (modern Pozzuoli), as in the *pulvis Puteolanus* of Seneca (*NQ* 3.20.3) and Pliny (*NH* 16.202). Roman authors call the material simply *pulvis*, "powder" or "dust" (Vitruvius 2.6.1). Pliny explicitly calls it "the worst part" of the earth (*pessumam eius partem ideoque pulverem appellatam*, *NH* 35.166). The entry differs from most in this dictionary: the material is not lost. The ash beds of the Campi Flegrei are still there, and Roman harbour structures made with them survive and have been cored (Oleson et al. 2004; Brandon et al. 2014). What is lost is the technical knowledge that turned ash and lime into a concrete that hardened in seawater. Scientific work since 2013 has changed the picture of how that concrete works and how it was mixed (Jackson et al. 2013, 2017; Seymour et al. 2023; Vaserman et al. 2025). The related process entry P-01 (hot mixing of lime mortars) treats the lime side of the question in detail.

The Greek text checked (Strabo) uses ἄμμος, "sand", and ἀμμοκονία, "sand-lime mortar". It has no technical term equivalent to *pulvis Puteolanus*. The heading of this article reflects that.

The texts agree on the region (Baiae, Cumae, Puteoli, Vesuvius) and on the effect (setting under water). Only Vitruvius gives proportions, and his two ratios are not directly comparable. In 2.5.1 he gives sand to lime ratios for ordinary mortar. In 5.12.2 the ratio is "two to one" with no units, and the text does not say which component is the "two". The usual reading takes it as two parts *pulvis* to one of lime, but that reading is an inference from the Latin, which is elliptical.

## 3. Proposed identifications

The identification itself is not disputed. Ancient *pulvis* is the pyroclastic material (ash and tuff) of the Phlegraean Fields and the Vesuvian area. The texts name the region (Vitruvius 2.6.1; 5.12.2; Strabo 5.4.6; Pliny, *NH* 35.166). Trace-element analysis of a Baianus Sinus breakwater block confirmed that its pyroclastic rock "comes from Flegrean Fields volcanic district, as described in ancient Roman texts" (Jackson et al. 2013, abstract).

The modern debate concerns three other questions.

1. **The reaction chemistry.** The standard view held that pozzolanic reaction of volcanic ash with hydrated lime dominates the cementing fabric. Jackson et al. (2017) argue instead that long-term "post-pozzolanic" water–rock reactions with seawater were decisive. In their reading, this is the process Pliny describes as a mass "stronger every day" (*NH* 35.166).
2. **The lime.** Vitruvius' *cum ea erit extincta* (2.5.1) has usually been read as slaked lime. Seymour et al. (2023) and Vaserman et al. (2025) argue that quicklime was also used, in "hot mixing" (see P-01).
3. **The extent of the trade.** Builders far from Campania imported Campanian ash for maritime work. Oleson et al. (2004) list "the trade in the volcanic ash" among the subjects their coring programme illuminates. Dilaria et al. (2024) identified Phlegraean pozzolan in a first-century CE structure in the Venice Lagoon.

## 4. Analytical and experimental evidence

**Coring programme.** The ROMACONS project collected large cores from concrete blocks at Roman harbours and other maritime structures. It analysed the materials, the method of placement and the structural characteristics of the concrete, with attention to the wooden formwork (Oleson et al. 2004, abstract). The synthesis volume *Building for Eternity* presents this programme (Brandon et al. 2014). It was not consulted for this review.

**Al-tobermorite in lime clasts (Jackson et al. 2013).** A 2000-year-old block from the Baianus Sinus breakwater in the Bay of Pozzuoli contains Al-tobermorite in relict lime clasts. Its composition is aluminium-rich and silica-poor compared with geological occurrences. The concrete is about 45 vol% glassy zeolitic tuff and 55 vol% hydrated lime–volcanic ash mortar, and lime made up less than 10 wt% of the mix. An adiabatic thermal model of the breakwater gives maximum temperatures of 85–97 °C, with cooling to seawater temperature in two years. The authors consider the elevated temperatures, seawater and alkali- and alumina-rich ash critical to Al-tobermorite crystallisation (abstract read).

**Post-pozzolanic mineral cements (Jackson et al. 2017).** Synchrotron X-ray microdiffraction maps of cores from the Baianus Sinus and Portus Neronis breakwaters and a Portus Cosanus pier show Al-tobermorite in further locations: the leached perimeters of feldspar fragments, zeolitised pumice vesicles, and phillipsite fabrics in relict pores. Dissolution–precipitation, cation exchange and/or carbonation reactions with Campi Flegrei ash produced alkaline pore fluids. These fluids created pathways to phillipsite and Al-tobermorite crystallisation at ambient seawater and surface temperatures. The authors conclude that long-term chemical resilience "relied on water-rock interactions, as Pliny the Elder inferred" (abstract read; the paper is open access).

**Lime clasts and hot mixing (Seymour et al. 2023).** Multiscale correlative elemental and chemical mapping of relict lime clasts provides evidence that the Romans employed hot mixing, "using quicklime in conjunction with, or instead of, slaked lime". The authors propose that the clasts act as reservoirs of reactive calcium for later pore and crack filling. Modern lime-clast mixtures showed self-healing potential in tests (abstract read; full text not obtained).

**A Pompeian building site (Vaserman et al. 2025).** In Pompeii, Regio IX, insula 10, house 1, construction was interrupted by the eruption of 79 CE. The excavated rooms contained dry material piles, walls under construction, earlier walls and repairs. Petrography, SEM-EDS, XRD, FTIR, Raman and stable-isotope (δ¹³C, δ¹⁸O) analyses indicate that quicklime was pre-mixed dry with pozzolan before water was added. The mortar mixture could exceed 200 °C. The pozzolan at this inland site is Somma-Vesuvius material with leucite, sanidine and biotite in the lithics, not Phlegraean ash. The authors suggest that Vitruvius' account "should not be assumed to be exhaustive or temporally universal". They note that its wider application across the Empire remains to be assessed (full text read, open access).

**Phlegraean ash outside Campania (Dilaria et al. 2024).** Four mortar samples from a submerged first-century CE well-cistern in the northern Venice Lagoon are lime mortars with ceramic fragments and local sands. They also contain pyroclastic aggregate foreign to local geology. SEM-EDS and LA-ICP-MS on preserved fresh glass match it to the Phlegraean Fields. The matrices show M-A-S-H hydrates and almost no carbonation of the lime. The authors relate the find to Vitruvius 5.12.2 (abstract read).

**A terrestrial comparison (Jackson et al. 2014).** The Rome mortar of Trajan's Markets uses a different pozzolan: Pozzolane Rosse from the Alban Hills, not Campanian ash. A reproduction of this mortar gained fracture toughness over 180 days as C-A-S-H coalesced and strätlingite and katoite crystallised. Strätlingite plates reinforce interfacial zones in the ancient mortar (abstract read). The study shows that "pozzolana" in the Roman world covered several volcanic sources with different reaction products.

## 5. Discussion: what remains lost

Nothing about the *substance* is lost: the source deposits are known, and the ancient products have been analysed at the crystal scale. The *recipe* is lost in at least four respects.

1. **Lime state.** Vitruvius prescribes slaked lime (2.5.1). Two recent studies present evidence of quicklime and hot mixing (Seymour et al. 2023; Vaserman et al. 2025). How common each practice was, by date, region and type of structure (land or sea), is not established. Vaserman et al. (2025) say so explicitly.
2. **Proportions.** The only explicit marine ratio, "two to one" (Vitruvius 5.12.2), is elliptical. The one well-characterised breakwater block contained less than 10 wt% lime (Jackson et al. 2013). A systematic comparison between the text and the measured proportions across many structures was not found in the sources reviewed here, though *Building for Eternity* (Brandon et al. 2014), not consulted, may contain one.
3. **Placement.** Vitruvius describes caissons (5.12.3), and ROMACONS studied formwork (Oleson et al. 2004). How the mortar and rubble were delivered and compacted under water is described only in outline in the sources reviewed.
4. **Trade and selection.** Phlegraean ash travelled as far as the Venice Lagoon (Dilaria et al. 2024). How it was quarried, graded and shipped, and whether builders distinguished grades, is not documented in the sources reviewed.

## 6. Open questions and proposed research

1. **Text-to-mix trials.** Prepare marine mixes from Vitruvius 2.5.1 and 5.12.2 under both readings of "two to one", each with slaked lime and with quicklime (hot mix). Cure them in seawater and track mineral growth (Al-tobermorite, phillipsite) and strength over years, using the analytical protocols of Jackson et al. (2017).
2. **Lime-state census.** Apply the indicators of Seymour et al. (2023) and Vaserman et al. (2025), namely lime-clast textures, FTIR grinding curves and C/O isotopes, to dated maritime and terrestrial samples. The aim is to map where and when quicklime was used.
3. **Provenance network.** Extend glass-shard geochemistry of the kind used by Dilaria et al. (2024) to harbour concretes across the Mediterranean, to reconstruct the ash trade.
4. **Lexicon.** Collect the Greek terminology for volcanic mortar sand beyond Strabo's ἄμμος and ἀμμοκονία (5.4.6). No Greek equivalent of *pulvis Puteolanus* was found for this review.

# P-08. Synthesis of Egyptian blue: state of the art

*The Apolothecary — state-of-the-art review. Version 1.0, 22 November 2026. Project DOI: [10.5281/zenodo.24422717](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24422717).*

**Certainty:** Medium to high. Laboratory syntheses reproduce the ancient phase assemblage and microstructure, and the main steps are attested by Vitruvius and by crucibles and pellets, but firing temperature, duration, atmosphere and batch recipe of any given workshop are still inferred · **What is lost:** the batch proportions and raw-material choices of individual workshops, the firing schedule (temperature, time, atmosphere, cooling), the organisation of the second firing, and the reasons production ceased.

## Abstract

Egyptian blue, whose chromophore is calcium copper tetrasilicate (CaCuSi₄O₁₀, the synthetic equivalent of cuprorivaite), is the oldest known human-made pigment, attested from about 3100 BCE (Kovalev et al. 2023). The material itself is treated in entry M-03; this article reviews what is known of the process. Only one ancient text, Vitruvius 7.11.1, describes manufacture, and it omits the lime that the chemistry requires. Theophrastus (*De lapidibus* 55) and Pliny (*NH* 33.161–162, 37.119) add that it was manufactured, in grades, and at Puteoli. Laboratory work since the 1980s has established that quartz, a calcium source, a copper source and a small amount of alkali react at roughly 850–1000 °C, that higher temperatures destroy the blue phase, and that a second firing changes texture and hue. Archaeology has identified primary production through crucibles at Amarna, Qantir, Memphis, Kos and the Bay of Naples. Provenance studies point to plant-ash fluxes and, for Roman material, to Campanian sand and sulphidic copper ore. What remains unknown is workshop-level practice: proportions, firing time and atmosphere, and why the industry disappeared.

## 1. Introduction

Egyptian blue is a heterogeneous, multi-phase material: the blue crystals of CaCuSi₄O₁₀ are intergrown with silicate glass, unreacted silica polymorphs and, depending on the batch, wollastonite, tenorite or other copper phases (McCloy et al. 2025). Its production spread from Egypt and Mesopotamia across the Mediterranean; use declined sharply in late antiquity, though isolated occurrences continue into the sixteenth century, the latest cited being Raphael's *Triumph of Galatea* (1511–1512) and a painting by Giovanni Battista Benvenuto of 1524 (Kovalev et al. 2023). Modern interest has been renewed by its near-infrared luminescence and its exfoliation into nanosheets (Accorsi et al. 2009; Johnson-McDaniel et al. 2013; Nicola et al. 2023); those properties are covered under M-03.

The process is a solid-state and partly melt-assisted reaction run in ceramic vessels at kiln temperatures (Kovalev et al. 2023). Because the starting materials are common and widely available, the questions that remain open are less "how is it possible?" than "how was it actually done, where, and with what?" — questions about specific recipes, kiln practice and the organisation of production (Kovalev et al. 2023).

Notes on the sources. (1) Vitruvius names sand, natron and copper but not lime, although calcium is an essential constituent of CaCuSi₄O₁₀ (McCloy et al. 2025). (2) Pliny 33.161–162 is cited by Kovalev et al. (2023) as "NH 33.57", i.e. by chapter; the section numbers used here are those of the LacusCurtius text. (3) McCloy et al. (2025) state that Theophrastus "indicates that there were four possible colors" of Egyptian blue; in the Greek of *De lapidibus* 55, as read here, the four colours are attributed to the Scythian κύανος, and Pliny 33.161 says the same. Whether "Scythian κύανος" was a natural mineral or a manufactured product is not settled by either text. (4) It is not established that κύανος, *caeruleum* and *cyanos* always denote Egyptian blue: Theophrastus explicitly distinguishes a native κύανος, and Pliny 37.119 treats *cyanos* as a gemstone. (5) Mesopotamian cuneiform glass-making texts were not consulted for this article; whether they contain recipes relevant to Egyptian blue was not checked.

## 3. Reconstructed process

The stages below follow the *chaîne opératoire* proposed by Kovalev et al. (2023), with other evidence added where cited.

**3.1 Raw materials.** Four components are required: silica, calcium, copper and an alkali flux (McCloy et al. 2025).

- *Silica*: crushed quartz pebbles or clean sand (Kovalev et al. 2023). For Roman pellets from Aventicum and Augusta Raurica, and for a fifth/sixth-century wall painting at St. Peter above Gratsch (South Tyrol), Raman-detected accessory minerals match quartz sand from the northern Phlegraean Fields / Volturno river sediments, in Campania (Dariz & Schmid 2021, 2022). At Cumae, both blue and green frits were made from a quartz–feldspar–calcite-rich sand (Grifa et al. 2016).
- *Calcium*: either added separately (crushed limestone or shell) or brought in with lime-rich sand (Kovalev et al. 2023). The source is debated: some authors favour a calcareous sand, others a lime-bearing natron (McCloy et al. 2025). Skovmøller et al. (2016) describe the calcium compound as "powdered limestone and sand rich in calcium carbonate". The README statement that the ancient lime "probably came as an impurity in the sand" is therefore one of two hypotheses in the literature, not a consensus.
- *Copper*: metal, alloy, ore (malachite or azurite) or copper oxide scale from roasted scrap (Kovalev et al. 2023). Tin appears in Egyptian blue only from the 18th Dynasty onwards, which is read as a shift to bronze as copper source (Jaksch et al. 1983, as summarised by Kovalev et al. 2023); Roman pellets show higher lead, possibly from lead-rich bronze (Kovalev et al. 2023). Vitruvius's filed copper (*aes cyprium limis crassis uti scobe facta*, 7.11.1) fits a metallic source. For Roman and early medieval Campanian-type material, chalcocite and chalcopyrite relics instead point to a sulphidic ore (Dariz & Schmid 2021, 2022).
- *Alkali flux*: mineral natron (M-10) or halophytic plant ash (Kovalev et al. 2023). Vitruvius names *flos nitri*. Variable K₂O in the glass phase of archaeological cakes suggests plant ash (Kovalev et al. 2023), and McCloy et al. (2025), citing Hatton et al. (2008), state that Egyptian material appears to use plant ash although some contemporary green frits suggest natron. Water-insoluble salts in the Campanian-type material are interpreted as a mixed-alkaline plant ash (Dariz & Schmid 2021, 2022). The README's description of the flux simply as "natron" is thus too narrow.

**3.2 Grinding, mixing, shaping.** Vitruvius requires grinding "like flour" and binding the moist mixture into hand-rolled balls (7.11.1). Laboratory and analytical studies summarised by Kovalev et al. (2023) show that proportions matter: excess copper gives tenorite and cuprite, excess quartz gives free silica, excess quartz and lime give wollastonite; with about 0.3 % alkali crystal growth is limited, and with about 10 % a green glassy mass forms. Most archaeological cakes and pellets carry a marked excess of SiO₂ over the stoichiometric composition (Kovalev et al. 2023). Late Bronze Age workshops made large round or rectangular cakes (up to about 15–25 cm), Iron Age examples are smaller blocks, and Hellenistic–Roman workshops made pellets of about 1–4 cm, as Vitruvius describes; the reason for the change is unknown (Kovalev et al. 2023).

**3.3 First firing.** Laboratory reproductions place cuprorivaite formation at roughly 850–1000 °C under moderately reducing conditions, depending on the mix; at about 1050 °C the blue phase was found to transform completely into green glass (Bianchetti et al. 2000, as summarised by Kovalev et al. 2023). The range of temperatures hypothesised in the literature for ancient practice is wider, 742–1100 °C, with 850–950 °C the most commonly assumed (McCloy et al. 2025). From newly formed calcium silicates and feldspathoids in Roman crucibles at Cumae, Grifa et al. (2016) estimated 950–1050 °C for the open crucibles used for blue and over 1100 °C for the closed ones used for green frit. The duration of ancient firings is unknown (Kovalev et al. 2023). The README's figure of "800–900 °C" for modern synthesis is therefore lower than the range given in the sources reviewed here.

Firing took place in ceramic vessels, as Vitruvius says (*urceus fictilis*). Crucible fabrics vary: a porous, lime-rich fabric at Late Bronze Age Qantir; Nile silt with a lime-rich internal parting layer at Hellenistic–Roman Memphis; CaO-rich clay with volcanic temper and no parting layer at Cumae (Kovalev et al. 2023; Grifa et al. 2016). Kovalev et al. (2023) note that a lime-rich fabric is the common feature and that its rationale is unclear.

**3.4 Second firing and finishing.** Tite et al. (1984), comparing ancient samples with laboratory products, concluded that a two-stage firing, with grinding and moulding between the firings, was probably used for small objects. A second firing of the ground product gives a lighter, finer-textured blue (Kovalev et al. 2023). Excess silica in Late Bronze Age Mesopotamian samples has been read as dilution with quartz before re-firing to adjust colour (Hatton et al. 2008, as summarised by Kovalev et al. 2023). The Kos workshop shows the same two phases: first the pellets, then further processing to obtain different tonalities (Kostomitsopoulou Marketou 2022). Pliny's *lomentum* (washing and grinding, 33.162) and Theophrastus's grades by particle size (55) describe a post-firing separation that fits the finding that particle size alone changes measured colour (McCloy et al. 2025).

**3.5 Related materials and processes.** Egyptian green frit was made from the same raw materials in different proportions, with more alkali and higher temperatures (Pagès-Camagna & Colinart 2003; Grifa et al. 2016). The same materials and temperature range link Egyptian blue to faience and glass production (P-03), and Amarna and Qantir show glass, faience and Egyptian blue made in closely associated workshops (Kovalev et al. 2023). Berke (2007) considers the Egyptian and Chinese copper-silicate pigments (Han blue, Han purple) to have been developed independently, probably out of glazing techniques.

## 4. Analytical and experimental evidence

**Early technological studies.** Jaksch et al. (1983) treated cuprorivaite as "a window to ancient Egyptian technology"; their abstract could not be accessed for this review, and the points attributed to them above are taken from Kovalev et al. (2023). Tite et al. (1984) analysed Egyptian, Mesopotamian and Roman samples by atomic absorption and scanning electron microscopy, synthesised Egyptian blue over a range of compositions and firing procedures, and compared the two (abstract).

**Egypt and Mesopotamia, second millennium BC.** Hatton et al. (2008) studied the production technology of blue and green frits from Egypt and Mesopotamia; only metadata and citations by others were accessible. Kovalev et al. (2023) report from it that the glass phase of archaeological material is often weathered, that coarse quartz (>100 µm) and less homogeneous mixing characterise the Late Bronze Age, and that high silica distinguishes Mesopotamian samples, although similarly quartz-rich cakes exist in Egypt. Pagès-Camagna & Colinart (2003) analysed raw blue and green cakes and fifty pigment samples from paintings in the Louvre, made modern replicas, and examined the role of firing temperature, kiln atmosphere and cooling rate; they demonstrated that Egyptian green is a distinct product and proposed manufacturing processes for both (abstract). Seymour et al. (2020) identified two possible production groups in New Kingdom shabtis from Deir el-Medina and, with Raman imaging, distinguished luminescing and non-luminescing silicate phases that may relate to production technique (abstract).

**Hellenistic and Roman production.** At Kos, a first-century BCE workshop yielded 138 pellets graded from successful deep blue to unsuccessful grey, green and brown, together with litharge rods and earth pigments (Kovalev et al. 2023; Kostomitsopoulou Marketou 2022). In the Bay of Naples, crucible fragments with blue and green crusts are known at Cumae and Liternum, while the evidence at Puteoli itself is limited to late nineteenth-century records of sherds with blue and green incrustation (Kovalev et al. 2023). Grifa et al. (2016) linked open crucibles at Cumae to blue and closed ones to green frit and proposed widening the Vitruvian production area to the northern Phlegraean Fields, including Cumae and Liternum (abstract). Dariz & Schmid (2022) argued that Roman pellets from Switzerland support a monopolised production site in the northern Phlegraean Fields during the first centuries AD; they also detected disordered cuprorivaite and a copper-bearing green glass phase, the latter probably dependent on the alkali concentration, while solid-state reactions dominate (abstract). Dariz & Schmid (2021) similarly concluded, for an early medieval sample, that melting into glass played a negligible role in the synthesis (abstract).

**Recent laboratory reconstruction.** McCloy et al. (2025) produced twelve recipes from quartz, CuO, azurite or malachite, CaCO₃ or CaO, with or without Na₂CO₃, all fired at 1000 °C for 1–11 hours, and compared them with modern pigments, copper-doped glasses and two Egyptian artefacts (read in full). They found that as little as about 50 wt % cuprorivaite gives a deep blue, that particle size measurably changes colour, and that a copper-doped glass phase, formed only when soda is present, adds a green component. They deliberately chose a temperature above the commonly assumed ancient range, so their recipes are colour references rather than a reconstruction of a particular ancient kiln.

**Overall assessment.** The reaction, its temperature window and its sensitivity to batch composition are well established by repeated syntheses that match ancient microstructures (Tite et al. 1984; Pagès-Camagna & Colinart 2003; McCloy et al. 2025). What the analyses cannot yet do is recover a complete workshop recipe, because the glass phase that carries the flux signature is commonly weathered (Kovalev et al. 2023).

## 5. Discussion: what remains lost

1. *Lime.* No ancient text names a calcium source; whether it was added deliberately, came in with the sand, or arrived with the natron is unresolved (McCloy et al. 2025; Kovalev et al. 2023).
2. *Firing schedule.* Proposed ancient temperatures span 742–1100 °C (McCloy et al. 2025). Durations are unknown, kiln atmosphere is inferred from laboratory work, and cooling regimes are known only from experiments (Kovalev et al. 2023; Pagès-Camagna & Colinart 2003).
3. *Kilns.* No Egyptian blue workshop in the Bay of Naples has been located, and no kilns are known at Cumae or Liternum; Kovalev et al. (2023) also stress the lack of direct production evidence for much of the period of use.
4. *Second firing.* Its temperature and purpose (colour adjustment, moulding of objects, or both) are known only in outline (Tite et al. 1984; Kovalev et al. 2023).
5. *Disappearance.* Whether the post-Roman decline reflects lost knowledge, a change of pigments, or the reuse of old stock is an open question; medieval occurrences could be reused Roman material or continued production with the same raw materials (Kovalev et al. 2023; Dariz & Schmid 2021).
6. *The texts themselves.* Vitruvius's "sweats" do not describe the chemistry, and the identity of Theophrastus's and Pliny's Scythian and Cyprian kinds is not established (section 2).

## 6. Open questions and proposed research

- Run replications of Vitruvius 7.11.1 with filed bronze, natron or plant ash, and sands of known calcium content (including Volturno/Phlegraean sands), varying temperature and time inside the 850–1050 °C window. Compare the products with the Kos, Cumae and Memphis material for phase assemblage, unreacted-quartz size and glass composition. This would test both the calcareous-sand and lime-bearing-natron hypotheses (McCloy et al. 2025; Dariz & Schmid 2022).
- Use crucible-fabric reactions as thermometers, as Grifa et al. (2016) did, at Memphis, Kos and Qantir, to obtain workshop-specific temperatures.
- Systematically describe the external features of cakes and pellets, which Kovalev et al. (2023) report has not yet been done.
- Search for flux signatures that survive weathering of the glass phase, since the glass is where the alkali record lies (Kovalev et al. 2023).
- Re-read Theophrastus 55 and Pliny 33.161 and 37.119 on Scythian κύανος alongside analyses of natural blue copper minerals, to decide whether the "four colours" refer to a natural mineral.
- Combine experimental grading by washing (Pliny's *lomentum*) with quantitative colour measurement, building on McCloy et al. (2025).

# P-06. Garum and fish sauces: state of the art

*The Apolothecary — state-of-the-art review. Version 1.0, 22 November 2026. Project DOI: [10.5281/zenodo.24422717](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24422717).*

**Certainty:** Medium — the main steps (salting of whole small fish or viscera, maceration with stirring, straining into a liquid and a residue) are stated in the texts and fit the archaeological residues, but the salt ratio, duration, temperature and additives used in a given workshop are still inferred · **What is lost:** the working parameters of each product (salt-to-fish ratio, time, temperature, additives), the exact distinction between *garum*, *liquamen*, *muria* and *allec* in a given period and place, and how the brine fraction (*muria*) was drawn off.

## Abstract

Salted and fermented fish sauces (Greek γάρος, Latin *garum*, *liquamen*, with the residue *allec* and the brine *muria*) were a staple condiment of the Roman world. Their names and rough method are known from Pliny the Elder, from a tenth-century Byzantine compilation (the *Geoponika*), from a recipe transmitted under the name of Gargilius Martialis, from the Apicius collection and from painted amphora labels. This review sets out what those texts say, checked against online editions, and what laboratory work has added since 2010. Residues in the dolia of the "Garum Shop" at Pompeii (I.12.8) have been analysed for amino acids, minerals and fatty acids and compared with a laboratory reconstruction made by the universities of Cádiz and Seville; archaeozoology has identified anchovy in the dolia and picarel in an amphora; ancient DNA from a third-century AD vat at Adro Vello (Galicia) has confirmed sardine. These studies agree with the outline given by the texts but do not fix its parameters. The process is therefore rated Medium on the Apolothecary scale. The review closes with a list of tests that could narrow the remaining unknowns.

## 1. Introduction

Fish sauce is the best documented of the Roman food processes. Pliny the Elder already calls it a "liquid of an exquisite kind" (*liquoris exquisiti genus*) and says that, apart from perfumes, hardly any liquid fetched a higher price (Pliny, *NH* 31.93–94; text checked, see section 2). Modern scholarship on the subject is large: García Vargas et al. (2014: 66) call the literature on Roman salted fish and fish sauces "immense" and cite Curtis (1991) as a standard reference. What remains debated is less the principle than the practice. García Vargas et al. (2014: 66) note that the texts are too terse to say how each named product was made, and that this makes the degraded residues hard to match to a product. Rodríguez-Alcántara et al. (2018: 149) state in their abstract that "its production process is still unknown, and few remains have been found in the whole ancient world".

The biochemical principle is not in dispute among the authors read here. García Vargas et al. (2014: 67–68) describe fish sauce as salted fish that liquefies by enzymatic autolysis, and stress that the digestive tract of the fish must be in the mix for hydrolysis to take place; with large fish (tuna, bonito, mackerel) the viscera, blood and gills were used, with small fish (anchovy, sardine) the whole animal. Espregueira Themudo et al. (2025: 1050) likewise describe the sauces as made by crushing whole fish and fermenting them in brine.

This article covers the four products named in the entry title. It does not cover solid salted fish (*salsamenta*) except where the texts treat them together.

## 3. Reconstructed process

### 3.1 Raw materials

- **Fish.** The texts name small whole fish and the viscera of larger fish. *Geoponika* 20.46.1 names fish entrails together with small fish, "especially *atherinai*, or small *triglia*, or *mainidia*, or *lykostomoi*, or whatever seems small"; the Bithynian recipe (20.46.3) adds *sauroi* and *skombroi*. Pliny (*NH* 31.93–95) names intestines and discards, the *scomber*, *apua* and the *lupus* of Forum Iulii. Gargilius Martialis (as transcribed by García Vargas et al. 2014: 81) names fatty fish such as salmon, eel, shad, sardine and herring. Modern species identifications of these Greek and Latin names were not checked for this review and are not given.
- **Salt.** Always present. Quantities survive only in *Geoponika* 20.46.3 (two Italian *xestai* of salt to a *modius* of fish) and in the layer thickness of Gargilius (salt "two fingers deep"). No other Apolothecary entry covers salt.
- **Herbs, wine, honey, boiled must.** Gargilius layers dried herbs with the fish and later boils the liquid with wine, herbs and honey; *Geoponika* 20.46.4–5 mentions old wine, oregano and boiled must (ἕψημα) as options.
- **Possible mineral additives.** Proposed from analysis, not from texts: lime and iron compounds (section 4). Quicklime as a building material is covered in P-01.

### 3.2 Steps attested in the texts

1. **Salting and layering.** Fish or viscera are put into a vessel and salted (*Geoponika* 20.46.1); in Gargilius, layers of herbs, fish and salt are repeated to the top of a pitched vessel. In the Bithynian recipe the fish are first mixed with the salt in a baker's kneading trough and left overnight (20.46.3).
2. **Maceration with stirring.** In the sun, "stirred often" (πυκνῶς δονούμενα, 20.46.1); in the Bithynian recipe, in an uncovered jar in the sun "for two or three months", stirred with a stick at intervals (20.46.3). Gargilius closes the vessel for seven days, then stirs two or three times a day for twenty days.
3. **Separation.** A long, close-woven basket is pushed into the full vessel; the sauce flows into it and the strained liquid is drawn off as *liquamen*; "the rest becomes *alix*" (τὸ δὲ λοιπὸν πάτημα γίνεται ἅλιξ, 20.46.2). Pliny's definition of *allex* as "imperfect and unstrained dregs" (*NH* 31.95) fits this step. García Vargas et al. (2014: 68) add that perforated conical vessels thought to be garum strainers have been identified archaeologically, citing Bernal & Sáez (2006); that study was not read for this review.
4. **Quick method by boiling.** For immediate use, the *Geoponika* (20.46.5) boils the fish in a brine strong enough that "an egg put in it floats" (ὡς ὠὸν ἐμβληθὲν ἐπιπλεῖν; if it sinks, there is not yet enough salt), with oregano, until it begins to reduce, then strains it two or three times until clear. Gargilius' second stage, boiling the liquid with wine, herbs and honey to half its volume, is also a cooked finish. García Vargas et al. (2014: 79) read this cooking as a faster replacement for months of exposure to the sun.
5. ***Haimation*.** The "better *garos*" is made from tuna entrails with the gills, serum and blood, salted, left in the vessel "for two months at most"; the vessel is then pierced and the sauce called *haimation* runs out (20.46.6). Martial's *garum sociorum* "from the first blood of a still-breathing mackerel" (13.102) is a poetic reference to a blood-based sauce.

### 3.3 The four product names

The texts read here support the following working definitions, which remain debated (García Vargas et al. 2014: 66):

- ***garum* / *liquamen***: the strained liquid. Pliny uses *garum*; the *Geoponika* gives the strained product the Latin loanword λικουάμεν; Apicius uses *liquamen* throughout. García Vargas et al. (2014: 68) argue that *liquamen* first named the sauce of whole small fish and that from the third century AD the two words were used as synonyms; this chronology is theirs.
- ***allec* (*allex*, *hallec*, *hallex*)**: the unstrained residue (Pliny, *NH* 31.95; *Geoponika* 20.46.2), but also, according to Pliny, a product made on purpose from tiny fish.
- ***muria***: a brine. Columella 12.55.4 uses it for the brine that forms around salted meat. García Vargas et al. (2014: 68, 79) identify it as the brine that rises above the layered fish in the first days and propose that it was drawn off as a cheap third product; they saw it form in their experiment but did not separate it. Martial 13.103 ties a *muria* to tuna from Antipolis.

### 3.4 Modern reconstructions

García Vargas et al. (2014: 71–72) followed Gargilius Martialis in a thermostated, water-jacketed fermenter. They built layers of spices, 500–700 g of fish and 250–300 g of salt, up to a total of 3.5 kg of fish and 1.5 kg of salt, kept them still for a week, then stirred for twenty days, and filtered through linen, first by gravity and then by pressing. They obtained a liquid *liquamen* and a paste that they identify with *hallec*; the bones in the paste were reduced to very small rounded fragments by weeks of stirring, which they propose as a way of recognising *hallec* in archaeological residues (García Vargas et al. 2014: 72–73). They did not perform Gargilius' second, cooked stage (García Vargas et al. 2014: 79).

Rodríguez-Alcántara et al. (2018: 152) describe the reference batches used for their later comparison as 80% anchovy, 15% salt and 5% Mediterranean spices, held at 50 ± 5 °C, sealed in the dark for a week to simulate an amphora in the sun, then stirred five minutes a day for seven more days. These proportions and this schedule differ from the 2014 description (about 70:30 fish to salt, twenty days of stirring). The 2018 paper cites the 2014 one for its procedure, so the difference is unexplained in the papers read.

## 4. Analytical and experimental evidence

### 4.1 The Garum Shop at Pompeii (I.12.8)

The building was found in 1960 by Amedeo Maiuri and yielded about a hundred Dressel 21–22 amphorae with *tituli picti* and visible contents; an Italian–Spanish team has studied its archives, stratigraphy, fauna and residues to model how space was organised for producing and selling fish products in 79 CE (Bernal-Casasola & Cottica 2026, abstract only). The fish remains in the dolia, sampled from a depth of 10 cm in six vessels, were found without lapilli or ash (Rodríguez-Alcántara et al. 2018: 152).

- **Species.** Earlier zoological study of the dolia identified anchovy (*Engraulis encrasicolus*) and pollen of culinary plants (Rodríguez-Alcántara et al. 2018: 152–153, citing Curtis 1979 and Bernal et al. 2013, neither read here). García Vargas et al. (2014: 74–75) report anchovy vertebrae in all dolia except dolium 5, of very similar size, and a few other minor species. Carannante (2019, abstract) found that the remains in an amphora from the shop were picarel (*Spicara smaris*), left to macerate whole, neither beheaded nor filleted, and so little disturbed that the product was probably still macerating when the eruption occurred.
- **Amino acids and minerals.** Smriga et al. (2010) analysed remnants from the shop; the paper was not read here (abstract not available). According to the summary in Rodríguez-Alcántara et al. (2018: 152), they found free amino acids, especially glutamate, at very low levels in two vessels, and sodium levels far below those of modern fish sauces, perhaps because rain washed the contents after excavation.
- **Bone fragmentation.** García Vargas et al. (2014: 79–80) graded the degree of bone breakage and proposed that dolia 1 and 2 held salted fish not yet processed, dolia 3, 4 and 6 a *hallec*-type product, and dolium 5 probably a filtered liquid. They give both a production reading and a shop-sale reading of this pattern.
- **Fatty acids and minerals.** Rodríguez-Alcántara et al. (2018) measured nitrogen, fat, ash, fatty acids and minerals in the six dolia and in their reconstructed *allec*. Differences in nitrogen and fat suggest that different batches were in progress at the eruption; the fatty-acid profiles of the dolia and the reference were similar (abstract, p. 149). In their conclusions they assign dolia 1, 2, 3 and 6 to production and dolia 4 and 5 to storage of finished product (p. 161). The calcium of dolium 5 is too high to come from fish bone alone and is not fully explained by volcanic ash; they connect it with the use of lime, and note an amphora full of lime found near the production area (p. 159). The iron content of all the dolia (3.8–7.42 mg/g) is far above that of fresh anchovy and of the reference *allec*; they suggest a deliberate iron-oxide colourant, possibly to imitate *haimation*, while allowing that fish blood or viscera could also be the source (p. 159). The English and Spanish abstracts of this paper differ on the calcium additive: the English names lime, the Spanish *yeso* (gypsum); the body text discusses lime (CaO).
- **Floors and vessels.** Pecci et al. (2018) combined phosphate, fatty-acid and protein-residue tests on the floors of the shop with gas-chromatographic analysis of organic residues in its vessels. As reported in the abstract, the results point to garum production, cooking and reuse of amphorae and dolia before the eruption (abstract only).

### 4.2 Other contexts

- **Adro Vello (O Grove, Galicia).** Espregueira Themudo et al. (2025) extracted DNA from crushed vertebrae at the bottom of vat 1 of a Roman fish-salting plant, one of four large vats at the site (p. 1051). The fish remains have a radiocarbon range of cal AD 84–394 at 95.4% (cal AD 162–321 at 68.2%), and the stratigraphy places the abandonment of the plant in the early third century AD (pp. 1055–1056). The DNA confirms European sardine (*Sardina pilchardus*), and population analyses link the ancient fish with modern Western and Central groups of modern sardine genomes (abstract; p. 1056). The authors stress that usable DNA survived the acidic, enzyme-rich fermentation environment, and claim that this is the first DNA sequenced from the bottom of Roman fish-salting vats (p. 1061). Read in full (open access).
- **Comparative Atlantic and Baetican evidence.** The same paper summarises archaeozoological results from other plants: sardine at Douarnenez and La Falaise in Brittany (99.6% of the remains in vat 1 at La Falaise), more than 98% sardine in sixteen vats at Belém (Lisbon), and at Baelo Claudia some *cetariae* dominated by anchovy and others by sardine (95%), with sprat and axillary seabream also used (Espregueira Themudo et al. 2025: 1059–1061, citing works not read here).
- ***Haimation*.** Van Neer & Parker (2008) published what they call the first archaeozoological evidence for *haimation*. The paper was not read here. García Vargas et al. (2014: 74) summarise the criterion as follows: specific bones of the gill apparatus (pharyngobranchials) would mark a *garum* made from gills, blood and serum.
- **Ses Fontanelles wreck (Mallorca).** A multi-analytical study of this Late Roman wreck combined amphora petrography, archaeozoology and residue analysis, and concluded that the ship probably left the Cartagena area carrying fish sauce (*liquaminis flos*), oil and wine (Cau-Ontiveros et al. 2024, abstract).

### 4.3 Experimental reproduction

The 2014 reconstruction (section 3.4) produced a brown-ochre, oily, salt-saturated liquid with a mild smell of fish and spices, and a pasty, less salty *hallec* (García Vargas et al. 2014: 72). The authors state that the physical and food characteristics of their products match those described in the ancient sources and those of the organic residues analysed (abstract). Grainger (2021) is a monograph on fermented fish sauce and salted fish with a chapter titled "Modern fish sauce experiments" (pp. 146–170, from Crossref chapter metadata); its content was not read for this review.

## 5. Discussion: what remains lost

The texts give a recognisable process: salt, small fish or viscera, time in the sun with stirring, straining into a liquid and a residue, and a cooked shortcut. The archaeology fits this picture. The dolia contain fish in several stages of breakdown (García Vargas et al. 2014; Rodríguez-Alcántara et al. 2018), the species are the small fish the texts lead one to expect (anchovy, picarel, sardine), and the Adro Vello bones were crushed whole fish (Carannante 2019; Espregueira Themudo et al. 2025). This is why the process is rated Medium rather than Low.

It is not rated High, for four reasons.

1. **Parameters.** The only salt quantity in the texts, two Italian *xestai* of salt per *modius* of fish (*Geoponika* 20.46.3), has not been turned into a mass ratio in any study read here. Its conversion depends on the metrological values chosen and on the bulk density of salt and fish, which were not checked for this review. The two published laboratory schedules are not consistent with each other (section 3.4), and neither is presented as the Pompeian recipe.
2. **Product boundaries.** The laboratory has produced a *liquamen* and a *hallec*, but not a *muria* drawn off separately (García Vargas et al. 2014: 79), nor Gargilius' cooked final product. Whether *garum* and *liquamen* differed in the first century AD rests on philological argument (García Vargas et al. 2014: 68).
3. **Additives.** Lime and iron colourants are hypotheses built on mineral data that can also be explained by volcanic contamination, fish blood or post-excavation weathering (Rodríguez-Alcántara et al. 2018: 159; Smriga et al. 2010 as summarised there). No ancient text read for this review mentions either.
4. **Regional variation.** The species differ by region (anchovy and picarel at Pompeii, sardine on the Atlantic coast, mixed in Baetica), and the texts themselves give several recipes. A single reconstructed "Roman garum" is therefore probably the wrong target.

## 6. Open questions and proposed research

- **Metrology of the Bithynian recipe.** Convert *Geoponika* 20.46.3 into a mass ratio with an explicit choice of metrological values, and test it alongside the 2014 and 2018 schedules on the same fish.
- **Isolate *muria*.** Draw off the supernatant brine during the static phase, as García Vargas et al. (2014: 72) themselves propose, and characterise it against residues from vessels labelled *muria*.
- **Cooked versus sun-matured product.** Reproduce the second stage of Gargilius Martialis and the boiled method of *Geoponika* 20.46.5, and test whether heating leaves a chemical or bone-surface signature that could separate the two routes in archaeological residues.
- **Test the additive hypotheses.** Measure calcium, iron and their isotopic or mineralogical form in residues, set against local volcanic ash and fish-blood controls, to separate added lime or iron oxide from contamination.
- **DNA beyond the Atlantic.** Apply the Adro Vello protocol (Espregueira Themudo et al. 2025) to the Pompeii dolia and amphorae and to Baetican vats, both to confirm the species and to look for gill or blood tissue that would point to *haimation*.
- **Epigraphy.** Build a verified corpus of fish-sauce *tituli picti* (product names, qualities such as *flos*, producers) and match it against residue analyses of the same vessels.

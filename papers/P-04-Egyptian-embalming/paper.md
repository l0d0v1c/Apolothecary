# P-04. Egyptian embalming: state of the art

*The Apolothecary — state-of-the-art review. Version 1.0, 22 November 2026. Project DOI: [10.5281/zenodo.24422717](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24422717).*

**Certainty:** Medium — the main operations are described by Greek authors and the ingredients of many balms are now identified chemically, but no surviving text gives a complete recipe, and quantities, heating schedules and the order of application are still inferred · **What is lost:** full recipes and proportions; how the balms were prepared and heated; the sequence and timing of applications; how practice varied by period, place and rank.

## Abstract

Egyptian embalming combined desiccation of the body with the application of organic preparations to tissues and wrappings. Greek authors (Herodotus 2.86–88; Diodorus 1.91) describe evisceration, treatment with *nitron* (natron, M-10), "cedar" oil, myrrh, cassia and gum, but they wrote late and are vague about the substances. Egyptian texts name products such as *antiu* and *sefet* without saying what they were. Since about 2000, gas chromatography–mass spectrometry and related methods have changed the picture. Balms combining plant oils or animal fats, conifer resin, aromatic plant extracts and plant gums are present on wrappings from the fifth and fourth millennia BC. Later balms add beeswax, *Pistacia* resin and, from the New Kingdom onward, Dead Sea bitumen. The labelled vessels of a 26th-Dynasty workshop at Saqqara link Egyptian names to chemical contents for the first time: there, *antiu* was a mixture of cedar and juniper/cypress oils or tars with animal fat, not myrrh. This review sets out what the texts actually say, what the analyses have shown, and which parameters of the process remain unknown.

## 1. Introduction

Ancient Egyptian embalming is a process whose products survive in great numbers but whose recipes do not. Buckley and Evershed (2001, abstract) summarised the problem: natron is recognised as the central desiccant, but without organic preservatives the bodies would have decomposed in humid tombs, and the nature of those organic treatments remained obscure because the Egyptians left no written record of the process. The ancient accounts that do survive are secondary and Greek. Huber et al. (2023) note that the few Egyptian written sources, such as the Ritual of Embalming, do not give the exact ingredients of the balms. They add that the later Greek and Roman descriptions were not necessarily valid for practices more than a millennium earlier.

The process therefore belongs to the "Medium" class of the Apolothecary process scale. Its main steps are known, from texts and from the bodies themselves, but its key parameters are inferred. The materials involved overlap with several other entries: natron (M-10); possibly balsam-type exudates (O-03), although no analysis cited here identifies Judean balsam as such; the date palm (O-02), whose wine Herodotus names for rinsing; cinnamon and cassia, listed among the plant terms still to be investigated (O-05); and beeswax, also central to encaustic (P-05).

This review covers human embalming mainly, with shorter notes on animal and food mummies where the analyses bear on technique. It does not treat the anthropology or religion of mummification except where a text links a substance to an operation.

The Greek sources should be read with caution. Buckley and Evershed (2001, abstract) note that by the time of Herodotus (about 450 BC) the process had already declined, and the best results had been achieved centuries before. They also note that Herodotus is vague about the natural products used, and that his "cedar oil" is still widely disputed. Pliny's *cedrium* is a first distillate obtained while making pitch from *taeda* wood, not necessarily a product of the tree now called cedar (*Cedrus*). This suggests, though it is only an inference from the passage, that the Greek and Latin "cedar" terms may have covered more than one coniferous product.

## 3. Reconstructed process

The steps below combine the Greek accounts with archaeological and analytical evidence. Each step is marked as attested in texts, attested in the material, or inferred.

1. **Choice of grade and price.** Texts: Herodotus 2.86 and Diodorus 1.91.2–3 both describe three grades at different prices. Material: Evershed and Clark (2021, abstract) found that the balms of children and women had fewer ingredients than those of adult men, which fits a graded practice, although they do not interpret it in those terms in the abstract.
2. **Excerebration.** Texts: Herodotus 2.86.3 (iron hook through the nostrils, then drugs poured in). Material: Rageot et al. (2023) list excerebration among the established pharaonic procedures, and eight Saqqara vessels carry instructions for treating the head (see step 6).
3. **Incision and evisceration.** Texts: Herodotus 2.86.4; Diodorus 1.91.4–5 (left flank; heart and kidneys left in place). Herodotus 2.87 also describes a cheaper method that dissolves the viscera with an injected oil instead of cutting. Material: the Saqqara complex includes a subterranean evisceration facility, the *wabet* (Rageot et al. 2023). Organs could be embalmed and kept separately: Senetnay's lungs and liver were stored in canopic jars (Huber et al. 2023).
4. **Washing.** Texts: palm wine and aromatics (Herodotus 2.86.4; Diodorus 1.91.5). Material: a Saqqara bowl labelled "to wash" contained markers of conifer oil or tar (Rageot et al. 2023).
5. **Desiccation with natron (M-10).** Texts: Herodotus 2.86.5, 2.87, 2.88 (seventy days in natron). Diodorus 1.91 does not mention natron, and gives "over thirty days" for the treatment with oils and spices. Literature: Buckley and Evershed (2001) treat natron as the recognised desiccant. Jones et al. (2014) note, citing earlier work, that natron desiccation and evisceration are usually dated from about 2500 BC, after a long phase of treating wrappings with resinous preparations. Rageot et al. (2023) state that the whole process could take up to 70 days.
6. **Treatment of head, body and organs with balms.** Material: at Saqqara, three different mixtures were used on the head, with ingredients drawn from elemi, *Pistacia* resin, juniper/cypress and cedar oils or tars, animal fat, beeswax, probably castor oil, and an olive-type plant oil. A vessel naming Imseti, protector of the liver, held juniper/cypress oil or tar with elemi. One naming Duamutef, protector of the stomach, held only heated beeswax (Rageot et al. 2023). A vessel for treating the skin, possibly on the third day of embalming, held ruminant fat with heated beeswax. A bowl labelled "to make his odour pleasant" held ruminant fat with degraded Burseraceae resin (Rageot et al. 2023).
7. **Preparation of balms (heating and mixing).** Inferred from markers in the residues: heat-treatment markers for *Pistacia* resin and beeswax, and triterpenic palmitates showing that elemi was heated with fat or oil and dammar with beeswax and/or fat (Rageot et al. 2023). Conifer oils or tars imply distillation or pyrolysis (Rageot et al. 2023). Pliny's account (NH 16.52) of *cedrium* as the first liquid from pitch-making describes one such pyrolytic route.
8. **Wrapping.** Texts: byssus linen smeared with gum (Herodotus 2.86.6). Material: at Saqqara, eight vessels labelled for "wrapping or embalming with it" contained mainly a mixture (mixture E) of juniper/cypress and cedar oils or tars, fat and/or plant oil, and elemi (Rageot et al. 2023).
9. **Burial and later anointing.** Material: black coatings and poured ritual liquids on coffins of about 943–716 BC contain the same classes of substance as balms (Dead Sea bitumen, conifer resin, *Pistacia* resin), which suggests a link between embalming, coffin decoration and funeral rites (Fulcher et al. 2021).

## 4. Analytical and experimental evidence

### 4.1 Prehistoric origins

Jones et al. (2014) analysed directly radiocarbon-dated linen wrappings, skin and reed matting from Badarian and Predynastic tombs at Mostagedda (c. 4500–3350 BC). Using GC–MS and thermal desorption/pyrolysis GC–MS, they identified pine resin, an aromatic plant extract, a plant gum or sugar, a natural petroleum source and a plant oil or animal fat. In these recipes, a fat or oil "base" makes up most of the balm, with much smaller amounts of conifer resin and aromatic extract and minor amounts of wax and gum. The authors argue that these proportions are typical of later pharaonic balms.

Jones et al. (2018) extended this to an intact Predynastic body, Turin S. 293. Textiles from the body and its basket were radiocarbon dated to 3635–3380 and 3644–3386 cal BC (95.4%). The balm impregnating the textiles was mostly plant oil, with far smaller amounts of a heated conifer resin, an aromatic plant extract or "balsam", and a plant gum or sugar. The authors infer that the conifer resin would have been imported from the eastern Mediterranean.

### 4.2 Pharaonic and Graeco-Roman human mummies

Buckley and Evershed (2001) published chemical analyses of tissues, wrappings and "resinous/bituminous" materials from provenanced and dated mummies. Only the abstract was read for this review, and it does not list the results.

The largest synthesis available is Evershed and Clark (2021), covering balms from over 70 mummies dated c. 3500 BC to AD 395 (read in abstract only). They found that mummies before about 1000 BC were usually treated with fat or oil alone, which in many cases may have been added to the body from outside. Later balms contain fat or oil, beeswax and/or resin. In their sample, *Pistacia* resin occurred only in a few mummies of the Saite to Ptolemaic periods, whereas conifer resin was more common. Head and limb balms were simpler than torso balms. The bulk ingredients (fat, oil, beeswax) were probably local and cheap, while resins and bitumen were imported and expensive.

### 4.3 Bitumen

The dark colour of mummies long led to the assumption that bitumen was everywhere. Clark, Ikram and Evershed (2016) tested 91 samples from 39 mummies (c. 3200 BC – AD 395) for sterane and hopane biomarkers. They found no detectable bitumen before the New Kingdom, bitumen in 50% of New Kingdom to Late Period mummies, and in 87% of Ptolemaic and Roman mummies. Radiocarbon-based quantification showed that balms were never more than 45% bitumen by weight. They also showed that the dark colour can be reproduced by heating or ageing mixtures of fats, resins and beeswax. Strabo's statement (16.2.45) that the Egyptians used asphalt for embalming fits this late peak. Fulcher et al. (2021) and Rageot et al. (2023) both attribute the bitumen they found to the Dead Sea.

### 4.4 Senetnay (KV42, 18th Dynasty)

Huber et al. (2023) analysed balm scraped from the empty canopic jars of Senetnay, wet nurse of the future Amenhotep II, c. 1450 BC, excavated by Howard Carter in KV42. They combined GC–MS, HT-GC–MS and LC–MS/MS. The balms contained beeswax, plant oil, fats, bitumen, Pinaceae resins, a balsamic substance, and dammar or *Pistacia* resin. They also contained benzoic acid, coumarin and vanillic acid, whose plant sources are uncertain. Larixol in one sample points to larch as a possible Pinaceae source, though the authors treat this with caution because it rests on a single biomarker. The jar that held the lungs contained an aromatic resin (dammarenolic acid, probably dammar or *Pistacia*) absent from the liver jar, so the two organs were treated with different balms. The authors call these the richest and most complex balms yet identified for this period.

### 4.5 The Saqqara workshop and the meaning of *antiu* and *sefet*

Rageot et al. (2023), read in full, is the key study for the process. Their workshop, dated to about 664–525 BC, lies just south of the pyramid of Unas. It comprises the *wabet*, an aboveground structure probably corresponding to the *ibu*, and communal burial spaces. From a cache of 121 inscribed vessels they sampled 9 beakers and 22 bowls, plus 4 vessels from two burial chambers. Across the 35 vessels, they found:

- juniper/cypress oil or tar in 21 vessels and cedar oil or tar in 19;
- elemi (Burseraceae, probably *Canarium*) in at least 15;
- animal fat in 18;
- *Pistacia* resin in 5, plant oil in 5 and beeswax in 5;
- probable castor oil in 1;
- dammar in 1 and bitumen in 2, both only from the burial chambers.

Five vessels labelled *antiu* all held a mixture of cedar and juniper/cypress oils or tars with animal fat. The authors conclude that *antiu* was a product made by the embalmers, and that at Saqqara its translation as a raw material such as myrrh can be excluded. Three vessels labelled *sefet* held animal fat with juniper/cypress or elemi additives, so *sefet* was a scented, fat-based unguent. Elemi and dammar come from tropical forest trees. Rageot et al. (2023) take this as evidence of long-distance trade reaching beyond the Mediterranean, alongside Levantine and Mediterranean sources for cedar, juniper, cypress, olive and *Pistacia*. The authors restrict their identifications to Saqqara and add that the composition of *sefet* may have changed over time.

### 4.6 Animal and food mummies

Buckley, Clark and Evershed (2004, abstract) found that cat, hawk and ibis mummies carried complex mixtures of fats, oils, beeswax, sugar gum, petroleum bitumen, and coniferous, *Pistacia* and possibly cedar resins. These mixtures were comparable to human balms of the same period, against the view that animals were simply dipped in "resin". Clark, Ikram and Evershed (2013, significance statement) extended this to food offerings, reporting *Pistacia* resin on a beef-rib mummy. Diodorus 1.83.5 describes animals being treated with cedar oil and spices.

### 4.7 Volatile and minimally invasive methods

Two recent studies analyse the air around mummies. Paolin et al. (2025) combined a sensory panel with GC–MS–olfactometry on nine mummies in the Egyptian Museum, Cairo. They found volatiles from the original embalming materials, but also from conservation oils, synthetic pesticides and microbial decay. Insect repellents similar in composition to original materials make some attributions difficult. Zhao et al. (2026, abstract and Bristol repository summary) applied headspace solid-phase microextraction GC–Q-TOF-MS to balms and bandages. They report volatile markers specific to fat or oil, beeswax, resins (aromatics and sesquiterpenoids) and bitumen (naphthenic compounds), and propose the method for rapid, minimally invasive screening.

### 4.8 Experimental reconstruction

No published experimental reconstruction of a full embalming recipe, run under controlled conditions and checked against archaeological balms, was found for this review. Clark, Ikram and Evershed's (2016) heating and ageing of fat–resin–beeswax mixtures to imitate dark balms is the nearest case.

## 5. Discussion: what remains lost

**Proportions and quantities.** Relative abundances have been measured in degraded residues (Jones et al. 2014; Jones et al. 2018). But Jones et al. (2014) caution that present compositions do not necessarily reflect the original formulations, because the chemistry changes over time. No text gives quantities.

**Preparation.** Heating markers show that some resins and beeswax were heated and that some resins were cooked with fat (Rageot et al. 2023). Temperatures, durations and vessels are not known. Whether the conifer oils were steam-distilled or pyrolysed tars is left open by the authors' own wording ("oil or tar"). Pliny NH 16.52 describes one pyrolytic method, but for *taeda* and in a Roman context.

**Sequence and timing.** Herodotus gives seventy days in natron. Diodorus gives over thirty days of treatment with oils and spices, followed by myrrh and cinnamon. The Saqqara labels suggest distinct preparations for washing, the head, the skin, the organs and the bandages, and possibly a third-day skin treatment (Rageot et al. 2023). The full order of operations is not reconstructed for any period.

**Terminology.** The Greek "cedar" products, Pliny's *cedrium*, the cedar and juniper/cypress tars at Saqqara and the Egyptian *antiu* do not map onto one another in any proven way. The Saqqara result for *antiu* rests on five vessels from one workshop and one dynasty. The authors explicitly limit it to that context (Rageot et al. 2023). Herodotus' myrrh and cassia have not been matched to specific biomarkers in the studies reviewed here. Huber et al. (2023) could not assign the source of the coumarin and benzoic acid they found.

**Variation.** Analyses now show change over time: fat or oil alone before about 1000 BC in one large sample, more complex balms later, and bitumen from the New Kingdom onward (Evershed and Clark 2021; Clark, Ikram and Evershed 2016). Yet the Senetnay balms show that a highly complex recipe, including bitumen and a dammar- or *Pistacia*-type resin, existed by about 1450 BC (Huber et al. 2023). So individual high-status cases can differ sharply from the general trend. The sample of securely dated and provenanced bodies is still small relative to four millennia of practice.

## 6. Open questions and proposed research

- **Workshop-level comparison.** Saqqara is so far the only workshop where labels and contents have been studied together. Similar labelled embalming deposits from other sites and periods, if they exist in collections, would test whether the meanings of *antiu* and *sefet* found there hold more widely.
- **Text–chemistry concordance.** A systematic comparison of the substance names in the Ritual of Embalming (Sauneron 1952; Töpfer 2015) with residue data, done by Egyptologists and chemists together, would make clear which terms can and cannot be identified.
- **Controlled replication.** Prepare balms following the Saqqara mixtures (for example mixture E for bandages) with candidate ingredients: cedar and juniper tars made by pyrolysis versus steam-distilled oils, elemi, ruminant fat. Age them artificially, then compare their biomarker and heating-marker profiles with the archaeological residues. This would bound preparation temperatures, and it extends the ageing experiments of Clark, Ikram and Evershed (2016).
- **Myrrh and cassia.** Targeted searches for markers of *Commiphora* and *Cinnamomum* in balms, especially of the Late and Ptolemaic periods close to Herodotus and Diodorus, would test the Greek accounts directly.
- **Sourcing.** Extend biomarker and isotopic sourcing beyond bitumen (already traced to the Dead Sea) to the conifer products (Lebanon, Anatolia, elsewhere) and to the tropical resins (Asian versus African elemi, dammar).
- **Minimally invasive screening.** Volatile analysis (Zhao et al. 2026) could be used to choose samples across museum collections. Paolin et al. (2025) show that conservation treatments must be ruled out before results are interpreted.

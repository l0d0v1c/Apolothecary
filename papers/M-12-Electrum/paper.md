# M-12. Electrum (ἤλεκτρον; *electrum*): state of the art

*The Apolothecary — state-of-the-art review. Version 1.0, 22 November 2026. Project DOI: [10.5281/zenodo.24422717](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24422717).*

**Certainty:** High for the meaning — a gold–silver alloy, defined by Pliny and confirmed by the analysis of early coins. Medium for its source: recent analyses report that the alluvial gold of the Sardis streams is nearly free of silver, which undercuts the traditional link between Lydian electrum and the Pactolus. · **What is lost:** where the metal of the first coins came from, and how the Lydian state set and managed the gold–silver ratio

## Abstract

Greek ἤλεκτρον means both amber and a pale gold–silver alloy. Only the context decides which. Pliny defines *electrum* as gold containing a fifth part of silver, natural or made by adding silver (*NH* 33.80). Herodotus calls the pale gold of Croesus' Delphic offering "white gold" (λευκὸς χρυσός) as against "refined gold" (ἄπεφθος χρυσός) (1.50). He names the gold dust brought down from Tmolus by the Pactolus as Lydia's one wonder (1.93; 5.101). Strabo says that dust had run out by his day (13.4.5). Excavation at Pactolus North, Sardis, uncovered a sixth-century BC refinery for parting gold from silver by salt cementation, with cupellation to recover the silver. It was published by Ramage and Craddock (2000). Analyses summarised by Kroll (2010) show that Lydian lion-head coins hold about 54% gold, 44% silver and 1–2% copper. Natural Lydian electrum was valued at about 73% gold, so the coin alloy was artificially debased. Cahill et al. (2020), as reported by the Sardis expedition, found that natural gold from the Sardis streams contains virtually no silver. They suggest the electrum may have come from the Troad. Silver and copper isotopes now offer a test for parted gold (Berger et al. 2021).

## 1. Introduction

The README entry treats electrum as "a natural gold–silver alloy" from the Pactolus and rates the entry High. The first half of that statement holds. The second now needs qualifying. Pliny already knew electrum as both a natural and a manufactured alloy (*NH* 33.80). Some modern scholars restrict the word "electrum" to man-made artefacts and avoid it for the natural product (Greenewalt 2010, citing Cowell & Hyne in Ramage & Craddock 2000). What has become uncertain since 2020 is the source of the metal that went into the first coins (Greenewalt 2010, web version with an addition by N. D. Cahill, citing Cahill et al. 2020).

The double meaning of ἤλεκτρον, amber and alloy, is a second trap. When Homer lists ἤλεκτρον with gold, silver and ivory in Menelaus' palace (*Odyssey* 4.73), the text does not say which is meant. Pliny reads it as the alloy (*NH* 33.81). Herodotus' ἤλεκτρον brought from the edge of Europe along the Eridanus (3.115) and Pliny's *electrum* wept by Phaethon's sisters (*NH* 37.31) are amber.

## 3. Proposed identifications

There is no dispute that the metallurgical ἤλεκτρον/*electrum* was an alloy of gold and silver. Pliny's definition is explicit (*NH* 33.80). The early coins analysed in the laboratory are of such an alloy (Kroll 2010). The open questions concern three points.

**Natural versus manufactured.** Pliny admits both (*NH* 33.80). Kroll (2010) writes that electrum was "an alloy in which gold occurs naturally in stream-bed deposits" and was "indigenous to the region". He also writes that its gold–silver proportions "could be artificially manipulated by adding refined silver to dilute the gold content". Greenewalt (2010) describes electrum coins of the late seventh and early sixth centuries BC that contain more silver than is normal in secondary (placer) gold. He reads this as evidence that silver was artificially added.

**The Pactolus as source.** Greenewalt (2010, web version) notes that it "has long been assumed and stated as fact" that the electrum of Lydian coins and of the Pactolus North refinery was silver-rich alluvial gold from the Pactolus and neighbouring streams. The web version of his essay adds a section by N. D. Cahill reporting the analyses of Cahill et al. (2020). Natural gold from these streams and other sources around Sardis "contains virtually no silver, but is essentially pure gold". On that basis it proposes that the electrum of the coins came from Lydian-controlled mines in the Troad and Mysia, where gold does contain significant silver. Pure Pactolus gold would then have been used for other objects or as bullion. Herodotus' ψῆγμα (1.93; 5.101) could mean either pure gold or electrum, as the same essay notes.

**What the refinery refined.** If the local gold was pure, the Pactolus North installation may have been separating obsolete electrum coins and other objects into gold and silver, rather than refining native ore (Greenewalt 2010, addition by Cahill, citing Cahill et al. 2020). The same text describes the workshop as fairly small-scale, in a suburban quarter, rather than a large state-controlled operation.

## 4. Analytical and experimental evidence

**The refinery.** The installations at sector PN (Pactolus North) were excavated in 1968–1969 under A. Ramage and published by Ramage and Craddock (2000) (Greenewalt 2010). According to Greenewalt's summary, two techniques are directly attested. *Cementation* in small brick ovens parted gold from silver. Electrum sheets and grains were layered with salt, perhaps with brick dust or clay as "carriers", in coarse pots resembling Lydian cooking pots. The pots were heated at about 800 °C for many hours or days, the silver went into chloride and was absorbed by the clays, and the gold was left "basically pure". *Cupellation* in some 200 shallow "bowl hearths" recovered the silver from the lead-smelted clays. The remains are "the earliest known evidence for the cementation process anywhere" (Greenewalt 2010). Platinum-group element inclusions, typical of alluvial gold and present in Lydian coins of both electrum and refined gold, are absent from the gold foils found at PN. This is discussed in Ramage and Craddock (2000) as summarised by Greenewalt (2010).

**Coins.** Kroll (2010) summarises laboratory analyses of the royal lion-head coins at 54% gold, 44% silver and 1–2% copper. He argues they were officially valued as if made of natural Lydian electrum (on average about 73% gold and 27% silver), so the state profited from the difference. He dates the earliest electrum coins, found under the Artemision at Ephesus, to deposits of the later seventh century BC. Around the middle of the sixth century, "by which time the cementation process for parting electrum into silver and gold had certainly become available", Croesus replaced electrum with separate coinages of pure gold and pure silver. Kroll also notes that it is uncertain whether full parting of silver from gold was practised before the sixth century. He points to the opposing views of Ramage and Craddock (2000) and Le Rider (2001). Le Rider's work is cited here only from Kroll's notes and was not read for this review.

**Identifying parted gold.** Berger et al. (2021) ran salt-cementation experiments on gold–silver–copper alloys. They measured silver and copper isotopes, with trace elements, on the products and on gold objects from the Mediterranean and Central Europe. Very heavy silver and copper isotopic compositions are "clear evidence for parted gold". The usefulness of copper isotopes may be limited (abstract). This gives a way to test, object by object, whether a gold artefact passed through a refinery like that at Sardis.

**Why electrum for coins.** Mooring (2025) argues that electrum was an unusual material for money. He finds the usual explanation, that it was chosen for profit, insufficient. Instead he links its use to Lydian gift exchange, the military conditions of the Middle Lydian period and the rise of mercenaries (abstract).

## 5. Discussion: what remains lost

The *identity* of electrum is not lost: Pliny's definition and the coins agree. What has become less certain in the last few years is its *geology*. The evidence for nearly pure Sardis gold comes from a chapter that this review knows only through the Sardis expedition's summary (Cahill et al. 2020, reported in Greenewalt 2010). If it holds, three standard links weaken:

- the Pactolus as the source of coin electrum;
- the refinery as a plant for native electrum;
- Herodotus' "white gold" (1.50) as Pactolus metal.

The Troad and Mysian mines are proposed in their place. Their gold has not been matched to the coins in the work reviewed here.

The *economics* of refining are also still open, as the README says. Kroll (2010) gives the gap between face value (about 73% gold) and content (about 54% gold). The cost of parting, the scale of the Sardis workshop and the flow of silver recovered by cupellation are not quantified in the sources read for this review. The workshop's small, suburban character (Greenewalt 2010, addition by Cahill) suggests that state minting may have depended on other, undiscovered installations.

Agatharchides' recipe, preserved by Diodorus (3.14.3–4), and the salt-and-*misy* roasting in Pliny (*NH* 33.84) are the only ancient texts read here that describe a sealed-vessel purification of gold. Neither concerns Lydia.

## 6. Open questions and proposed research

1. **Read and test Cahill et al. (2020).** Obtain the full data on Sardis-area alluvial gold, including sampling sites, number of grains and analytical method. Extend the sampling to the Troad (Astyra) and Mysian deposits named in the same text.
2. **Match coins to ores.** Use trace elements, PGE inclusions and silver isotopes (Berger et al. 2021) to compare Lydian electrum coins with natural gold from the candidate regions.
3. **Apply isotope tests to Sardis finds.** Measure silver and copper isotopes in the PN gold foils and in Croeseid gold coins, to confirm that they were parted, following Berger et al. (2021).
4. **Quantify the refinery.** Estimate throughput from the 200 or so bowl hearths and the cementation ovens published by Ramage and Craddock (2000). Compare it with the volume of Croeseid coinage.
5. **Replicate the Agatharchides recipe** (lead, salt, tin, barley bran; five days) and Pliny's salt–*misy*–*schistos* roasting on electrum of known composition. Measure how much silver each removes.
6. **Clarify the terms.** Adopt and state a convention for "electrum" as natural alloy versus man-made alloy, following the restriction noted by Greenewalt (2010).

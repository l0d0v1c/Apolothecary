# M-09. Chrysocolla (χρυσόκολλα; *chrysocolla*, *santerna*): state of the art

*The Apolothecary — state-of-the-art review. Version 1.0, 22 November 2026. Project DOI: [10.5281/zenodo.24422717](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24422717).*

**Certainty:** Low — the ancient word covers at least a green copper mineral, a green pigment and a gold solder, and the one laboratory hypothesis that tried to fix the solder's identity (cadmium) was disputed in print and has not been confirmed. · **What is lost:** the identity of the goldsmiths' material and a tested reconstruction of the *santerna* recipe

## Abstract

*Chrysocolla*, "gold glue", is one of the most ambiguous mineral names in Greek and Latin technical writing. Theophrastus lists it among substances found in mines, in one case inside native *kyanos*. Dioscorides grades it as a medicine. Vitruvius and Pliny treat it as an expensive green pigment. Pliny also describes a goldsmiths' *chrysocolla*, called *santerna*, made from Cyprian verdigris, urine and *nitrum*. The Leiden papyrus X gives the name to a copper–silver–gold alloy. Modern commentators since Caley and Richards (1956) have read the natural substance as a green copper mineral, mainly malachite, and the soldering use as reduction of copper compounds in situ. In 1987–1989 Demortier proposed instead, from cadmium detected in solders on gold objects from Iran and Syria, that the goldsmiths' chrysocolla was the yellow cadmium sulphide greenockite. Meeks and Craddock (1991) published a critique of that claim. Demortier replied (1992). The only identification firmly supported by the texts is a green copper compound for the pigment and the medicine. For the solder, Pliny's recipe and the papyrus alloy remain untested in the laboratory.

## 1. Introduction

The Greek χρυσόκολλα and its Latin loan *chrysocolla* combine "gold" and "glue". Pliny states the link himself: goldsmiths "claim chrysocolla for themselves for gluing gold" (*adglutinando auro*), and say that all similar green substances are named after it (Pliny, *NH* 33.93; text checked, see Section 2). The same word is used in the same authors for a mine product, a painter's colour and a drug (Theophrastus, *De lapidibus* 26, 39, 40, 51; Pliny, *NH* 33.86–92; Dioscorides, *De materia medica* 5.89). The modern mineral called chrysocolla is a hydrated copper silicate. Caley and Richards (1956, commentary on § 26) argued that the ancient name covered earthy green copper minerals in general, malachite more often than the silicate. Agricola later applied the name to borax, and Caley and Richards call that use a misunderstanding (1956, commentary on § 26, citing Agricola, *De natura fossilium*, 1558).

The question for this dictionary is therefore not "which mineral is chrysocolla?" but "which material was meant in each passage?" The goldsmiths' material is the least certain.

## 3. Proposed identifications

**Green copper mineral (malachite and similar).** Caley and Richards (1956, commentary on §§ 26 and 51) drew three arguments from Theophrastus. Chrysocolla occurs in copper mines. It occurs inside native *kyanos*, which they identify as azurite. It appears in a list of pigments. From these they concluded that chrysocolla was "any bright-green copper mineral that occurred as an earthy incrustation", usually malachite, sometimes the copper silicate. Pliny supports a green material: the pigment is judged against young corn (*NH* 33.89), and all "similarly green" substances take its name (*NH* 33.93). So does Dioscorides, who calls the best kind leek-green (5.89).

**Solder made by reducing copper compounds.** Caley and Richards (1956, commentary on § 26) cite Guettard, through Bailey's edition of Pliny's chemical chapters, as having shown experimentally in the late eighteenth century that malachite can solder gold when a reducing agent is present. They also cite Maryon's 1936 study of granulated Etruscan and Greek work, and a 1934 British patent granted to Littledale for the same principle. On this reading, *santerna* is a copper salt (verdigris) that is reduced to copper in the joint and alloys with the gold. The Leiden recipe 31 is a different technique: a ready-made copper–silver–gold alloy, which the papyrus also calls by the name of chrysocolla (Caley 1926, repr. Jensen 2008, recipes 31 and 33).

**Cadmium sulphide (greenockite).** Demortier (1987) reported cadmium at up to several per cent in solders on ancient gold objects from Iran and Syria. He argued that the relative proportions of copper, silver and cadmium separate ancient from modern brazing. He proposed that "the chrysocolla of the goldsmith, gold solder, could be greenockite (natural cadmium sulphide) and not necessarily malachite" (abstract). In a 1989 chapter he repeated the claim, describing greenockite as "a yellow natural mineral" and basing it on thousands of PIXE analyses of solders. He argued that some of these results could not be explained if chrysocolla were malachite or the modern copper silicate (Demortier 1989, abstract). Demortier and Houbion (1987, abstract) argued that most electron microprobes with energy-dispersive detectors could not identify low cadmium contents in solders. In their view, negative results from such instruments did not contradict nuclear-microprobe results. Decroupet and Demortier (1991) published experiments on low-temperature diffusion bonding of high-carat gold with gold–cadmium alloys formed in situ (title only seen).

**Critique of the cadmium hypothesis.** Meeks and Craddock (1991) published "The detection of cadmium in gold/silver alloys and its alleged occurrence in ancient gold solders". Demortier's comment and the authors' reply followed (Demortier 1992). Neither text could be read for this review, so the content of the exchange is not summarised here. Its existence shows that the cadmium identification was contested in the same journal soon after it appeared. A later study of nineteenth-century Castellani jewellery states that cadmium-bearing solders were introduced into jewellery in the 1860s and became common only in the twentieth century. It also states that detecting cadmium (reported at c. 2–15 wt% in the cases it cites) is used as evidence of forgery in authenticity studies. Its authors note that cadmium peaks can be hidden by interfering peaks in SEM–EDS, PIXE and XRF, and they added a zinc-filtered detector to rule this out (Manca et al. 2022, citing Meeks & Craddock 1991 among others).

## 4. Analytical and experimental evidence

*Pigment.* Aliatis et al. (2009) analysed green samples from Pompeian wall paintings and a pigment pot by micro-Raman, FT-IR and SEM-EDX. They found that green earths (celadonite, glauconite) and malachite were used, together with mixtures of Egyptian blue and yellow ochre (abstract). This confirms a green copper carbonate in a Roman painter's palette. It does not show that the painters called it *chrysocolla*, and no study read for this review identifies the copper silicate mineral in a Roman paint layer. Caley and Richards (1956, commentary on § 51) had already noted that the silicate had not been found among the Greek pigments analysed by their time.

*Solders.* The cadmium data are Demortier's (1987; 1989), and they were challenged by Meeks and Craddock (1991). Manca et al. (2022) found no cadmium in the joins of Castellani jewellery. They did detect low cadmium (c. 0.3%) in the beads of one piece, which they judged too low to affect melting temperature or colour. Their work concerns nineteenth-century objects and cannot date ancient solders. Manca et al. (2022) also report that they never detected a copper increase alone, the signature of copper-salt soldering, in the Castellani pieces. They describe that technique as one in which the Etruscans excelled, citing the literature on Etruscan goldwork.

*Experimental reconstruction.* No published experiment was found that reproduces Pliny's *santerna* (Cyprian verdigris, boy's urine, *nitrum*, ground in Cyprian copper mortars) and tests it as a gold solder. The same holds for the Leiden alloy (copper 4 : *asem* 2 : gold 1). The eighteenth- and twentieth-century demonstrations cited by Caley and Richards (Guettard; Littledale's patent) used copper compounds with a reducing agent. They were not reconstructions of the Plinian mixture.

## 5. Discussion: what remains lost

The pigment and the medicine are not seriously in doubt. Theophrastus, Vitruvius, Pliny and Dioscorides describe a green mine product found near copper and traded from Armenia, Macedonia, Cyprus and Spain (Section 2). The identification as an earthy green copper mineral, malachite above all, rests on textual inference (Caley & Richards 1956). For Pompeii, it is compatible with analysis (Aliatis et al. 2009).

The goldsmiths' material is less clear, for three reasons.

First, Pliny's own text joins the solder to the green family. *Santerna* is made from *aerugo*, a copper acetate or carbonate product. Pliny says the goldsmiths named "all similarly green substances" after it (*NH* 33.93). A yellow cadmium sulphide fits awkwardly with that sentence. Demortier's reading (1987; 1989) depends on reinterpreting several paragraphs of Book 33, as his 1989 abstract says, and this review has not read his argument in full.

Second, the analytical basis for cadmium was contested (Meeks & Craddock 1991; Demortier 1992). Cadmium is now treated in authenticity studies as a marker of modern work (Manca et al. 2022). So any claim of ancient cadmium solder needs objects with secure contexts and an analytical set-up that rules out peak interference.

Third, at least two ancient techniques may hide under the same name. One is a copper compound reduced in the joint, compatible with Pliny's recipe. The other is a pre-alloyed copper–silver–gold solder, attested in the Leiden papyrus. The texts do not tell us which goldsmiths used which, or when.

What is lost, then, is not a single substance. It is the link between the name and the workshop practice: which recipe was used for which gold (Pliny's *argentosum* versus *aerosum*), and whether *santerna* works as described.

## 6. Open questions and proposed research

1. **Rebuild *santerna*.** Prepare verdigris from Cypriot-type copper. Add urine and a sodium carbonate flux (*nitrum*), grind in a copper mortar, and test the paste as a colloidal hard solder on high-silver ("*argentosum*") and copper-rich gold sheets. Analyse the joint by SEM-EDS against granulated ancient work. Record the temperature, atmosphere and failure modes.
2. **Rebuild the Leiden alloy** (Cu 4 : *asem* 2 : Au 1, with *asem* taken as a gold–silver alloy). Measure its melting range against gold sheet of known composition.
3. **Re-examine the cadmium question on well-provenanced material.** Analyse joins on excavated gold objects with a filtered-detector XRF or PIXE set-up like that of Manca et al. (2022). Publish negative results as well.
4. **Read the full Meeks–Craddock–Demortier exchange** (1991–1992) and the full text of Demortier (1989). This review saw only abstracts and titles. A future version should summarise the arguments directly.
5. **Look for the copper silicate in paint.** Run targeted Raman or FT-IR surveys of Roman and Hellenistic greens for chrysocolla sensu stricto, to test whether the modern mineral ever entered the painters' *chrysocolla*.
6. **Check the Greek of Leiden papyrus X recipes 28, 31 and 33** in Halleux (1981), and Theophrastus §§ 26, 39, 40 and 51 in a modern critical edition.

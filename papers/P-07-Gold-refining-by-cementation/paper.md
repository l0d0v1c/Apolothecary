# P-07. Gold refining by cementation: state of the art

*The Apolothecary — state-of-the-art review. Version 1.0, 22 November 2026. Project DOI: [10.5281/zenodo.24422717](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24422717).*

**Certainty:** Medium to High — salt cementation reliably parts silver from gold in modern experiments and is attested archaeologically at Sardis, but the Sardis recipe, temperature, duration and number of cycles are still inferred · **What is lost:** the exact composition of the cement, the firing schedule, the number of repeated cementations, and how (and whether) the silver was recovered from the spent cement and vessels

## Abstract

Cementation (parting) is the separation of silver from gold by heating thin gold–silver alloy with common salt, and possibly a porous carrier such as brick dust or clay, below the melting point of gold, so that the silver is converted to silver chloride and removed. The reference site is the Lydian refinery at Pactolus North, Sardis, of the first half of the sixth century BC, published by Ramage and Craddock (2000). This review sets out the ancient texts that bear on the process (Herodotus, Strabo, Diodorus after Agatharchides, Pliny), checked against online editions. It then summarises the archaeological and experimental evidence. Three developments since 2000 change the picture: (1) Mesopotamian texts and lead-isotope analyses of silver suggest that salt parting was practised a millennium or more before Sardis (Meadows 2021; Albarède et al. 2025); (2) analyses of modern Pactolus gold flakes show high purity, which undermines the assumption that Lydian electrum coins were struck from natural Pactolus electrum (Meadows 2021); (3) silver isotopes now offer a test for parted gold (Berger et al. 2021). The parameters of the Sardis process are still a reconstruction.

## 1. Introduction

Native gold almost always contains silver (Albarède et al. 2025). Pliny states that all gold contains silver in varying proportion, and that gold with a fifth part of silver is called *electrum* (Pliny, *NH* 33.80; see M-12). Lowering the silver content requires either a chemical separation or the addition of refined metals; increasing it requires a supply of silver. Cementation with salt is the pre-modern method of separation that has left the clearest archaeological trace: at Sardis, thin sheets of alloy were packed with salt, "perhaps combined with 'carriers' of clay or brick dust", in coarse pottery containers and heated "at relatively low temperatures of about 800° C for many hours, perhaps even days"; salt vapours converted the silver to silver chloride, which was absorbed by the clay materials (Greenewalt 2010).

The process matters for three reasons. It underlies the separate gold and silver coinages attributed to the reign of Croesus (Nimchuk 2001; Meadows 2021). It is one of the earliest chemical separations whose workshop survives (Greenewalt 2010). And it produces silver as a by-product, which raises the question whether that silver was recovered (Albarède et al. 2025). The present entry reviews the process. The alloy itself is treated in M-12 (electrum), and the analogous cementation of copper with zinc ore to make brass is listed under P-09 (with M-11).

Two observations follow. First, the only ancient text that gives a full recipe (Diodorus 3.14) mixes salt with lead, tin and bran, so it is not a pure salt cementation. Berger et al. (2021) nonetheless take Agatharchides to have "probably first described" gold parting. Second, Pliny's salt-and-*misy* recipe is given in a medical context, and the "purification" it reports is of the gold as a remedy. That it describes a parting cement is the present review's interpretation, not a statement in the text.

## 3. Reconstructed process

The following steps are reconstructed for Sardis. The sources are the excavation and the scientific study summarised by Greenewalt (2010) and the review of Ramage and Craddock (2000) by Nimchuk (2001).

1. **Raw material.** Gold-bearing alluvium of the Pactolus and neighbouring streams (Herodotus 1.93, 5.101; Strabo 13.4.5). Brooks (2025) argues, from modern small-scale mining practice and the high mercury content (11,615 ppm Hg) he measured in Sardis alluvial gold, that mercury amalgamation may have been used to collect the fine gold. That is a hypothesis drawn from analogy, not an excavated finding.
2. **Preparation of the alloy.** "Larger pieces of electrum were hammered into thin sheets" (Greenewalt 2010). Fragments of gold sheet were found in the excavation (Greenewalt 2010).
3. **Packing.** Sheets or grains were layered with common salt, perhaps with clay or brick dust as a carrier, in coarse pottery containers or ordinary Lydian cooking pots (Greenewalt 2010; Nimchuk 2001). The README entry lists "brick" as an ingredient; the primary summaries present it only as a possibility.
4. **Cementation.** The pots were heated in small brick ovens below the melting point of gold. Nimchuk (2001), summarising Ramage and Craddock, gives "between about 600–800 degrees Celsius"; Greenewalt (2010) gives "about 800° C for many hours, perhaps even days". The silver was converted to silver chloride, which soaked into the pot walls and furnace bricks (Nimchuk 2001; Greenewalt 2010).
5. **Assay.** Purity was checked by melting pieces on vessel fragments and by the touchstone (Greenewalt 2010). Pliny names the touchstone (*coticula*, also *Lydius lapis*) and cites Theophrastus for its origin in the river Tmolus (Pliny, *NH* 33.126; Latin text checked on LacusCurtius).
6. **Silver recovery.** The silver-impregnated materials were smelted with lead. The silver-lead alloy was then cupelled in saucer-shaped hollows in the ground ("bowl hearths"; up to about 200 were exposed), with bellows, in an oxidising atmosphere "up to about 1100° C" (Greenewalt 2010). An iron blowpipe tip and tuyères were found (Greenewalt 2010).

Materials involved: electrum and native gold (M-12); common salt; clay or brick dust (uncertain); lead for cupellation. For Pliny's variant, *misy* and *schistos* (no Apolothecary entries yet).

Dating. The refinery belongs to the first half of the sixth century BC (Greenewalt 2010), or about 575–550 BC (Nimchuk 2001), and is associated with Croesus. Wallace (2016) questions the traditional dates of Croesus' reign (560–546 BC) and argues that he was already ruling in the 580s. The absolute date of the workshop therefore depends partly on a chronological debate that is not settled.

## 4. Analytical and experimental evidence

**Sardis excavation and laboratory study.** The installations were excavated in 1968 and 1969 under A. Ramage (Greenewalt 2010) and studied scientifically at the British Museum (Nimchuk 2001). Chapters 5–9 of Ramage and Craddock (2000) cover the scientific findings and experiments (Nimchuk 2001). Their full text was not consulted for this review. Greenewalt (2010) calls the furnaces "the earliest known evidence for the cementation process anywhere". A later paper by Craddock, Guerra and Cowell (2005) on controlling the composition of gold in Lydian Anatolia exists. Only its bibliographic record could be consulted, so its content is not reported here.

**Coin compositions.** Nimchuk (2001) reports Ramage and Craddock's figures: Lydian electrum coins at about 55% gold and 45% silver with minor copper, and natural Pactolus gold at roughly 17–30% silver. LA-ICP-MS analysis of 21 Lydian lion's-head coins in the Bibliothèque nationale de France gave "55.3±1.6 % Au, 43.2±1.4 % Ag and 1.3±0.4 % Cu" (Blet-Lemarquand and Duyrat in van Alfen and Wartenberg 2020, as quoted by Meadows 2021). The tight spread suggests deliberate control. Meadows (2021) concludes that the issuers "were well in command of the metallurgy involved, and capable of manipulating the purity".

**Pactolus gold.** Cahill and colleagues (van Alfen and Wartenberg 2020, ch. 9, summarised by Meadows 2021) analysed flakes of natural gold from the Pactolus, two nearby streams and an industrial excavation near Sardis by SEM-EDS and LA-ICP-MS. All were between 97 and 99% gold. This contradicts the 17–30% silver figure cited above. The same authors found that the surfaces of two Lydian coins were gold-enriched, with flecks of silver chloride in cracks, and interpret this as a deliberate parting treatment of the finished coin surface. They also propose sources of gold to the north, in the Troad and Mysia (Meadows 2021).

**Before Sardis.** In a chapter of the same volume, Kleber argues from Mesopotamian texts that parting by salts at 600–800 °C "can be traced back at least to the first half of the 2nd millennium" (Meadows 2021, summarising Kleber). Albarède et al. (2025) analysed lead isotopes and elements in Egyptian, Elamite and Persian silver artefacts in the Louvre. They identify a group of silver with "future" (negative) lead model ages and high Na and Ca, which they attribute to silver recovered from gold cementation. It includes objects from the Tod treasure (c. 1900 BC) and Achaemenid vessels. They conclude that salt cementation was practised "as early as the Middle Bronze Age". They also propose that chalk was used as an absorbent and that the recovered silver was not cupelled. These conclusions rest on an indirect isotopic argument and conflict with earlier assessments they cite: Ogden placed routine refining in Egypt no earlier than the Late Period (Albarède et al. 2025). For the third millennium, Hauptmann et al. (2018) found depletion gilding on objects from the Royal Tombs of Ur, but state that parting "is not thought to have been known at this time".

**Experiments.** Berger et al. (2021) performed salt cementation on gold foils about 0.3 mm thick, starting at about 55% Ag and 2–3% Cu, in porcelain crucibles at 850 °C for 12 h. One cement was NaCl and fired clay (3:1 by mass); another added alum. After three cycles in the salt–clay cement, the silver content fell "from 54.7 % to 0.57 %". The residual silver became isotopically very heavy (δ¹⁰⁹Ag up to about 7.5‰). The authors propose that "very heavy silver and copper isotopic compositions are clear evidence for parted gold", with copper isotopes of more limited use. These figures come from the article page, read through a summarising fetch rather than as a full PDF. The experiments show that the reconstructed recipe works and that several cycles are needed for high purity. Albarède et al. (2025) also cite a 1974 replication of Egyptian refining by Notton, which recovered significant silver from the sublimates. That paper was not read for this review.

**Later comparison.** At Sado (Japan), early seventeenth-century salt cementation furnaces left earthenware enriched in Na₂O, K₂O, S, Cl and Ag. This is an analytical signature that could be sought in ancient sherds (Izawa and Nakanishi 2014).

## 5. Discussion: what remains lost

The principle of salt cementation is secure: it is chemically well understood and experimentally reproducible (Berger et al. 2021), and it is documented by a workshop at Sardis (Greenewalt 2010). The following are not known.

- **The cement.** Whether brick dust or clay was always added, in what proportion, and whether other additives were used. Diodorus' recipe includes lead, tin and bran (3.14.3); Pliny's includes *misy* or *schistos* (*NH* 33.84); Albarède et al. (2025) propose chalk. None of these can at present be tied to the Sardis residues from the literature consulted here.
- **Temperature and time.** The published summaries give 600–800 °C (Nimchuk 2001) or about 800 °C (Greenewalt 2010), for "many hours, perhaps even days". Diodorus gives five days and nights for the Egyptian process. No measured firing temperature for a Sardis vessel was found in the literature consulted.
- **Number of cycles.** Experiments needed three cycles to reach about 99% removal of silver (Berger et al. 2021). How many the Lydians used is unknown.
- **The starting material.** The contradiction between the 17–30% silver figure for Pactolus gold (Nimchuk 2001) and the 97–99% gold measured by Cahill and colleagues (Meadows 2021) bears on what was being refined at Sardis and why. If native local gold was already nearly pure, the refinery may have processed gold from elsewhere, or alloys made deliberately, and the role of cementation in the coinage needs restating.
- **The fate of the silver.** Sardis shows cupellation of silver from the spent materials (Greenewalt 2010). Albarède et al. (2025) argue that elsewhere parting silver was used without cupellation and was not economically competitive. How much silver was recovered remains an open quantitative question.
- **Priority.** The claim that Sardis is the earliest evidence anywhere (Greenewalt 2010) is challenged by textual (Kleber, via Meadows 2021) and isotopic (Albarède et al. 2025) arguments for second-millennium parting. The challenge is not yet backed by an excavated pre-Sardis cementation workshop in the literature consulted.

## 6. Open questions and proposed research

- Measure silver isotopes (Berger et al. 2021) on Croesid gold coins and on gold drops from the Sardis parting vessels, to test whether refined gold can be identified directly.
- Analyse Sardis vessel sherds for the Na, K, S, Cl and Ag enrichment pattern reported at Sado (Izawa and Nakanishi 2014), and for alkaline-earth signatures that would test the chalk hypothesis of Albarède et al. (2025).
- Reconcile the compositional data for Pactolus gold. Publish a sampled, located series of placer gold analyses with methods and uncertainties, and compare them with the figures in Ramage and Craddock (2000).
- Replicate the Diodorus (3.14) and Pliny (*NH* 33.84) recipes under controlled conditions, and compare the products and residues with the salt-and-clay cement of the Sardis reconstruction.
- Re-edit and translate Pliny *NH* 33.84 with a philological commentary, to settle whether it describes a refining cement or only a pharmaceutical preparation.
- Test the second-millennium hypothesis on gold objects (not only silver) from the Tod treasure and Middle Kingdom contexts, with the same isotopic method.

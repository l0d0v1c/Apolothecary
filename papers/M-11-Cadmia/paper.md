# M-11. Cadmia (καδμεία; *cadmea*, *cadmia*): state of the art

*The Apolothecary — state-of-the-art review. Version 1.0, 22 November 2026. Project DOI: [10.5281/zenodo.24422717](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24422717).*

**Certainty:** Medium — the ancient authors themselves say the word names two things, a stone used to make copper alloys and a furnace deposit used in medicine, and both fit zinc-bearing materials. Which one is meant, and what the stone was mineralogically, must be decided passage by passage. · **What is lost:** the boundary between natural and furnace *cadmia* in each text, the mineralogy of the "stone", and where and how Roman brass makers obtained it

## Abstract

Pliny says outright that καδμεία / *cadmea* names two things. One is a stone "from which copper alloy is made", necessary for founding but useless in medicine. The other is a deposit formed in furnaces that takes its name from the stone (*NH* 34.100). Dioscorides (5.74–75) describes the furnace product and its grades. He also describes copper workers sprinkling powdered *kadmeia* onto copper "wishing to improve it". Strabo, citing Posidonius, says that only Cypriot copper yields the "*kadmeia* stone" (3.4.15). Caley (1926) glossed the furnace product as, in a special sense, zinc oxide. The DE-CADMEA project reads the stone as a zinc-bearing ore and is re-reading Strabo, Dioscorides and Galen as possible evidence for brass making on Cyprus. Roman brass was made by cementation, which caps zinc at about 28% (Dungworth 1997, citing Craddock 1978). Renaissance crucible slags show that natural and furnace calamine can be told apart chemically (Martinón-Torres & Rehren 2002). In the literature reviewed here, no Roman cementation residue has yet been tied to a specific *cadmia* source.

## 1. Introduction

The README entry calls *cadmia* "a term with at least two meanings". The ancient authors agree. Pliny distinguishes the "stone" (*lapis*) used for founding from the furnace deposit used in medicine, and he names five grades of the latter (*NH* 34.100–103). Dioscorides opens with the Cypriot furnace product and its grades. He then says that some people mistake ordinary stones for *cadmia* and gives tests to tell them apart (5.74). The difficulty for modern readers is therefore not that the word is ambiguous. It is that each passage must be assigned to one sense or the other, and that the mineral sense ("stone") has no description precise enough to identify a species.

The term matters because brass (*orichalcum*, *aurichalcum*) was the alloy of Roman sestertii and dupondii. Pliny says Marian (Cordoban) copper "absorbs *cadmea* best" and imitates the quality of *aurichalcum* in those coins (*NH* 34.4). That sentence is about as close as a Roman text comes to describing cementation.

## 3. Proposed identifications

**Two materials, both zinc-bearing.** Caley, annotating Leiden recipe 11, wrote that *cadmia* "was applied to condensed fumes and smoke gathered from the interior of copper and brass smelters and hence was often a complex mixture of metallic oxides. In a special sense it meant zinc oxide" (Caley 1926, repr. Jensen 2008). Dioscorides' furnace *kadmeia* (5.74.3) and Pliny's *capnitis*, *botryitis* and *placitis* (*NH* 34.101–102) fit that description as furnace deposits. Their reading as zinc oxide rests on the modern chemistry of brass smelting rather than on analysis of an ancient sample.

For the "stone" sense — Pliny's *lapis aerosus quem vocant cadmean* (*NH* 34.2) and the stone "necessary for founding" (*NH* 34.100) — the DE-CADMEA page proposes a natural copper-bearing stone, perhaps mixed sulphide ores such as chalcopyrite with sphalerite, or secondary zinc-rich minerals such as calamine (A. G. Leventis Archaeological Science Laboratories, n.d.). That page also lists Pliny's furnace *cadmea* as clearly a zinc oxide by-product. For Roman Britain, Dungworth (1997) suggests that Roman brass makers probably used sulphide rather than carbonate zinc ore, because zinc and iron contents in Roman alloys are not correlated. This is an inference from artefact chemistry, not a direct identification of the ore.

**Cadmia and Cyprus.** Strabo's report of Posidonius (3.4.15), Dioscorides' preference for the Cypriot product (5.74–75) and Pliny's "best from the furnaces of Cyprus" (*NH* 34.103) are the basis of a current hypothesis. The DE-CADMEA project argues that the convergence of Strabo, Dioscorides and Galen points to *kadmeia* being identified, produced and exploited on Cyprus as a cementation agent for brass. It presents the reading of Dioscorides 5.75 as the deliberate addition of zinc compounds to copper in the furnace, not in crucibles, as "a working hypothesis" (A. G. Leventis Archaeological Science Laboratories, n.d.). The English translation the project quotes (Osbaldeston) renders χαλκός in Dioscorides 5.74–75 as "brass". The Greek word is the generic one for copper and its alloys, so that rendering already assumes the conclusion.

## 4. Analytical and experimental evidence

**Brass by cementation and its zinc ceiling.** Dungworth (1997), analysing more than 1,500 Iron Age and Roman copper-alloy objects from northern Britain, states that the Roman method of brass making was cementation (citing Craddock 1978) and could yield a maximum of about 28% zinc. He notes that alloys above 23% zinc are strikingly rare in his material. In his data, zinc is the characteristic new alloying element of the Roman period, high-zinc alloys belong to the early Roman period, and brass was rarely recycled alone (Dungworth 1997). Craddock, Cowell and Stead (2004) report XRF results of about 80% copper and 20% zinc for the foil over the stamps of a La Tène II sword from the Thames. They call this the earliest known use of brass in Britain (abstract). Rehren (1999) published on Roman brass production in Germania Inferior. Only its title and metadata were seen for this review.

**Telling natural from furnace *cadmia* in the archaeological record.** The most direct method found in this review comes from a later period. Martinón-Torres and Rehren (2002) analysed late fifteenth-century brass-making crucibles from Zwickau (Saxony) alongside Agricola's account. Agricola names both *cadmia fossilis* (natural zinc ore) and *cadmia fornacum* (furnace zinc oxide, also called tutty) as cementation charges. Published analyses of furnace calamine show 10–50 wt% lead oxide. The Zwickau crucible slags have almost no lead and high iron oxide, so the authors conclude that natural calamine was used (Martinón-Torres & Rehren 2002). The same reasoning could, in principle, be applied to Roman cementation residues. This review found no published Roman study that does so explicitly.

**Ancient sources of zinc minerals.** Pliny's list of sources for the stone — "overseas", Campania, the territory of Bergamo, Germania (*NH* 34.2) — has not been matched to specific deposits in the literature read for this review. Dioscorides' "mountain above Soloi" (5.74.4) and Galen's mine near Soloi (as reported by DE-CADMEA) are the only places tied to a working site.

## 5. Discussion: what remains lost

Three things are lost, at different levels of certainty.

*The sense in each passage.* Pliny's own two-sense statement (*NH* 34.100) is clear. Many passages are not. Strabo's "*kadmeia* stone" produced by Cypriot copper (3.4.15) describes a stone that copper *produces*, which fits neither sense neatly. The DE-CADMEA team reads it as evidence that Cypriot copper contained zinc. The Leiden recipes use *cadmia* "in crusts" (recipe 16), which suggests a furnace deposit, but recipe 11 does not say which kind. A systematic concordance of every occurrence, with the sense assigned and the reasons given, does not exist in the literature reviewed here.

*The mineralogy of the "stone".* No ancient text gives a colour, crystal habit or locality description detailed enough to choose between smithsonite, hemimorphite ("calamine" in the older sense), sphalerite or mixed ores. Dungworth's (1997) argument for sulphide ores is indirect.

*The supply chain.* Pliny names sources but gives no quantities. The only working sites named are the Cypriot ones. How the brass of the sestertii and dupondii was supplied with zinc, whether as natural ore or as furnace oxide, and from where, is not documented in the texts and has not been settled by analysis in the literature reviewed here.

## 6. Open questions and proposed research

1. **Apply the Zwickau method to Roman material.** Analyse slag adhering to Roman cementation crucibles for PbO, FeO and trace elements, following Martinón-Torres and Rehren (2002), to test whether natural or furnace *cadmia* was charged.
2. **Test Dioscorides 5.75 experimentally.** Sprinkle powdered zinc oxide, and separately smithsonite, onto molten copper in an open hearth. Measure (a) zinc uptake in the metal and (b) the composition of the white fume collected. This would show whether the passage can describe an alloying practice at all, as DE-CADMEA proposes.
3. **Sample Cypriot furnace deposits.** Look at Soloi-area (Skouriotissa) slags and furnace linings for zinc oxide condensates, to test the Cypriot hypothesis independently of the texts.
4. **Build a concordance** of every occurrence of καδμεία / *cadmea* / *cadmia* in Greek and Latin (TLG, PHI), with the sense assigned. Include the Galen passages, which this review could not check against Kühn's edition.
5. **Read in full** Craddock (1978), Craddock (1998) and Rehren (1999). The README attributes to Craddock (1978) the statement that about a third of Roman copper alloys were brasses by the third century AD. This review could not check that figure, so it is not repeated here.

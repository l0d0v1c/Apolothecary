# O-02. The Judean date (φοῖνιξ; *palma*, *caryotae*, *nicolai*): state of the art

*The Apolothecary — state-of-the-art review. Version 1.0, 22 November 2026. Project DOI: [10.5281/zenodo.24422717](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24422717).*

**Certainty:** High for the species (*Phoenix dactylifera*) and for the genotypes of the ancient palms revived from seed; Low for linking any revived palm to a named ancient variety · **What is lost:** the named cultivated varieties (*caryotae*, *nicolai*, *adelphides*, *patetae*), the plantations of Jericho and the Dead Sea shore, and the cultivation and processing practices

## Abstract

Roman authors praised the dates of Judea, above all those of Jericho, for their size, sweetness and juice. Pliny names several varieties, including the *caryotae* and the very large *nicolai*. The species, *Phoenix dactylifera*, is not lost. The cultivated populations of the Dead Sea region are: date culture continued into the eleventh century AD, and no trace of the plantations remained by the nineteenth century. Date seeds from Masada, Qumran and Wadi Makukh have since been germinated. Seven ancient genotypes, radiocarbon-dated between the fourth century BC and the second century AD, have been analysed. Their seeds are significantly larger than modern ones. Microsatellite markers show mixed eastern and western gene pools. Whole genomes show that segments from the Cretan palm *Phoenix theophrasti* had already entered the eastern Mediterranean date palm gene pool by about 2,400 to 2,000 years ago. This review sets out the ancient testimony checked against online editions and the results of these studies. It identifies what is still unknown: the fruit of the ancient palms, and the link between any genotype and a named variety.

## 1. Introduction

Unlike most entries in this dictionary, the Judean date has been partly recovered as a living organism. Its material has been brought back, though not its name or its craft. A seed from Masada, later nicknamed Methuselah, was the first to be germinated. The first report radiocarbon-dated it to the first century AD and called it the oldest directly dated viable seed. It also found high genetic variation compared with modern elite cultivars in Israel (Sallon et al. 2008). Six more seeds followed, giving seven ancient genotypes (Sallon et al. 2020), whose whole genomes have since been sequenced (Gros-Balthazard et al. 2021).

What remains lost is the set of named varieties the ancient writers describe. Also lost are the plantations of Jericho and Ein Gedi and the methods by which their fruit was grown, pollinated and stored. This review asks how far the biological results can be connected to the textual ones.

The texts agree that the region produced many named varieties with distinct qualities. Pliny alone gives names and a size, and the size is extreme. Sallon et al. (2020) take this to mean fruits "measuring up to 11 cm".

## 3. Proposed identifications

The species is not in doubt. The ancient sources describe *palma*/φοῖνιξ, and the germinated seeds are *Phoenix dactylifera* (Sallon et al. 2008; Sallon et al. 2020). The question is which population or varieties the Judean dates were, and whether any survive.

- **An extinct population.** Sallon et al. (2008) described the Masada seedling as "a representative of an extinct date palm population". Sallon et al. (2020) set out the decline. Date culture continued "during the Byzantine and Arab periods (4th to 11th century CE)". After that, elite cultivars "no longer conserved by vegetative propagation (offshoots) were gradually replaced by seedling date palms", and by the nineteenth century "no traces of these historic plantations remained".
- **A hybrid eastern–western origin.** Sallon et al. (2020) genotyped the seven ancient palms with microsatellites. Methuselah, Hannah and Adam, the oldest, have a mainly eastern nuclear genome with some western contribution. Boaz and Judith are the most admixed. Jonah and Uriel, the youngest, are the most western. The authors suggest that genetically different, "foreign" males were used for pollination.
- **Introgression from *Phoenix theophrasti*.** Gros-Balthazard et al. (2021) sequenced the whole genomes of the same seven palms. They found that by about 2,400 to 2,000 years ago the eastern Mediterranean *P. dactylifera* gene pool already contained segments from the Cretan palm *P. theophrasti*. Introgression was minimal in the two oldest samples, about 3.3% on average in the intermediate ones and about 10.3% in the three youngest. Methuselah and Hannah cluster with West Asian date palms; the others are closer to North African ones. The authors caution that "it is unclear if some of their descendants still exist among modern date palm varieties". They suggest that "Judean date" may have been less a discrete genetic population than "a designation of origin".

No study has matched a revived palm to a named ancient variety. The names (*caryotae*, *nicolai* and the rest) are known only from texts, while the genotypes are known only from seeds without fruit descriptions.

## 4. Analytical and experimental evidence

**The seven ancient genotypes.** Sallon et al. (2020) list Methuselah, Adam and Hannah as the oldest (first to fourth centuries BC), Judith and Boaz as intermediate (mid-second century BC to mid-first century AD) and Uriel and Jonah as the youngest (first to second centuries AD). The seeds come from Masada, Qumran and Wadi Makukh. Judith and Hannah are female; Uriel, Jonah, Boaz, Adam and Methuselah are male (Sallon et al. 2020). Gros-Balthazard et al. (2021) give the overall range as the fourth century BC to the second century AD. The dating of Methuselah therefore differs between the first report, which gives the first century AD (Sallon et al. 2008), and the 2020 study, which places it among the oldest samples, first to fourth centuries BC (Sallon et al. 2020). The reason for the difference was not established for this review.

**Seed size.** The ancient seeds are on average 27.69% wider and 38.37% longer than the combined modern wild and cultivated samples (Sallon et al. 2020). The authors note that seed size has been allometrically linked to fruit size. They take this as consistent with the ancient reports of large Judean dates, though a seed does not measure the fruit directly.

**Fruit.** In the peer-reviewed literature consulted, the fruit of the female ancient palms has not been described. Sallon et al. (2020) list as future work the study of "the properties of dates produced by using ancient male seedlings to pollinate ancient females".

**Storage.** Pliny's remark that only dates from salty, sandy ground keep, naming Judea and Cyrenaica (*NH* 13.49), fits Sallon et al.'s (2008) suggestion that conditions at the Dead Sea may have contributed to the seeds' longevity. Neither text nor study connects the two directly.

## 5. Discussion: what remains lost

The biological material is no longer wholly lost. Seven ancient genomes exist in living plants, and they show that the date palms of the Dead Sea basin in the Hellenistic and Roman periods were genetically mixed and changing over time (Sallon et al. 2020; Gros-Balthazard et al. 2021). That result fits the texts' picture of many varieties "different from each other in taste and name" (Josephus *BJ* 4.468). It also fits the deliberate choice of pollinators that the Y-chromosome lineages suggest (Sallon et al. 2020).

What remains lost is the correspondence between genotype and named variety. Elite date cultivars are conserved by vegetative propagation from offshoots. When that practice lapsed, they were replaced by seedling palms whose fruit varied considerably within the progeny (Sallon et al. 2020). Each palm grown from an ancient seed is thus a seedling, the offspring of a mother and an unknown pollen parent, and not a copy of a named cultivar. Even a seed taken from a *caryota* fruit would not give a *caryota*. This is consistent with the caution of Gros-Balthazard et al. (2021) about descendants among modern varieties.

Also lost are the agronomy of Jericho's irrigated plantation (Pliny *NH* 5.70; Strabo 16.2.41; Josephus *BJ* 4.468–469), the processing of dates into "honey" and wine (Pliny *NH* 13.44; Josephus *BJ* 4.468), and the commercial grading behind Pliny's names.

## 6. Open questions and proposed research

1. **Describe the fruit.** Characterise the fruit of the female ancient palms (Hannah, Judith) under controlled pollination: size, sugar profile, moisture, keeping quality. Compare it with Pliny's criteria of juice, honey-and-wine taste, dryness and size (*NH* 13.44–45). Sallon et al. (2020) list this as future work.
2. **Match fruits, not seeds.** Morphometric comparison of desiccated archaeological *fruits*, where preserved, with fruit of the revived palms would test Pliny's sizes more directly than seed morphometry.
3. **Look for clonal survival.** Screen modern cultivars of the Levant, North Africa and Iraq for clonal identity with ancient genotypes. A clonal match would be the only evidence that a named variety survived (Gros-Balthazard et al. 2021).
4. **Map *P. theophrasti* introgression through time** at more Levantine sites, to see whether the rise from the oldest to the youngest samples (Gros-Balthazard et al. 2021) reflects a change in breeding practice.
5. **Gather the textual dossier.** Locate and check the Galen passages and the rabbinic references cited by Sallon et al. (2020), which were not checked for this review.

# O-03. Judean balsam — opobalsamum (βάλσαμον, ὀποβάλσαμον; *balsamum*, *opobalsamum*): state of the art

*The Apolothecary — state-of-the-art review. Version 1.0, 22 November 2026. Project DOI: [10.5281/zenodo.24422717](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24422717).*

**Certainty:** Low — *Commiphora gileadensis* is the conventional identification, but no archaeobotanical remains of the ancient Judean plant have been verified, and the only *Commiphora* recovered from the region is a different species · **What is lost:** the cultivated plant (species or cultivar), the plantations of Jericho and Ein Gedi, and the tapping, grading and refining methods

## Abstract

The balsam of Judea was a shrub grown in irrigated royal gardens near Jericho and at En-Gedi (Milwright 2021). Its sap, *opobalsamum*, was ranked by Pliny above every other scent and sold for twice its weight in silver. Theophrastus, Strabo, Pliny, Josephus and Tacitus describe its gardens, its cutting with non-iron or special tools, its grading and its adulteration. Since the eighteenth century it has been identified with *Commiphora gileadensis*, the "balm of Gilead". That identification has never been tested on ancient material. The plantations disappeared from the region in the first millennium AD, while a balsam garden at Matariyya in Egypt survived until 1615. In 2024 a seed from a cave in the Judean desert, radiocarbon-dated to AD 993–1202, was germinated. It proved to be an unknown *Commiphora* species, not closely related to *C. gileadensis*, with little fragrance. Its authors link it to the biblical *tsori* rather than to the Roman balsam. This review sets out the checked ancient testimony and the modern evidence, and identifies the analyses that could settle the identification.

## 1. Introduction

Strabo calls Jericho's balsam a costly remedy "produced in no other place" (16.2.41). Pliny says that balsam is preferred to every other scent and was granted "uni terrarum Iudaeae" (*NH* 12.111). The modern literature agrees on its economic weight. Ben-Yehoshua et al. (2012) call it the source of "the highest price ever paid for an agricultural commodity". Sallon et al. (2024) call it "the most valuable export of ancient Judea".

Three things are lost. The first is the plant, since no ancient specimen of the cultivated balsam has been identified. The second is the plantations, which disappeared from the region in the first millennium AD (Sallon et al. 2024). The third is the craft of tapping, collecting, grading and processing the sap. The entry also has to separate the Roman *balsamum* from the biblical *tsori* (צֳרִי), which the JPS translation renders "balm". Whether the two are the same is debated, and the 2024 seed study bears directly on this question.

Two disagreements stand out. The first concerns the tool. Theophrastus has the cutting done with "bent pieces of iron" (*HP* 9.6.2, in Hort's translation). Pliny insists on glass, stone or bone because iron kills the plant (*NH* 12.115). Tacitus has stone or potsherd because steel makes the veins shrink (*Hist.* 5.6), and Josephus a sharp stone (*AJ* 14.54). The second concerns the price. Theophrastus (*HP* 9.6.4) and Pliny (*NH* 12.117) both give twice its weight in *silver*, whereas Ben-Yehoshua et al. (2012) write "twice that of gold". On the ancient testimony checked here, the silver figure is the attested one.

## 3. Proposed identifications

***Commiphora gileadensis*.** Sallon et al. (2024) state that since the eighteenth century this species, "commonly known as 'Balm of Gilead'", has been the leading candidate for the Judean balsam. They add that the identification is "contestable due to morphological differences between the two" and due to the "lack of verified archaeobotanical remains of any *Commiphora* species in the Southern Levant". Ben-Yehoshua et al. (2012) treat the balm of Gilead of Judea as "derived from *Commiphora gileadensis*" and note that it "has been extinct in this area for many centuries". Milwright (2003) describes the medieval balsam tree of Matariyya in Egypt as "probably a cultivar of *Commiphora opobalsamum*". The relationship between the names *C. gileadensis* and *C. opobalsamum* is not examined in the sources read for this review. Pliny's account of propagation from cuttings, vine-like training and three named kinds (*NH* 12.112–114) points to a cultivated plant, perhaps a clone or set of cultivars, not a wild one. Theophrastus says the same: it "is said not to grow wild anywhere" (*HP* 9.6.4).

**The *tsori* question.** The biblical *tsori* is a product associated with Gilead and with healing (Gen. 37:25; Jer. 8:22) and traded by Judah (Ezek. 27:17). Its identification with the Roman *balsamum* is traditional. Sallon et al. (2024) note that the biblical sources do not describe *tsori* as fragrant, and they propose that it may have come from a different, now locally extinct *Commiphora* (section 4).

**Later history.** Hepper & Taylor (2004) identify a "mysterious bush" on the sixth-century Madaba mosaic map as the opobalsam. Because it is shown on both banks of the Jordan, they infer the extent of its cultivation at that time. Sallon et al. (2024) state that by the ninth century AD Judean balsam had disappeared from the region. Milwright (2021) traces balsam cultivation from the fourth century BC to the seventeenth century AD, centred on the walled garden of Matarea (Matariyya) in Egypt, where the last tree died in 1615. Milwright (2001; 2003) studies its medieval trade and medical uses. Patrich (2006) discusses improvements in balsam cultivation in antiquity in the Qumran context; only its title and bibliographic data could be consulted.

## 4. Analytical and experimental evidence

**No ancient balsam has been analysed.** Sallon et al. (2024) note the lack of verified archaeobotanical remains of any *Commiphora* in the southern Levant before their own find. In the peer-reviewed literature consulted, no chemical analysis of a residue securely attributed to Judean *opobalsamum* was found.

**The "Sheba" seedling (Sallon et al. 2024).** A seed found in the excavation (1986–87) of a cave in lower Wadi el-Makkuk, in the northern Judean desert, was germinated and radiocarbon-dated to AD 993–1202. The result was:

- **Phylogeny.** The plant is a *Commiphora* in the "Spinescens" clade. It is sister to three southern African species (*C. angolensis*, *C. neglecta* and *C. tenuipetiolata*) and distinct from all species sampled so far. It is "not closely related" to the commercially used aromatic species, including *C. gileadensis*.
- **Chemistry.** The hexane extract is dominated by squalene (more than 30%), with pentacyclic triterpenoids (α- and β-amyrin, lupeol), fatty acids, phytosterols, n-alkanes and a previously undescribed series of glycolipids. There are "minimal fragrant compounds", and leaves, bark and resin show minimal or no fragrance.
- **Interpretation.** The authors propose that the plant, nicknamed "Sheba", represents an extinct or extirpated native *Commiphora*. They suggest that its resin may correspond to the biblical *tsori*, valued for healing but not described as fragrant. They leave open a possible agricultural relationship with the historic balsam (Sallon et al. 2024).

The seed is medieval, and the plant is not the fragrant balsam the Roman sources describe. It therefore neither confirms nor excludes *C. gileadensis* as the Judean balsam. It does show that at least one other resin-producing *Commiphora* was present in the region.

## 5. Discussion: what remains lost

The ancient texts give a detailed picture of a managed crop. It grew in a few royal gardens near Jericho, later extended under Roman fiscal control (Pliny *NH* 12.111–113). It was irrigated, pruned and propagated by cuttings (Theophrastus *HP* 9.6.3; Pliny *NH* 12.112). Its sap was tapped at the height of summer and gathered on wool into horns (Pliny *NH* 12.116). It was graded into sap, seed, bark and wood products and heavily adulterated (Pliny *NH* 12.118–123). None of this has been matched to a living plant.

The *C. gileadensis* identification rests on the name "balm of Gilead" and on resemblance, not on ancient material (Sallon et al. 2024). The descriptions are themselves uneven. Theophrastus' pomegranate-sized tree (*HP* 9.6.1) contrasts with Pliny's vine-like shrub under two cubits (*NH* 12.112–113). The texts also disagree on the tool (section 2), which may reflect changing practice or different informants.

What is lost is the cultivated plant and its genetic identity, the plantations, and the tapping and grading craft. The relationship between the Roman *balsamum*, the medieval balsam of Matariyya (Milwright 2003; Milwright 2021) and the biblical *tsori* (Sallon et al. 2024) is also unsettled.

## 6. Open questions and proposed research

1. **Residue chemistry.** Analyse residues in juglets and unguentaria from Jericho, Ein Gedi and Qumran-area contexts by GC-MS. Compare the results with resins of *C. gileadensis* and with the "Sheba" *Commiphora* profile (squalene, amyrins, lupeol, glycolipids; Sallon et al. 2024).
2. **Archaeobotany.** Systematically search Dead Sea plantation sites for *Commiphora* seeds, wood or charcoal. Sallon et al. (2024) stress the absence of verified finds.
3. **Genetics of *C. gileadensis* populations.** Place Arabian and East African populations of *C. gileadensis* in the phylogeny used by Sallon et al. (2024), to see which of them, if any, could have been the source of a Judean clone.
4. **Matariyya as a bridge.** Any surviving herbarium or museum material traceable to the Matarea garden (Milwright 2021) could be sequenced as the closest available proxy for the late antique cultivar.
5. **The tools.** Experimentally test the effect of iron, glass, stone and bone incisions on resin flow in *C. gileadensis*, to see whether the iron prohibition in Pliny and Tacitus reflects an observable effect.
6. **The text.** Check Dioscorides 1.19 and the Greek of Josephus and Strabo against critical editions, which was not done for this review.

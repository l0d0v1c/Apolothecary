# P-01. Hot mixing of lime mortars: state of the art

*The Apolothecary — state-of-the-art review. Version 1.0, 22 November 2026. Project DOI: [10.5281/zenodo.24422717](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24422717).*

**Certainty:** Medium — the use of quicklime dry-mixed with pozzolan is now attested materially at one site (Pompeii, 79 CE), but proportions, water dosage, timing and the geographical and chronological extent of the practice are inferred, and the texts describe slaked lime. · **What is lost:** proportions and water quantity, storage time of the dry mix, quicklime grain size, the interval between wetting and laying, and how widespread the method was.

## Abstract

"Hot mixing" denotes the preparation of a lime mortar by mixing quicklime (CaO) directly with aggregate or pozzolan and water, instead of first slaking the lime into a putty or hydrate. For Roman pozzolanic mortars, the hypothesis was put forward on material grounds by Seymour et al. (2023), who interpreted the ubiquitous white lime clasts of mortars from Privernum as the product of quicklime hydrating in water-poor, hot conditions, and proposed that these clasts serve as a calcium reservoir for later crack filling. Vaserman et al. (2025) then reported an unfinished building site at Pompeii (Regio IX, Insula 10) with dry piles of quicklime granules pre-mixed with pozzolan, matching the mortar of walls under construction. The ancient texts, checked here against online editions, describe slaked lime (*calx extincta*, *restincta*, *macerata*) and give volumetric ratios of sand to lime, but they never describe adding unslaked lime to the mix. This review sets out the textual evidence, the analytical case, and the conservation literature on hot-lime mortars, and identifies what remains unknown: the recipe parameters, the extent of the practice, and how far the two published sites can be generalised.

## 1. Introduction

Roman mortars and concretes bound with lime and volcanic ash (*pulvis*, pozzolana; see M-02) have been studied intensively for their durability. Most of the mineralogical work has concerned the reaction products of lime with volcanic ash: Al-tobermorite and C-A-S-H binder in relict lime clasts of a breakwater in the Bay of Pozzuoli (Jackson et al. 2013), strätlingite reinforcing interfacial zones in the mortar of Trajan's Markets (Jackson et al. 2014), Al-tobermorite and phillipsite formed by low-temperature water–rock reactions in harbour concretes (Jackson et al. 2017), and long-term reactivity of leucite-rich tephra in the Tomb of Caecilia Metella (Seymour et al. 2022).

The question treated here is earlier in the chain of operations: in what state did the lime enter the mix? Two routes are possible. In the conventional reading of Vitruvius, quicklime was slaked first (to a putty or powder) and the slaked lime was then mixed with sand or pozzolan. In hot mixing, quicklime goes into the mix directly, alone or with slaked lime, and slakes in contact with the aggregate and water, releasing heat (Seymour et al. 2023). Hot-lime mortars are not a Roman specificity: the conservation literature considers that many historic mortars of later periods were made this way (Pesce et al. 2021; Pavia et al. 2023). The specifically Roman questions are whether the white lime clasts of Roman mortars are the signature of this process, and whether it was the normal practice.

## 2. Ancient sources

No ancient author describes hot mixing as such. The relevant passages concern lime burning, slaking and proportions. All passages below were read in Latin in the online texts indicated.

Two points emerge. First, both Vitruvius (2.5.1) and the Puteoli contract specify slaked lime (*extincta*, *restincta*) for structural mortar, while *macerare* is used for the long soaking of lime for plaster (Vitruvius 7.2.1; Pliny, *NH* 36.177). Seymour et al. (2023) suggested that *extincta* might refer to lime hydrated together with the other components, as distinct from *macerata*; this is a philological hypothesis and the Latin of Vitruvius 2.5.1 does not settle it, since the sentence places the slaking ("cum ea erit extincta") before the mixing ("tunc materia ita misceatur"). Second, the texts know the defect produced by unslaked particles, but only in plaster (Vitruvius 7.2.1), and they insist on aged lime for that purpose (Pliny, *NH* 36.176). Lancaster (2021) examined the literary evidence on mortar ingredients and processes chronologically and by genre; only the abstract and notes of that article could be consulted for this review.

## 3. Reconstructed process

**Materials.** Quicklime burnt from limestone, marble or travertine (Seymour et al. 2023); pozzolanic volcanic ash (M-02) or, in other contexts, crushed ceramic (*cocciopesto*) and sand; water; coarse aggregate (*caementa*) such as tuff and brick (Seymour et al. 2023; Vaserman et al. 2025).

**Steps attested or inferred.**

1. *Burning.* Limestone is calcined to quicklime in a kiln. Cato (*Agr.* 38) gives kiln dimensions and selection criteria for the stone; Vitruvius (2.5.1) and Pliny (*NH* 36.174) prefer white, hard stone for structural lime. Attested in texts.
2. *Dry pre-mixing.* At Domus IX 10, 1 in Pompeii, a pile in room 14 contained low-density quicklime granules several millimetres in size dry-mixed with pozzolan and stored for use, with the imprint of a shovel-like tool; a second pile in the atrium contained pre-mixed lime and aggregates beside a pile of *cocciopesto* (Vaserman et al. 2025). Attested archaeologically at one site.
3. *Wetting and mixing.* Water is added to the dry mix. Vaserman et al. (2025) suggest that the fountain pool of the atrium and the area around it were likely used for adding water. The hydration of CaO is exothermic; Seymour et al. (2023), citing the conservation literature, give a temperature rise of about 55–60 °C above ambient with local hot spots above 200 °C. These figures come from modern hot-lime mortars, not from measurements on Roman material. Inferred.
4. *Adjustment with slaked lime.* Higher Ca/Si ratios in a repair mortar and a buttress wall at Pompeii, compared with the pre-mixed pile and a wall under construction, led Vaserman et al. (2025) to propose that slaked lime was sometimes added to hot-mixed base mixtures; lime-containing amphorae were found on the site, and the authors connect slaked lime with repair mortars and the plaster layers of wall paintings. Seymour et al. (2023) also note an earlier proposal of a two-step addition of slaked lime and quicklime. Inferred.
5. *Placing.* The mortar is laid with *caementa* in courses within formwork or between facings (Vaserman et al. 2025). The interval between wetting and laying is not documented.
6. *Setting and long-term evolution.* Lime clasts with a reaction rim enriched in Si and Al and a calcium-rich core persist in the matrix; on later cracking, water dissolves calcium from the clasts, which reprecipitates as calcium carbonate or reacts with residual pozzolan (Seymour et al. 2023; Vaserman et al. 2025). This is the proposed self-healing mechanism.

**Proportions.** The only ancient figures are volumetric ratios of sand to lime (3:1 for pit sand, 2:1 for river or sea sand: Vitruvius 2.5.1; Pliny, *NH* 36.175), the "two to one" of harbour mortar (Vitruvius 5.12.2), and one part in four of slaked lime in the Puteoli contract. None of them refers to quicklime. A conical lead weight found at the Pompeii site is interpreted by Vaserman et al. (2025) as a possible counterweight for scales used in measuring material ratios; this is an interpretation, not evidence that mortar was batched by weight.

## 4. Analytical and experimental evidence

**Privernum (Seymour et al. 2023).** Mortar from the city wall of Privernum (near Rome), an open-air structure, was studied by large-area SEM-EDS, powder XRD and confocal Raman imaging. The lime clasts contain silicon and aluminium in their rims but keep calcite in their cores; Raman spectra of the carbonate phases were interpreted, by comparison with experiments on CaO carbonating at different relative humidities, as pointing to water-depleted conditions, which the authors attribute to the heat of quicklime hydration. Internal cracks and high porosity in the clasts were attributed to the roughly 33 % volume expansion of CaO on hydration. The authors list the alternative explanations for lime clasts proposed earlier: hydration and partial carbonation of lime before mixing, over-burned lime, under-burned lime, and coarse quicklime (Seymour et al. 2023). Modern hot-mixed mortars made by the same team showed the same type of clasts, cracking and rims; cracked specimens re-mated with a 0.5 ± 0.1 mm gap stopped transmitting water after 30 days of flow, while lime-clast-free controls did not (Seymour et al. 2023).

**Pompeii, Regio IX, Insula 10 (Vaserman et al. 2025).** Samples were taken from the dry pre-mixed pile, a wall being built in 79 CE, a completed buttress wall, a pre-existing structural wall and a repair mortar. FTIR, XRD and petrography showed similar compositions across the samples, with lime clasts from 0.2 to over 1 mm showing porosity, birefringence and shrinkage fractures. The authors used FTIR ν2/ν4 band ratios under progressive grinding and carbon and oxygen isotope compositions to distinguish carbonate formed from quicklime from carbonate formed from slaked lime: clasts from the Pompeian mortars were lighter in δ13C and δ18O than slaked-lime samples collected at Pompeii and approached modern quicklime references. They concluded that hot mixing was used both in the original construction of the house and in the repairs after the earthquake of 62 CE. They also cite mortar mixing pits with partially slaked residues at Augusta Raurica (Switzerland) as evidence for hot mixing on a large construction site (Vaserman et al. 2025, citing Hufschmid 2023; the latter could not be consulted).

**Earlier interpretations of lime clasts.** White lumps in mortars are a general phenomenon, studied for instance in fifth- to seventeenth-century mortars of northern Italy (Bruni et al. 1997, abstract only). Leslie and Hughes (2002) showed that unmixed lime fragments do not act as binder and inflate binder:aggregate ratios in analyses of historic mortars, so that petrographic examination is needed before quantitative interpretation. In Roman marine concretes, relict lime clasts are the site of Al-tobermorite crystallisation (Jackson et al. 2013); there, an adiabatic model of a 5.7 m thick breakwater gave maximum temperatures of 85–97 °C from lime hydration and C-A-S-H formation, with cooling to seawater temperature in two years, and lime formed less than 10 wt% of the mix (Jackson et al. 2013). The model concerns heat from hydration and pozzolanic reaction in a massive structure and does not by itself distinguish quicklime from slaked lime.

**Conservation science of hot-lime mortars.** Pesce et al. (2021) slaked a calcic quicklime with water at 20 and 75 °C and with steam at 90 °C: steam-slaked lime gave portlandite with smaller crystallites and mortars with higher water retention and flowability, and slower carbonation at 40–50 % relative humidity. The RILEM review by Pavia et al. (2023) concluded that the quantity of slaking water is the main variable controlling hot-lime mortar properties, because it determines the slaking temperature, and that historic hot-lime mortars are practically impossible to recreate exactly because limestones, kilns, calcination regimes and slaking and storage methods differed; it also notes that hydraulic and magnesian quicklimes were used historically for hot mixing. These studies concern historic (largely post-Roman) practice and modern replication, not Roman mortars specifically.

**Experimental archaeology.** Full-scale reproductions of Roman maritime concrete have been made, including a *pila* at Brindisi in 2004 (Oleson et al. 2006, title and metadata only consulted). Whether such reproductions used quicklime or slaked lime could not be verified for this review. No published reproduction of a Roman hot-mixed pozzolanic mortar following the Pompeian evidence (dry pre-mix stored, then wetted) was found, apart from the modern Roman-inspired mixes of Seymour et al. (2023), which were designed as modern concretes.

## 5. Discussion: what remains lost

The evidence now consists of two independent lines, both from the same research group: an interpretation of clast chemistry at Privernum (Seymour et al. 2023), and a site with raw dry pre-mix at Pompeii supported by FTIR and isotopic criteria (Vaserman et al. 2025). The Pompeian evidence turns the use of quicklime in at least some structural mortar from an inference into an observation. Several things remain unknown.

- **Extent.** Pompeii is a single house under repair in 79 CE, and Privernum a single wall. Vaserman et al. (2025) themselves note that Vitruvius' description may reflect late Republican practice and should not be assumed to be exhaustive or temporally universal; the same caution applies in reverse to generalising from Pompeii. The Puteoli contract of 105 BCE specifies slaked lime for structural work in the ground, so both routes appear in the record.
- **Marine versus terrestrial mortars.** Seymour et al. (2023) note debate on whether preparation differed between marine and terrestrial structures. Vitruvius' harbour recipe (5.12.2) gives no state for the lime.
- **Parameters.** Proportions of quicklime to pozzolan, the quantity of water, the storage time of the dry mix, the grain size of the quicklime, and the interval between wetting and laying are not given by any text and have not been quantified from the archaeological material. Pavia et al. (2023) identify water quantity as the decisive variable in hot-lime mortars, which makes its absence the main gap.
- **Diagnostic criteria.** Seymour et al. (2023) list several alternative origins for lime clasts. The FTIR and isotopic criteria of Vaserman et al. (2025) are promising but have so far been applied by one team to one site; independent replication on other Roman mortars, and on mortars of known slaked-lime preparation, has not been found in the literature searched.
- **Function.** The self-healing role of the clasts is supported by experiments on modern Roman-inspired mixes over 30 days (Seymour et al. 2023); its contribution to the observed durability of ancient structures over centuries is a hypothesis.

## 6. Open questions and proposed research

1. Apply the FTIR grinding-curve and δ13C/δ18O criteria of Vaserman et al. (2025) blind to a set of Roman mortars of different dates, regions and functions (marine, foundation, vault, repair), together with reference mortars prepared by known slaked-lime and hot-mixed routes.
2. Re-examine published thin sections and analyses from harbour concretes (e.g. those studied by Jackson et al. 2013 and 2017) for the cracking and porosity features that Seymour et al. (2023) and Vaserman et al. (2025) associate with quicklime hydration.
3. Reproduce the Pompeian sequence experimentally: dry pre-mix of quicklime granules and Vesuvian pozzolan, varying storage time, water quantity and grain size, then measure temperature during mixing, setting time, clast morphology and isotopic signature, and compare with the archaeological samples.
4. Re-read the Latin vocabulary of slaking (*extinguere*, *restinguere*, *macerare*, *intrita*) across Cato, Vitruvius, Pliny and the epigraphic contracts, building on Lancaster (2021), to test whether any term can designate hydration within the mix.
5. Survey excavated Roman construction sites for slaking pits, mixing platforms and lime stores, to map where slaked lime and dry pre-mix are each attested.

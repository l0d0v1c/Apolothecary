# M-06. Samian earth (Σαμία γῆ; *terra Samia*): state of the art

*The Apolothecary — state-of-the-art review. Version 1.0, 22 November 2026. Project DOI: [10.5281/zenodo.24422717](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24422717).*

**Certainty:** Low — the texts describe two white earths from Samos with clear physical criteria, but no ancient sample, workings or deposit has been identified, and the one laboratory study tested modern clays and borates, not the ancient product. · **What is lost:** the identity of the two varieties (*collyrium* and *aster*), the deposit and workings on Samos, and the mineral that made the earth medicinal, if any.

## Abstract

Samian earth was a white earth from the island of Samos. It was used as a medicine and, according to Theophrastus, on cloth. This review sets out the ancient testimony, checked against online editions. Theophrastus (*De lapidibus* 62–63) describes narrow workings in which miners lay on their backs or sides, and a layered vein whose best layer was called *aster*. Dioscorides (5.153) and Pliny (*NH* 35.191) name two kinds, *collyrium* and *aster*. Their criteria are whiteness, lightness and sticking to the tongue, and their uses are against spitting of blood, in plasters, in eye medicines and as an antidote. Galen (Kühn XII.178–180) prefers the *aster*, says it needs no washing, and calls it milder than Lemnian earth. Samian earth used to be equated with kaolin. Photos-Jones et al. (2015) proposed instead a link with the borates found near bentonitic clays on Samos. Their tests showed that a few hundred ppm of boron makes such clays antibacterial. No archaeological sample has been analysed, and the identity of the earth remains open.

## 1. Introduction

Samian earth is one of the "earths" (*terrae*) of the Greek and Latin pharmacological tradition. It is named after its island of origin, like the Lemnian, Chian, Cimolian and Melian earths. It is listed in the README "terms to investigate" and was promoted to an entry. It has received much less laboratory attention than Lemnian earth (M-05). The single dedicated scientific study (Photos-Jones et al. 2015) states the general difficulty in its abstract: "there are no archaeological samples thereof, at least with irrefutable proof that they were used as medicines". Any identification must therefore combine the texts, the geology of Samos and laboratory tests on candidate materials.

Two terms should be kept apart. In Pliny, *Samia* also refers to Samian pottery. He says that "Samian ware is still praised for tableware" (*Samia etiam nunc in esculentis laudantur*, *NH* 35.160) and that the priests of the Mother of the Gods castrate themselves with a Samian potsherd (*NH* 35.165). Neither passage concerns the medicinal earth. Nor should the earth be confused with the "Samian stone" (λίθος Σάμιος, *lapis Samius*), which was used to polish gold (Dioscorides 5.154; Pliny, *NH* 36.152).

The README entry gives "Dioscorides book V *(passage to be confirmed)*". The passage is 5.153 in Wellmann's numbering. In the texts checked here, the uses listed in the README ("eye salve, cleaning cloth") rest on Pliny (eye medicines, *NH* 35.191) and Theophrastus (use on cloaks, *Lap.* 63). Dioscorides mentions neither use. The name *collyrium* for one variety means "eye salve". The texts do not say whether the name records a use or only a shape.

## 3. Proposed identifications

**Kaolin.** The abstract of Photos-Jones et al. (2015) states that "previously, Samian Earth has been equated with kaolin". The abstract does not say who made that equation or when, and this review has not traced it to a specific author.

**Bentonite (smectite) with borates (2015).** Photos-Jones et al. (2015) proposed that the composition of Samian earth "was probably more closely associated with the local borates to be found in the proximity of bentonitic clays". Venieri et al. (2020) restate the result in their review of medicinal earths: the antibacterial properties of Samian earth "may have been attributed to its smectite being naturally enriched in Boron". An archived web page of the University of Glasgow project on the earths of the Aegean describes earlier fieldwork near Platanos, in western Samos, where bentonite and borate deposits occur. It reports that "the antiseptic testing was negative" for the material sampled at that stage. It also suggests that "the colyrium could well be, or contain, a borate", while "the aster was probably a type of fuller's earth, an absorbent clay" (University of Glasgow, undated). The page cites a study by Chiotis, Demou, Hall, Perdikatsis and Photos-Jones as "in prep". This review found no published version of it.

**Two different materials under one name.** Both Dioscorides and Pliny distinguish two kinds. Their criteria (very white, light, glutinous on the tongue; versus flaky, dense, lumpy) could describe two different clays, or two layers of one deposit. Theophrastus, who describes a layered vein ending in the *aster*, fits the second reading. That remains an interpretation; no study tests it.

## 4. Analytical and experimental evidence

**The 2015 study.** Photos-Jones et al. (2015) tested hydrated layered silicates (kaolin and bentonite) from Samos and Melos, alone and with the borates colemanite and tincalconite. They tested them against *Staphylococcus aureus* and *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*. According to the abstract, "even a few hundred ppms of boron/boric acid in the Samos bentonitic clays can have a significant effect on the reduction of bacterial colonies". The authors take this as support for the view that the medicinal properties of the earths were tied to the mineralogy of specific localities. The full text of the article was not available for this review. Sampling sites, boron concentrations in the natural clays, and quantitative results are therefore not reported here.

**Related experiments on spiked clays.** Christidis et al. (2020) worked on Lemnian and Silesian earths, not Samian earth. They found that reference smectites and kaolinites with no antibacterial activity became active when spiked with boron or aluminium, but not with titanium or iron. Their boron-spiked samples were made from clays from Melos and from reference clays (accepted manuscript, read in full). The result shows that boron can make a smectite antibacterial in the laboratory. It does not show that ancient Samian earth contained boron.

**What has not been done.** No archaeological or museum sample labelled as Samian earth has been analysed in the literature reviewed here. No ancient working on Samos matching Theophrastus' low galleries has been published in that literature. No study has tested the physical criteria the ancient authors give: whiteness, lightness, adhesion to the tongue (a common field test for clays), the flaky texture of the *aster*, and performance on cloth.

## 5. Discussion: what remains lost

The texts are consistent with one another. They describe a very white, light earth that sticks to the tongue, and a flakier, denser *aster*. Both were mined in cramped workings on Samos. Theophrastus says the earth was used on cloth. Dioscorides, Pliny and Galen use it against bleeding, fluxes, inflammations and poisons, and Pliny also in eye medicines. Galen gives one practical detail not found elsewhere: unlike Lemnian earth, Samian earth did not need washing. If that is right, the useful property of the raw material was available without processing. This makes Samian earth a different kind of problem from Lemnian earth, where processing is central.

The modern proposal of boron-bearing bentonite is supported by one laboratory result on modern materials. It is consistent with the geology of Samos as reported by the Glasgow project. Three gaps remain: the boron content of any ancient product, the location of the ancient workings, and the relation between *collyrium*, *aster* and particular clay layers. The README's assessment, that both the identity (kaolin, or bentonite with borates) and the deposit are lost, still stands. The README's further phrase about clays "spiked with boron or aluminium" (Christidis et al. 2020) refers to experiments on other clays and should not be read as evidence about Samian earth.

## 6. Open questions and proposed research

1. **Locate the workings.** Theophrastus' description is specific enough to guide a field survey: galleries too low to stand in, a vein about two feet high flanked by rock, and an inner layered structure ending in a distinct layer. The target would be bentonite and borate occurrences in western Samos, the area the Glasgow project already sampled.
2. **Test the ancient criteria on candidate clays.** Samian kaolins and bentonites could be compared for whiteness, bulk density, adhesion to the tongue, flakiness and behaviour in fulling. This would check which candidate matches the *collyrium* and which the *aster*.
3. **Look for boron in archaeological material.** If stamped or unstamped earths labelled "Samian" survive in pharmacy collections, they could be analysed for boron and clay mineralogy, as the Basel Lemnian samples were. The README already proposes this.
4. **Publish the field data.** The Glasgow project page reports sampling with negative antiseptic tests. The 2015 article reports positive results with boron. Publishing the sampling locations and the boron content of the natural (unspiked) Samian clays would show whether the natural material, as opposed to spiked mixtures, was ever active.
5. **Trace the kaolin identification.** The origin of the traditional equation of Samian earth with kaolin is not identified in the sources consulted. A short historiographical search, beginning with the commentaries on Theophrastus and Pliny, would settle where it comes from.

A stopping criterion for the first stage: identification, on Samos, of at least one clay deposit that meets the Theophrastean and Dioscoridean physical criteria, with a measured boron content in its natural state.

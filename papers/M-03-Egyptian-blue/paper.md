# M-03. Egyptian blue (κύανος; *caeruleum*): state of the art

*The Apolothecary — state-of-the-art review. Version 1.0, 22 November 2026. Project DOI: [10.5281/zenodo.24422717](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24422717).*

**Certainty:** High — the chromophore is CaCuSi₄O₁₀, identified in ancient samples since the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, and Roman production debris has been found in Campania. · **What is lost:** the working recipe (raw materials, proportions, firing and cooling schedule) and the reasons for its decline after antiquity.

## Abstract

Egyptian blue is a synthetic copper-calcium silicate pigment. Its crystalline chromophore, CaCuSi₄O₁₀, is the synthetic analogue of the rare mineral cuprorivaite. Greek and Latin sources call it κύανος (Theophrastus) and *caeruleum* (Vitruvius, Pliny). They describe its manufacture in Egypt and later at Puteoli, credited to one Vestorius. This review checks those passages against online critical texts: Theophrastus, *De lapidibus* 55; Vitruvius 7.11.1; Pliny, *NH* 33.161–163. It summarises the scholarship from Pabst's (1959) structure determination to recent work. Crucible debris from Cumae and Liternum documents first-century AD production in the northern Phlegraean Fields. Raman studies of trace phases tie Roman and early medieval pigment to Campanian sands. The pigment's near-infrared luminescence is the basis of non-invasive imaging. Laboratory syntheses show how strongly colour depends on raw materials, firing time and cooling rate. What remains lost is the exact ancient recipe, including Vitruvius' silence on lime and the firing schedule. Also lost is an explanation of why use declined after antiquity, given finds as late as the ninth century and an isolated sixteenth-century case.

## 1. Introduction

Egyptian blue is often called the first synthetic pigment (Tite and Hatton 2007). In the Roman period it was traded as pigment balls up to about 15 mm across. It appears to have been the most common blue on wall paintings throughout the Empire (Tite and Hatton 2007, abstract). It is a heterogeneous, multi-phase material: crystals of CaCuSi₄O₁₀ with partially reacted quartz and variable glass (Tite and Hatton 2007; McCloy et al. 2025). The material itself is not lost. Ancient samples survive in quantity, and the pigment can be made again in the laboratory (McCloy et al. 2025). What is lost is the specific technical knowledge of ancient workshops. Our principal Roman source, Vitruvius, gives ingredients but no quantities or temperatures (Dariz and Schmid 2021). The process entry P-08 (synthesis of Egyptian blue) treats reconstruction of the firing in detail. This article concentrates on identity, sources and the state of evidence.

The Greek term needs care. κύανος in Theophrastus covers both a stone and several blue pigments (Caley and Richards 1956, 183). Caley and Richards identify the Egyptian kyanos with "the well-known Egyptian blue frit". They identify the Cyprian kind with azurite and, by elimination, the Scythian with lapis lazuli (Caley and Richards 1956, 183–184). Pliny's *caeruleum* likewise covers several kinds, and only his Egyptian, Puteolan, Spanish and *Vestorianum* kinds are clearly manufactured (*NH* 33.161–162).

## 3. Proposed identifications

**Nineteenth-century analyses.** A small pot of the pigment found at Pompeii in 1814 was examined by Humphry Davy. This made Egyptian blue one of the first ancient materials studied by modern scientific methods (Tite and Hatton 2007, abstract). Later X-ray diffraction identified the compound as CaCuSi₄O₁₀ and established that Egyptian blue and cuprorivaite are the same material (Tite and Hatton 2007, abstract).

**Crystal structure (Pabst 1959).** Pabst grew single crystals of CaCuSi₄O₁₀ ("Egyptian blue") and of its Sr and Ba analogues. He showed that their structure, in space group *P*4/*ncc*, is similar to that of gillespite, BaFeSi₄O₁₀, and he determined the structural parameters for CaCuSi₄O₁₀. He noted that the copper compounds resist mineral acids, which he attributed to the stability of copper in square-planar fourfold coordination. He judged it "probable" that the "imperfectly described mineral cuprorivaite" is the natural analogue (Pabst 1959, abstract). The dictionary entry's phrase "the same structure as cuprorivaite" therefore rests, in Pabst, on a probability statement. The later identification is reported by Tite and Hatton (2007).

**Ancient identifications.** Caley and Richards (1956, 183–184) identify Theophrastus' Egyptian kyanos with Egyptian blue frit. They note that the pigment was used in coarse particles: when ground very fine it turns a dull bluish grey, as they found experimentally on a specimen from the Athenian excavations. Theophrastus' remark that the finest particles are palest (§ 55) and Pliny's comment on its difficult grinding (*NH* 33.163) fit this behaviour. That reading is an inference, not a claim made by those sources.

**Date of invention.** The secondary literature consulted gives different starting dates. Tite and Hatton (2007, abstract) date its first use on Egyptian tomb paintings to around 2300 BC. McCloy et al. (2025) give an origin about 5000 years ago, and Nicola et al. (2023) give an origin more than 5000 years ago. The dictionary entry's "about 2600 BC" could not be matched to a source consulted for this review.

## 4. Analytical and experimental evidence

**Roman production in Campania.** Excavations along the Via Domitiana at Cumae recovered crucible fragments with residues of blue and green frits. Mineralogy of the crucible bodies shows high firing. Calcium silicates formed at no less than 950 °C. Sodalite–nosean feldspathoids indicate higher working temperatures for closed forms (over 1100 °C) than for open ones (950–1050 °C), consistent with green and blue frit respectively. Both frits used a quartz–feldspar–calcite-rich sand. Grifa et al. (2016, abstract) conclude that the production area named by Vitruvius can be widened to the northern Phlegraean Fields, including Cumae and Liternum. At Liternum, first-century AD crucibles with blue residues show Egyptian blue frit made from a local silica-lime sand, an unidentified flux and copper scale, in crucibles of local clay (Lazzarini and Verità 2015, abstract as reproduced in a bibliographic database). Dariz and Schmid (2021) report that the Liternum crucible residues are tin-free.

**Trace-phase provenance.** Raman microspectroscopy of a fifth/sixth-century AD blue fragment from St. Peter above Gratsch (South Tyrol) detected 26 accessory minerals besides cuprorivaite. Natural impurities of the quartz sand point to production in the northern Phlegraean Fields. Chalcocite and chalcopyrite suggest a sulphidic copper ore, and water-insoluble salts suggest a mixed-alkaline plant-ash flux. Unreacted quartz and minimal glass indicate that solid-state reactions predominated (Dariz and Schmid 2021, full text read). A follow-up on Roman pigment balls and murals from Aventicum and Augusta Raurica matched the sand to Volturno river sediments in the Gulf of Gaeta. It supports "a monopolised pigment production site located in the northern Phlegrean Fields" in the first centuries AD (Dariz and Schmid 2022, abstract). These sands lie on the stretch of coast that Pliny names, for glass-making, as the white sand of the Volturno "on six miles of shore between Cumae and Liternum" (*NH* 36.194, checked against Mayhoff). Dariz and Schmid (2021) make that link explicitly.

**Luminescence and imaging.** Cuprorivaite has an exceptionally high near-infrared emission quantum efficiency (λmax = 910 nm, Φ = 10.5%) and a long excited-state lifetime of 107 µs (Accorsi et al. 2009, abstract). Verri (2009) applied photo-induced luminescence digital imaging to the spatially resolved characterisation of Egyptian blue, Han blue and Han purple. Only the title and bibliographic record of that paper were consulted. Bulk CaCuSi₄O₁₀ exfoliates into monolayer nanosheets with strong near-IR luminescence (Johnson-McDaniel et al. 2013, abstract). A two-part review in *Rendiconti Lincei* surveys these properties and modern applications (Nicola et al. 2023, abstract).

**Laboratory synthesis.** McCloy et al. (2025) synthesised 12 recipes from silica, calcium and copper sources (including malachite and azurite), some with added soda. They fired each at 1000 °C for 1–11 h and compared the products with modern pigments and two Egyptian artefacts in the Carnegie Museum of Natural History. Longer high-temperature treatment and slower cooling produced bluer pigment. In an air-cooled versus furnace-cooled pair, the slowly cooled sample was clearly bluer and had about 70% more cuprorivaite. A copper-doped glass phase appeared only when soda was present. The artefacts proved multi-phase, with cuprorivaite intergrown within colourless particles. The authors note that Vitruvius does not mention lime, although cuprorivaite requires calcium (full text read). A 2026 preprint, not peer reviewed, reports that synthetic CaCuSi₄O₁₀ begins to decompose by incongruent melting at about 1020 °C. It decomposes irreversibly into tridymite and a green Cu–Ca–Si glass (Yuryev et al. 2026, abstract). If confirmed, this sets a narrow upper bound on the firing window.

**Persistence after antiquity.** Egyptian blue was identified in a ninth-century fresco in the lower church of San Clemente in Rome. This extended its known period of use by five centuries over what was then accepted (Lazzarini 1982, abstract). It occurs mixed with lapis lazuli in the eighth-century wall paintings of San Saba in Rome, which shows that the change between the two blues was not sudden (Gaetani et al. 2004, abstract). It has also been documented in tenth-century murals at Castelseprio (Nicola et al. 2018, title; Dariz and Schmid 2021). An isolated use occurs in a 1524 painting by Giovanni Battista Benvenuto (Bredal-Jørgensen et al. 2011, title and bibliographic record only). Skovmøller et al. (2016, abstract) note that it "seems to have ceased being widely applied sometime after the fall of the empire". They also stress that its hue ranged from almost black blue to light blue, bluish green and purple, depending on materials and process.

## 5. Discussion: what remains lost

1. **The full recipe.** Vitruvius names sand, *nitri flos* and copper filings, rolled into balls and fired in pots (7.11.1). He gives no proportions or temperatures, and he does not mention a calcium source (Dariz and Schmid 2021; McCloy et al. 2025). The calcium in the Campanian product probably came from calcareous sand (Grifa et al. 2016; Dariz and Schmid 2022). For the Gratsch sample, the trace-phase evidence points to a sulphidic copper ore and plant ash, not filed metal and natron (Dariz and Schmid 2021). The text may therefore describe a simplified or outdated process.
2. **The firing and cooling schedule.** Laboratory work shows that colour depends on time at temperature and on cooling rate (McCloy et al. 2025). The crucibles show working temperatures between 950 °C and over 1100 °C (Grifa et al. 2016). Yet a preprint places the onset of decomposition near 1020 °C (Yuryev et al. 2026). How Roman furnaces were run is not documented.
3. **Pliny's obscure operations.** The statement that *caeruleum* is "dyed and boiled in its own plant" (*NH* 33.161) has no explanation in the sources reviewed. Nor do the meaning of *Vestorianum* made "from the lightest part of the Egyptian" (*NH* 33.162) or the window use of Puteolan blue.
4. **The decline.** The pigment was still in use in eighth- to tenth-century Italy (Lazzarini 1982; Gaetani et al. 2004; Nicola et al. 2018) and appears once in 1524 (Bredal-Jørgensen et al. 2011). Whether its decline reflects lost know-how, the end of a monopolised Campanian supply (Dariz and Schmid 2022) or a change of taste is not settled by the sources reviewed. Dariz and Schmid (2021) note that the apparent decline might simply reflect insufficient data.

## 6. Open questions and proposed research

1. **Vitruvian replication with Campanian raw materials.** Fire Volturno-coast sand, a sulphidic copper ore and plant ash (the materials indicated by Dariz and Schmid 2021, 2022), and separately Vitruvius' filings and natron. Use Cumae-type crucible forms (Grifa et al. 2016) and controlled cooling. Compare the trace-phase assemblages and colour with Roman pigment balls (coordinated with P-08).
2. **Pliny's tests.** Reproduce the coal test and the violet-dyed Eretrian earth fraud (*NH* 33.163) to see what an ancient buyer could detect.
3. **Provenance atlas.** Extend Raman trace-phase fingerprinting to dated Egyptian, Hellenistic and Roman samples, to test whether a single Campanian centre supplied the West after the first century AD.
4. **Medieval survey.** Use visible-induced near-infrared luminescence imaging (Accorsi et al. 2009; Verri 2009) systematically on early medieval murals, to tell a genuine decline from a gap in sampling.

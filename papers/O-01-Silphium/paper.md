# O-01. Silphium (σίλφιον; *laser*, *laserpicium*): state of the art

*The Apolothecary — state-of-the-art review. Version 1.0, 22 November 2026. Project DOI: [10.5281/zenodo.24422717](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24422717).*

**Certainty:** Low — no specimen, seed or securely identified residue of Cyrenaic silphium is known, and every proposed species rests on textual and iconographic resemblance only · **What is lost:** the identity of the plant, the living source in Cyrenaica, and the harvesting and curing technique of the resin

## Abstract

Silphium (Greek σίλφιον, Latin *laserpicium*, its juice *laser*) was a gum-resin-producing plant of Cyrenaica (eastern Libya), used as a condiment and a medicine and exported in quantity. Pliny the Elder reports that in his day it could no longer be found in Cyrenaica and that a single stalk found "within our memory" was sent to Nero. This review sets out the ancient testimony that could be checked against online editions (Herodotus, Theophrastus, Aristophanes, Catullus, Strabo, Pliny, Apicius) and the modern work on its identity and disappearance. The plant is generally placed in the Apiaceae, probably in or near *Ferula*. Since 2021 the Anatolian endemic *Ferula drudeana* has been proposed as a surviving relative or ecotype and has been studied chemically and in culinary experiments, but molecular phylogenies place it in a Central Asian clade of *Ferula*, apart from the North African species. The classic explanation of the extinction by overharvesting and grazing has been challenged by an argument from habitat change and the germination needs of related species. No archaeobotanical specimen has been identified, and the species remains unknown.

## 1. Introduction

Silphium's juice was weighed against silver: Pliny says it was "ad pondus argentei denarii repensum" (*NH* 19.38). The plant was Cyrene's emblem, shown on its coins and in its art (Fabbricotti 1993; Koerper & Kolls 1999). Its disappearance is often called the first recorded extinction of a species (Parejko 2003). Pollaro & Robertson (2022) cite Parejko's essay as the most recent statement of that claim and accept the description while disputing the cause.

Three things are lost. The first is the identity of the plant, since no specimen has been recognised. The second is the source, the wild stands of the Cyrenaican steppe. The third is the know-how of cutting and curing the juice that Theophrastus and Pliny describe. This review asks what the texts actually say, which identifications have been proposed and on what grounds, and what analytical work exists. The most recent general reviews are Amigues (2004) and Briggs & Jakobsson (2022).

Two points from the checked texts matter for any identification. First, Theophrastus himself records two incompatible accounts and does not settle between them (*HP* 6.3.4–6). Second, Pliny separates the Cyrenaic product from the Persian, Median, Armenian, Syrian and "Parthian" *laser* that replaced it (*NH* 19.40; 22.100; also Apicius' "Cyrenaicum vel Parthicum"). Any modern candidate therefore has to match the Cyrenaic plant specifically, not the substitutes.

## 3. Proposed identifications

**An umbellifer, probably a *Ferula*.** Amigues (2004, p. 191) opens her review by describing silphium as an umbellifer in which every part contained a strongly aromatic juice ("une Ombellifère, dont toutes les parties renfermaient un suc fortement aromatique"). Only that first page could be consulted for this review; her later arguments are not reported here. The *ferula*-like stalk in Theophrastus (*HP* 6.3.1) and Pliny (*NH* 19.42) is the textual basis for placing it near *Ferula*. Miski (2021) calls the source "most likely" a *Ferula* species.

**The asafoetida group.** Pliny's statement that Persian and Median *laser* replaced the Cyrenaic (*NH* 19.40) links silphium with the Asian gum-resins. Agnini et al. (2026) treat asafoetida (*Ferula assa-foetida*) as "the substitute resin (also called silphium) used in Roman medicine and food in the later empire". Miski (2021), using Dioscorides, contrasts the "predominant and gentle" smell of the Cyrenaic juice with the "poisonous smell" of the Syrian and Median ones. On that basis he argues that the foul-smelling *Ferula* resins are not the Cyrenaic silphium.

***Ferula tingitana*.** The README entry lists *F. tingitana* as a proposed identification. This review found no peer-reviewed source for that proposal that could be checked, so its origin and arguments are not assessed here.

***Ferula drudeana* (Miski 2021).** Miski proposes that *F. drudeana*, a rare endemic of central Anatolia, is a surviving form of silphion. By his account it was first collected by Walter Siehe in 1909 in northern Adana province. Miski encountered it near Hasan Dağı in Cappadocia in 1983, and it was found again in 2004 at Siehe's locality. It is known from three populations "near formerly Greek villages" (Miski 2021). His arguments are of three kinds:

- morphological: opposite inflorescence branches compared with the opposite leaves on Cyrenaic coins; leaf-like mericarps compared with Theophrastus' leaf-like fruit; a large root;
- organoleptic: a "very pleasant-smelling" gum-resin;
- chemical: about thirty secondary metabolites (28 isolated from the root and 3 more identified by HPLC-MS), several known from medicinal plants (Miski 2021).

To explain the plant's presence in Anatolia, he suggests that ancient smugglers carried seed there (Miski 2021). Later work by the same group calls *F. drudeana* "the putative Anatolian ecotype of the silphion plant" (Tosun et al. 2023; Kuran et al. 2025).

**Phylogenetic objection.** Piwczyński et al. (2018) sequenced nrDNA ITS of *F. drudeana* among seven poorly known *Ferula* species. They placed it, with *F. huber-morathii*, in the clade "mostly composed of Central Asian species". The two North African species they sampled, *F. marmarica* and *F. tunetana*, fell in a separate clade "mostly composed of Mediterranean taxa" (Piwczyński et al. 2018). Their study does not discuss silphium. Its bearing on Miski's hypothesis is an inference: if silphium was a native Cyrenaican *Ferula*, one would expect it in the Mediterranean clade, not the Central Asian one. Panahi et al. (2018) found strong incongruence between nuclear and plastid trees in *Ferula*. They interpret it as intensive reticulate evolution, particularly in the Irano-Turanian region, which complicates any single-gene argument either way.

## 4. Analytical and experimental evidence

There is no analysis of ancient silphium itself. No specimen, seed or chemically identified residue of Cyrenaic silphium is reported in any of the literature consulted. Briggs & Jakobsson (2022) suggest that underwater archaeology off the Libyan coast, and ancient DNA from botanical remains in wrecks, may offer the best chance of recovering it.

All laboratory work concerns modern *Ferula drudeana* as a proxy:

- **Root and resin constituents.** Miski (2021) reports about thirty metabolites, including sesquiterpene coumarins, spathulenol and phenolics. Kuran et al. (2025) isolated four new sesquiterpene coumarin ethers and a new phenylpropanoid from a hexane root extract, together with nineteen known sesquiterpene coumarins and four known phenylpropanoids. Several were cytotoxic to renal, mesothelioma and colon cancer cell lines.
- **Fruit essential oil and extracts.** Tosun et al. (2023) found shyobunone (44.2%) and 6-epishyobunone (12.6%) as the main components of the fruit oil. They report weak to moderate antimicrobial activity for most extracts, strong anticandidal activity of a petroleum-ether root extract, and strong antioxidant activity of methanolic extracts.
- **Aroma and cuisine.** Agnini et al. (2026) profiled the resin's volatiles by gas chromatography–olfactometry, describing pinenes, β-myrcene, α-gurjunene and pyrazines. They also cooked Apicius recipes with it. The resin acted as a flavour enhancer and damped bitterness, and dishes made with it were "distinctly different" from the same dishes made with asafoetida.

These studies show that *F. drudeana* is a pharmacologically and gastronomically interesting plant. They cannot show that it is silphium, because there is no ancient reference material to compare it with.

**Extinction.** Pliny gives an explicit cause: tax-farmers leasing the pastures let flocks graze the plant out (*NH* 19.39). Strabo adds a deliberate destruction of roots by nomads (17.3.22). Theophrastus records the plant's refusal of cultivation (*HP* 6.3.3). Pollaro & Robertson (2022) argue that "strict cold-stratification requirements for the germination of silphium's closest living relatives" were probably shared by silphium. In their view, deforestation and the spread of cropland in Cyrenaica accelerated desertification and disturbed its habitat, so that anthropogenic environmental change, not direct exploitation, was "the dominant factor". Their argument depends on treating *F. drudeana* and *F. assa-foetida* as the closest living relatives (Pollaro & Robertson 2022), which the phylogenetic results above make uncertain. Miski (2021) stresses instead the plant's monocarpic habit and slow growth as the reason it could not withstand overharvesting. Robertson & Pollaro (2023) place Pliny's account in the history of ancient ideas about extinction.

**Iconography.** Fabbricotti (1993) surveys silphium in Cyrenaican art. Silphium dominates archaic and royal coinage, nearly disappears afterwards, and reappears in second-century AD capitals at Beida and Cyrene as a deliberate revival "even if the plant of silphium itself has become very rare". Koerper & Kolls (1999) studied the coin motif as marketing of a medicinal plant; only the title and bibliographic data of that paper could be consulted.

## 5. Discussion: what remains lost

The ancient evidence is richer than the modern evidence. The Greek and Latin texts give a fairly detailed description: a *ferula*-like stalk, celery-like leaves, a broad winged fruit, a thick black-barked root, two grades of juice, and a curing process with meal or bran. They also give an approximate range along the Syrtis coast and a date and cause of disappearance. But they come from at least two inconsistent informant traditions (*HP* 6.3.4–6; *NH* 19.45), and Pliny already mixes Cyrenaic and Persian information. No ancient author wrote from autopsy of the living plant in a way we can verify.

The modern candidates are matched to this description and to coin images, both of which are stylised. The *F. drudeana* hypothesis has produced a real body of chemistry (Miski 2021; Tosun et al. 2023; Kuran et al. 2025; Agnini et al. 2026). It faces a biogeographical problem, since the plant grows in Anatolia, not Libya. It also faces a phylogenetic one: the plant groups with Central Asian *Ferula* (Piwczyński et al. 2018). The "ecotype" wording in the later papers does not solve this. It assumes the transfer from Cyrenaica that the hypothesis needs to prove.

The cause of the extinction is also open. Overgrazing (Pliny), deliberate destruction (Strabo) and habitat change (Pollaro & Robertson 2022) are not mutually exclusive, and none can be tested without knowing the plant's ecology. What is lost, then, is the identity of the species, its living source, and the harvesting, grading and curing practice that Theophrastus describes. That practice includes the regulated cutting "like those in use in mines" and the shaking with meal for export (*HP* 6.3.2).

## 6. Open questions and proposed research

1. **Archaeobotany in Cyrenaica and in transport contexts.** Look for silphium seeds, pollen or resin in Cyrenaican sites and in shipwreck cargoes off Libya, as Briggs & Jakobsson (2022) propose. A securely dated specimen would be the only decisive evidence.
2. **Phylogenetic placement with ancient DNA.** If any such remains are found, sequence them against the Mediterranean and Central Asian *Ferula* clades (Piwczyński et al. 2018; Panahi et al. 2018). The test is simple: does the material fall with the North African *Ferula*, or with *F. drudeana*?
3. **Chemical markers.** Define a marker set for Cyrenaic-type resin that separates it from asafoetida. The sesquiterpene coumarins and volatiles of *F. drudeana* (Kuran et al. 2025; Agnini et al. 2026) and those of *F. assa-foetida* are possible starting points. Then screen residues in Cyrenaican and Roman containers.
4. **Germination ecology.** Test whether North African *Ferula* species, not only the Asian ones, require the cold stratification on which Pollaro & Robertson (2022) build their climate argument.
5. **The curing recipe.** Experimentally reproduce the treatment with meal (Theophrastus *HP* 6.3.2) or bran (Pliny *NH* 19.44) on a modern *Ferula* gum-resin, and measure its effect on colour and keeping quality.
6. **A critical text of the silphium dossier.** Bring together Dioscorides 3.80, Arrian *Anab.* 3.28.6–7 and the Synesius letters cited by Miski (2021), none of which were checked for this review, in a single verified dossier.
